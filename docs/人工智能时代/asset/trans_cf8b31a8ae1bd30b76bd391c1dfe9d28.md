## Kill Anyone Who Gets in the Way

Or more to the point, if you recently visited a web page because of a particular product, but didn't end up buying it—say, a certain model of running shoe—you're more likely to respond when you see an ad for that product again soon. The problem is that once you leave the seller's site, the maker of those running shoes has no chance to communicate with you again. So this is where the organizations that store `cookies` on your computer come in handy. When you show up somewhere else—say, on a site making dinner reservations—they recognize you as the person who was looking for shoes last week, and they show you an ad, reminding you of your interest. This is called `retargeting`, and it's one of the most valuable forms of online advertising today.

Companies like `Rocket Fuel` have built sophisticated mathematical models to predict the likelihood that you'll respond to any given ad they might show you, on behalf of their various advertising clients. They know, statistically speaking, how much you're worth to each of those advertisers. So they know how much an advertiser is willing to pay to put an ad in front of you when you load a web page.

This is where synthetic intelligence shines. Keeping these analyses up to date is a staggeringly complex task, far beyond the limits of human ability. To do it well, synthetic intelligence must continuously gather and analyze massive amounts of data. But for machine learning systems that can harness massive computing power and huge data stores, this task is child's play. They are forever sifting through the stream of information, panning for nuggets of valuable correlation, lying in wait for the next time you visit a page, wherever that page may appear. The trouble is, the synthetic intelligences of all those other organizations that have left `cookies` on your computer are doing the same thing.

Each one represents a different advertiser, and each has predicted the different value of showing you an ad at different times of day, on different browsers, in different positions on different pages.

So what happens if someone just wants to run a website by selling ad space? Apart from a handful of the biggest, most successful sites, selling ad space directly to individual advertisers is a completely impractical proposition. Even selling to middlemen who represent lots of advertisers (like `Rocket Fuel`) is a nightmare. Complex electronic ad exchanges have emerged, whose job is to auction off the right to show an ad on the page you're loading, based on the actual price. The website operator simply provides a catalog of their available ad space to the ad exchange. Then the middlemen weigh in, and the game begins.

When you load a web page, the page requests an ad of a specific size from the ad exchange. The middlemen bid directly on the ad, looking for their `cookies` on your computer. If they find one, they perform a complex evaluation to estimate how much they're willing to pay for this opportunity, factoring in every past encounter with you, where you've been, what you've done before. They also factor in the site you're visiting now, the content of the page you're browsing, and the likelihood you'll transact with their existing advertisers.

This is where things get complicated. The middlemen may also have bought information about you from other companies that aren't in the ad-serving business but have agreed to share your `cookies` with them for a fee. Even at the speed of light, there's no time for multiple rounds of bidding, so each bidder picks a specific ad from their roster and submits their single best offer.

The bidders also tell the ad exchange which advertiser's message they intend to show, because the site hosting the ad doesn't want certain messages appearing on its pages. For instance, a site catering to children might refuse ads for adult products like casinos, even if the person browsing is a promising buyer. Or a diabetes information site might not want to show ads for desserts. But almost every site refuses to show ads for its competitors. In the end, the ad exchange awards the opportunity to the highest bidder, but charges only the second-highest bid (this encourages participants to bid their true maximum value).

After expending more computing power than it took to put a man on the moon, an ad appears seamlessly on the page you're loading… for special vitamins for your cat, to fight feline leukemia. Amazing! How did they know you just got a cat?

### Computer Programs Duking It Out

In a conversation, `Rocket Fuel` CEO George John pointed out a delicious irony: the art of persuasion (which you might think is a uniquely human endeavor) is better performed by synthetic intelligence. Countless customers comment on the `Rocket Fuel` website that spending their ad budget through `Rocket Fuel` works far better than doing it themselves. You may have noticed I haven't yet addressed a key question: how do the bidders know how much a given ad impression is worth to the advertiser? The answer is that the advertisers have an equally complex, completely parallel system that feeds back to the middlemen when you act on an ad they've shown you. That action might be clicking the ad immediately, or independently visiting the advertiser's site later. This delayed action is called `viewthrough attribution`.

CTO Mark Torrance showed me the uncanny precision of his computers in predicting and influencing customer behavior. He showed me how he estimates the likelihood that you'll buy a pizza from his client (a major international fast-food pizza chain) within two weeks of seeing their ad. In a carefully colored chart called a `heat map`, he toggled off a green cell, and I could see a carefully selected group of consumers, 9.125% to 11.345% of whom would buy his client's pizza within two weeks—even the client didn't know which of these people actually ate pizza. The actual number reported back by the client later was 10.9%.

In this high-stakes game, the various players aren't all friends, and all manner of shenanigans and gamesmanship ensue. For instance, the winning bidder in any auction knows the second-place bid, from which they can infer a great deal about their competitors—who else is chasing the same space, and what other organizations are willing to pay. So bidding organizations need sophisticated strategies to beat the other players, much like expert poker players deliberately lose hands to gauge the competition.

The ad exchange's synthetic intelligence manages all the bids, and it's no slouch either. It learns each bidder's strategy and profits from it, perhaps by cherry-picking the best deals, or by playing similar bidders against each other to drive up the price.

With all this effort, you might think these ads must be incredibly valuable, but the opposite is true. Though these synthetic intelligences fight hard in every battle, the price of a single ad served through an ad exchange can be as low as $0.00005. In ad parlance, that's a `CPM` (cost per thousand impressions) of 5 cents. The unit price is low, but the volume of these transactions is enormous.

Three friends founded `Rocket Fuel` in 2008; as I write this, the company is worth about $2 billion. You've probably guessed that both CTO Mark Torrance and CEO George John studied AI at Stanford.

What's the root cause of this electronic free-for-all? Are the dueling computer programs gambling in our financial system, or influencing our consumer behavior? Can't synthetic intelligence just play nice, like decent civilized people?

The answer is surprisingly simple. These systems are designed for a single purpose; they don't know or care about side effects. As I'll discuss later, the combatants in this electronic arena have no reason to show mercy to one another, and they never pay more than the absolute minimum for what they want. Conversely, they charge as much as they can, extracting the maximum possible profit.

### Putting the Genie Back in the Bottle

Queuing is a great cultural leveler because it makes everyone pay the cost of waiting with their own personal time. That's why we feel uncomfortable when lobbyists pay people to queue for congressional hearings—it effectively squeezes ordinary citizens out of the chance to attend. Some argue queuing costs the rich more than the poor, but don't forget: we don't want certain resources to become economically fungible commodities. That's why buying and selling votes or kidneys is illegal in most civilized countries.

The same principle, reasonably generalized, applies to any arena where electronic agents compete with humans—not just queuing. Do the participants have different abilities or costs to use the resource? This must be answered case by case, but the concept is clear. For instance, if I send my robot to move my car every two hours to avoid a ticket, or have my self-driving car repark itself, is my cost the same as someone without a robot driver or self-driving car? What if my cost of using a robot equals your cost of using a human administrative assistant?

I'd argue that letting synthetic intelligence duke it out to show you ads is far fairer than letting high-frequency trading programs loose in the securities markets. In the ad example, humans generally don't compete (though they did in the early internet), so every bidder stands on more equal footing.

We can easily see if the boss's robot is moving his car. But in other cases, it's not so easy to tell—like the weekend Yellowstone campsites open to the public, where you want to camp but a whole row of sites has been snapped up by someone's clever script. We need to bring these concepts into public discourse before we can extend our sense of fairness into the electronic realm. Today, this vast open frontier is shrouded in impenetrable darkness, attracting all manner of deception.

There are more subtle issues to resolve on synthetic intelligence's path to becoming true agents.

## 05. Robot Crime: Who's Responsible?

### The Law of the Future

You didn't do it; your robot did.

In the Middle Ages, animals were put on criminal trial. Records exist of prosecutions against chickens, rats, field mice, bees, gnats, and pigs.[^1] That era was very different; people believed animals knew right from wrong and could act on principle—they believed animals possessed `moral agency`.

A widely accepted definition of a moral agent requires the capacity to do two things. It must be able to perceive the morally relevant aspects of its actions, and it must be able to choose between courses of action.

Interestingly, neither condition depends on any subjective, innate sense of right and wrong. It merely requires the moral agent to control its behavior according to recognized moral standards and assess the impact of its actions. For a moral agent, it doesn't matter whether the standard is self-generated, whether they understand the reasoning behind it, whether they agree with it, or whether they can "feel" the difference between justice and evil.

Consider psychopaths by comparison. They lack empathy or remorse entirely, yet many—perhaps most—are highly intelligent, understand moral concepts, and can control their behavior accordingly—they just don't have an emotional reaction to moral issues. Psychologists estimate over 1% of the US population are psychopaths.[^2] Yet we don't see one in a hundred people randomly committing crimes. A psychopath might privately think "what's the big deal," but they know how they're supposed to act, and most manage to get along just fine.

Today we might find the medieval view of animal culpability absurd, but the modern interpretation of moral agency has long since extended beyond humans.

### Sophie's Choice

In 2010, the Deepwater Horizon oil rig in the Gulf of Mexico suffered a blowout. Eleven workers died, and massive amounts of oil polluted waters and beaches. The US federal government brought *criminal* charges (not just civil) against the rig's owner—BP. BP eventually settled for $4 billion, far more than any civil penalty.

The criminal prosecution of BP shows that a moral agent need not be conscious or sentient. In our legal system, a corporation bears moral responsibility and criminal liability. That is, BP *should* have known better and had the capacity to do the right thing to prevent the disaster, but failed. Unlike an employee, a corporation has a duty to exercise sufficient control over its operations to prevent such occurrences.

Modern legal theory accepts that both people and corporations can be moral agents, and thus can be charged with crimes. What about synthetic intelligence? Can it meet the requirements for moral responsibility?

Yes.

If a synthetic intelligence has sufficient capacity to perceive morally relevant aspects of its environment and choose its actions, it qualifies as a moral agent. These systems need not be complex enough to cross some seemingly anthropological threshold. For instance, a lawnmower would need to recognize that it's about to run over a child's leg, not a stick, and choose whether to stop or continue. The question, of course, is how it "knows" when to stop and when to continue. Without some form of guidance, we can't expect it to reason to the right decision.

#### Intelligence Insight

This isn't a distant problem. A quiet intellectual debate is already underway on how to program self-driving cars. However much we'd like to avoid it, self-driving cars will easily generate morally challenging scenarios, and such products are almost inevitably coming to our streets. Your self-driving car can run over a dog to save you: obviously, you'd want it to. But what if the choice is running over an elderly couple or hitting a group of schoolchildren crossing the street? If it must kill one of your children—the one in the front seat or the one in the back—what is your "Sophie's Choice"?[^14] We can ignore these painful questions, but ignoring them is itself immoral.

Okay, let's grit our teeth and program with moral guidelines. Sounds like an engineering problem, but it's not that simple. Though we've poured enormous effort into this, experts haven't reached consensus on what those moral guidelines should be. For centuries, philosophers have built elaborate fortifications of moral theory and ethical argument to decide what is best or most workable, yet the debate remains unresolved.

Even if we reach consensus, there's no reason to believe it can be easily reduced to practice and implemented in code. Some researchers in the new field of computational ethics are trying a "top-down" approach to create "artificial moral agents." They reason to select and implement moral principles, then build systems that attempt to follow them (deontological normative ethics). Others pursue a "bottom-up" strategy, showing machine learning algorithms enough relevant examples to derive the answers. But this has an obvious flaw: like humans, machines can't be guaranteed to acquire and implement the required moral principles, let alone articulate them clearly. Other methods include "case-based reasoning," essentially solving moral problems by associating them with a catalog of known cases (under similar premises). A problem plaguing this nascent field is that part of our moral compass seems to come from our human capacity for empathy and compassion—we instinctively reason that if something hurts us, we shouldn't inflict that pain on others. This shortcut to moral behavior probably won't work for machines. In short, we still don't know how to instruct engineers in the practice of moral programming.[^3]

### Arrest That Robot

Beyond the issue of machine moral agency, we have another problem: when decisions go wrong, who is responsible? To answer this, we need to understand the legal theory of the relationship between "principal" and "agent." To grasp this, we return to the BP case.

You might wonder how a corporation commits a crime, as opposed to its employees. Eleven people died on the Deepwater Horizon, but that doesn't mean any individual was negligent or engaged in criminal activity. Quite the opposite—every employee may have fulfilled their assigned duties, and no one's job was to kill those eleven people.

Employees are the *means* by which a corporation commits a crime. By analogy, when you rob a bank, your legs are just the means to get you inside. Your legs obviously bear no criminal liability. But there's a crucial difference: your legs carrying you into the bank is different from Deepwater Horizon managers failing to monitor or correct dangerous conditions. Managers are the corporation's "agents," and thus potentially share responsibility.

An agent is an authorized entity that, by agreement, acts on behalf of a principal. Your legs are neither an independent entity nor did they knowingly agree to act on your behalf. BP's employees, however, are independent entities who knowingly acted for BP.

When representing you, your agent has a fiduciary duty to carry out your wishes and protect your interests—but with limits. For instance, if your agent knowingly commits a crime for you, they can't escape liability. Assuming you know you're part of a criminal conspiracy, if I hire you to kill my rival, you're liable for the murder too.

But what if an agent commits a crime unknowingly? I say "press this button," you do, and a bomb goes off at the Super Bowl.[^15] You're my agent, but you're only liable if you reasonably should have known the consequences.

Now let's reverse it. Suppose an agent commits a crime in the principal's service, unknowingly. I send you to the bank to withdraw $100. You go with a gun, hand the teller a note demanding cash in a bag. You return and give me the money. Am I responsible for your robbery? In most cases, no. (I'm simplifying: an innocent principal who benefits from a crime may still face liability.)

There's a long history of legal principles and precedent on who bears responsibility in principal-agent relationships, or more precisely, how to apportion liability when responsibilities among entities are unclear.
