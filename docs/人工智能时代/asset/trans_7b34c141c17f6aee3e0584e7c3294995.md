# Introduction

## Welcome to the Future

The question of whether machines can think is like the question of whether submarines can swim.

Edsger Dijkstra
`Edsger Dijkstra`, Computer Scientist

After 50 years of effort and billions of dollars in research funding, we seem to be on the verge of cracking the AI code. But in fact, AI is not the same as human intelligence, at least not yet. But that doesn't matter. As computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra said: "The question of whether machines can think is like the question of whether submarines can swim." Websites that help you find dates and robots that mow your lawn—whether they do it the same way you do doesn't matter; they complete these tasks at speeds, accuracy, and lower costs you can never achieve.

Accelerated development of computer technology has driven progress in robotics, perception, and machine learning. These achievements allow a new generation of systems to match or even surpass human capabilities. These developments may well open an unprecedented era of prosperity and comfort, but the transition process may be long and brutal. If we do not adjust our economic systems and regulatory policies, we may fall into endless social turmoil.

Warning signs are everywhere. The two great disasters of the modern developed world—persistent unemployment and worsening income imbalance—torture our society, even during sustained economic growth. If we turn a blind eye to these phenomena, we may see large-scale poverty occurring against a backdrop of increasing comfort and wealth. My goal in writing this book is to serve as a guide, leading you through the technological advances driving this transformation, and showing you the challenges this change will bring to society. I will also propose some free-market solutions that can drive progress while reducing government intervention in our lives.

### Intelligence Is Freeing Your Hands

AI research has broken through in two directions. The first type of new system has entered the application stage; they learn from experience. But unlike humans, whose ability to absorb experience is limited by breadth and scale, these systems can examine massive meaningful samples at extremely high speeds. They can not only understand the visual, auditory, and written information familiar to us, but also understand the data "traversing" computers and networks that we are unfamiliar with. Imagine how smart you would become if you could see with thousands of eyes, hear sounds from afar, and read all published content! You could then savor and contemplate this world at leisure, and you would know how these systems perceive their environment.

We accumulate data from more and more `sensors` (monitoring air quality, traffic flow, wave height, etc.), and our own digital footprints (web searches, blogs, credit card transactions) are growing wider. These systems can grasp patterns and insights beyond the reach of the human brain. You might think they exhibit superhuman intelligence, but that is absolutely a misunderstanding—at least in the foreseeable future—because these machines have no consciousness, cannot reflect, and show no independent will or personal desires. In other words, they have no thoughts. They are extremely good at certain things, but we don't fully understand how they accomplish these tasks. In most cases, this is because for simple creatures like us, there simply is no explanation we can understand.

Research in this field doesn't have a widely accepted name. Depending on focus and methodology, researchers call it machine learning, neural networks, big data, cognitive systems, genetic algorithms, etc. I call them "Synthetic Intellects" based on the general purpose of these products.

Synthetic Intellects are not obtained through traditional programming. You assemble materials and set goals from a variety of ever-increasing tools and modules, point them at a series of instances, and then set them free. The final form of the system is unpredictable and not controlled by its creators. Soon, Synthetic Intellects will know you better than your mother, predict your behavior more accurately than yourself, and warn you of undetectable dangers. I will describe in detail how Synthetic Intellects work and why these systems can surpass our general understanding of computers.

The second type of new system comes from the combination of `sensors` and `actuators`. They can see, hear, feel, and interact with their environment. When sensors and actuators combine, you view this system as a "robot," but putting both in the same physical container is not necessary. In fact, in most cases, doing so is not ideal. Sensors can be scattered in an environment, like on streetlights or in smartphones, while the observation end of the sensors is collected and stored on a distant server cluster, which uses this information to formulate plans. This plan may be executed directly (e.g., by controlling remote devices) or indirectly (e.g., coaxing you into making certain expected moves). Usually, the results of these moves are perceived immediately, and the system continuously corrects the plan, just as you do when using your hand to pick up an object.

When you follow a navigator's guidance, you are in such a system. This program monitoring your location and speed (usually via `GPS`) commands you. It aggregates information from you and other drivers to detect traffic conditions and uses this information to formulate more efficient routes for you and others.

Perhaps the more powerful these systems are, the simpler they seem, because they perform tasks people take for granted. Although these systems lack common sense and general intelligence, they can tirelessly complete a vast array of tasks in chaotic and changing environments.

So far, common so-called automation mostly refers to concentrating special-purpose machines in factory workshops to perform repetitive single tasks, and the surrounding environment is designed for them. In contrast, new systems will perform tasks outside: they will farm, paint houses, clean sidewalks, wash and fold clothes. They may lay pipes in concert halls alongside human workers, harvest crops with farmers, build houses with workers, or be deployed alone to dangerous or human-inaccessible environments to fight fires, inspect dangerous bridges, mine the seabed, or fight on battlefields. I call these systems "Forged Labors."

Of course, these two types of systems—Synthetic Intellects and Forged Labors—can collaborate to complete physical tasks requiring advanced knowledge and skills, such as repairing cars, performing surgery, and cooking gourmet meals.

In principle, these new systems will not only free you from painful chores but also make you more efficient—provided you can afford them. Customized `electronic agents` can maximize your personal interests, represent you in negotiations, and even teach you calculus—but not all these systems will work for you.

Humans are easily deceived by immediate interests. Prescient thinker Jaron Lanier calls such systems "Siren Servers": they tailor short-term incentives to your wishes, persuading you to do things not in your long-term interest.[\[1\]](#part0031.xhtml#bz100) The irresistible temptation of impulse buying and rapid delivery may blind you to the slow, destructive impact on the lifestyle you cherish. You can buy a rice cooker online tonight and have it delivered tomorrow, but the value does not include the shops slowly closing near your home or your neighbor who is about to lose their job.

Having these systems recommend music you should listen to or toothbrushes you should buy is one thing, but when we allow them to act autonomously, the situation is completely different. Because their reaction time is almost imperceptible to us, once they master massive data that humans cannot understand or control, they can cause unimaginable disasters in a split second—shut down power grids, ground all flights, cancel millions of credit cards.
