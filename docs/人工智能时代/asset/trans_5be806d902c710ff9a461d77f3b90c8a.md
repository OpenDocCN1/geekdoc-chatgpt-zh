
Whatever you hear in the news, global warming isn't all bad. There are winners and losers, the deciding factor being where you happen to live. For me, I live in a place that's a bit chilly, and the upside of global warming is that the average temperature where I live is projected to rise a few degrees over the coming decades. Sounds good.

Global warming itself isn't the problem. After all, life on Earth has survived countless cycles of cooling and heating. The real problem with global warming is its rate of change. Without enough time for organisms to adapt, rapid climate change sows the seeds of disaster, to say nothing of unstable weather patterns. The consequences of catastrophic climate change can last for centuries, during which the habitats of all manner of species suffer terrible calamities, leading to mass extinction.

## It's Not Just Jobs That Are Obsolete, It's Skills

The impact of technological change on the labor market is much the same. As long as change is gradual, the market adjusts automatically. If change happens too fast, the market becomes a mess. Just like my preference for a specific climate, this situation simultaneously creates winners and losers.

The latest advances in artificial intelligence may disrupt our labor market in two fundamental ways. The first is the simple fact that most automation replaces workers, thereby reducing job opportunities. That means fewer places where human labor is needed. This threat is easy to see and easy to measure. Employers bring in robots by the truckload and clear out the workers. But sometimes the change isn't so obvious. Every new workstation server might reduce the need for 1/5 of a salesperson, or free `Skype` calls might let you work one day a week from home more effectively, pushing the need to hire a new employee into the next quarter.

If these things happen slowly, the efficiency gains and cost reductions they bring will eventually create wealth and stimulate job growth, and these benefits will compensate for losses elsewhere. This growth may appear directly in the newly improved enterprises, because lower prices and better quality increase sales, creating demand to hire more workers. For customers who no longer have to pay as much for certain goods or services, they may decide to spend the money in a completely different sector of the economy. If drilling technology lowers the price of natural gas, you'll have more money to buy that sailboat you've had your eye on.

The second threat is more subtle and harder to predict. Many technological advances change the rules of the game by forcing businesses to reorganize and rebuild their operations. Such organizational evolution and process improvements often obsolete not just jobs, but skills.

A bank installs `ATM`s and lays off tellers; upgraded services create demand for network engineers rather than tellers. Even if the bank eventually increases its total headcount, the tellers are still out of luck. Textile workers eventually learned to operate power looms, gardeners learned to use lawnmowers, and doctors learned to use computers to select the right antibiotics—once they realized synthetic judgment surpassed their own professional opinion. But learning new skills isn't instantaneous, and sometimes surplus workers simply lack the capacity to adapt—leaving us to wait for a new generation of workers.

Take, for example, a successful labor market transition we've already experienced—agriculture. In the 19th century, farms still employed 80% of the workforce.[[1]](#part0031.xhtml#bz169) Think about what that means. Until recently, food production was still the primary occupation people pursued to stay alive, so there's no doubt this pattern had never changed since the invention of agriculture 5,000 years ago.

But by 1900, that number had dropped by half to 40%, and today it's only 1.5%, including unpaid family farm workers and undocumented immigrants.[[2]](#part0031.xhtml#bz170) Essentially, we successfully automated nearly everyone's job out of existence, yet without causing mass unemployment: people were freed from labor to pursue many other productive, wealth-creating activities. So over the past 200 years, the US economy was able to absorb about 50% of the agricultural workforce per year without any noticeable disruption.

Now, imagine if all this happened over 20 years instead of 200. Your father worked on the farm, and so did your grandfather. A massive transformation in agriculture completely upended the entire industry, and it all seemed to happen in the blink of an eye. Shiny new plows, threshers, and harvesters rumbled across the land; the air smelled of diesel. Food prices plummeted, and backed by deep-pocketed Wall Street financiers, corporations began buying up farms everywhere. Within a few years, your family farm and everything on it would be foreclosed, leaving you with nothing but a `Bible`.

You and your five siblings had an average of a third-grade education. You found that your various skills—shoeing horses, plowing straight furrows, baling hay—were now completely useless. So were your neighbors'. But you still had to eat. A friend of a friend worked 12 hours a day operating a new machine in exchange for three meals a day; he might have found that job in Topeka, the capital of Kansas, so you moved to the vast tent cities springing up on the fringes of major Midwestern cities, hoping to find work—any work. Before long, you heard your parents sold the `Bible` to buy medicine for your youngest sister, but she died of dysentery anyway. Eventually, you lost track of your other siblings.

For the 1% who still had jobs, they lived in small company houses, just getting by, but they were the envy of everyone else—at least they had a solid roof over their heads. Every day, you queued outside their gated communities, hoping for a chance to do their laundry or deliver their lunch. Rumor had it that the daughter of that famous entrepreneur who changed the world used his vast fortune to build a magnificent art museum made of crystal in a small town in Arkansas. But all this happened before the revolution. After that, things got much worse.

I'm telling you this because I believe a similar earth-shattering change is imminent, though it certainly won't be this dramatic and will be more humane.

In the early stages of application, many new technologies will directly replace workers, doing the job in much the same way. But other innovations will not only idle workers but obsolete the very types of work they do.

Consider how `Amazon` has continuously improved storage patterns in its warehouses. If a human were planning the warehousing, goods might be arranged in a logical, easy-to-understand way—like keeping similar items together—so when you want something, you know where to look. But the synthetic intelligence `Amazon` built doesn't obey such constraints. Similar items might be placed next to other items frequently shipped together, or on any shelf where they can be packed more tightly. To a human, it looks like a mess—products of different sizes and shapes randomly stuffed into every nook and cranny. That's why this type of warehouse organization is called "chaotic storage."[[3]](#part0031.xhtml#bz171) But synthetic intelligence can track everything and guide workers precisely to the right locations to fulfill orders, far more efficiently than any human organizer.

A side effect of introducing this innovation is that it reduces the training and knowledge required of warehouse workers, making them easier to replace with artificial workers. These employees no longer need to be familiar with where goods are on the shelves; indeed, it's nearly impossible for people to do so in a random, constantly changing environment. As the first company to simplify the skills required for the job, `Amazon` can now replace those busy workers picking orders in the warehouse. That's probably why `Amazon` spent $775 million in 2012 to acquire the robotics company `Kiva Systems`.[[4]](#part0031.xhtml#bz172)

## The Chronically Unemployed vs. The Unemployable

Considering the two distinct impacts of automation on labor—replacing workers and rendering skills obsolete—economists have given two different names to these types of unemployment. The first is called cyclical unemployment, referring to people cycling between employment and unemployment.[[5]](#part0031.xhtml#bz173) During economic downturns, the number of unemployed poor may grow, leading to higher unemployment rates. But historically, once the economy recovers, idle workers find new jobs. The number of unemployed decreases, and the duration of unemployment shortens. This is like the real estate market: in a down market, more houses are for sale, and they take longer to sell. But when the market picks up, the excess inventory is immediately absorbed.

I was surprised when I got data on US labor market turnover. In 2013, a fairly normal year, 40% of workers changed jobs.[[6]](#part0031.xhtml#bz174) This is a highly fluid market. By contrast, less than 4% of houses are sold each year.[[7]](#part0031.xhtml#bz175) So when we talk about an 8% unemployment rate, it doesn't take long for small changes in the rate of job creation and destruction to absorb that unemployment, or conversely, to put more people out of work.

The other form of unemployment is called structural unemployment, meaning there are unemployed people who simply cannot find suitable work. They send out resumes all day, but no one wants to hire them because their skills don't match any available jobs.[[8]](#part0031.xhtml#bz176) In real estate terms, this is like having houses for sale that don't suit the existing buyers. Couples wanting three kids need more bedrooms than those wanting fewer, or for those who need to commute by flying car, they need a flat roof for takeoff, while most current houses have pitched roofs.

As you can see from these examples, the factors that change the criteria for an ideal house usually change slowly, giving builders and renovators plenty of time to adapt. But with automation, it's not like that, because the pace of invention and the speed of application change rapidly and unpredictably. The characteristics of entire labor sub-markets change much faster than people can learn new skills—if they can be retrained at all. We are buffeted by these capricious tides precisely because these changes are hard to predict and nearly impossible to measure.

Economists and scholars who study labor markets have a natural bias toward quantifiable problems. Understandably, to sound a credible alarm, they must have hard data. Their conclusions must withstand objective, independent peer review, which essentially means they must speak with numbers. But as I've learned in business, spreadsheets and financial statements can only express specific things, and those trends that cannot be reduced to measurable metrics are often the key drivers of outcomes. (Of course, it's worth noting that the erratic business cycles that plague our economy are largely because returns are easy to calculate, but risks are not.) I've seen countless management teams make exquisitely detailed but completely false sales forecasts. At work, I sometimes feel my most important contribution as a manager is anticipating data that hasn't yet become quantifiable.

For the labor market as a whole, unemployment statistics or aggregate rates of change obscure the real situation because the types of useful skills are changing irregularly. Disappearing labor habitats and evolving work ecologies intertwine into a vast web whose complexity cannot be analyzed with traditional mathematical tools. That's why efforts to quantify the whole process often drown in a sea of charts, ending in futility.

Fortunately, I'm not bound by the same professional constraints, so buckle up for a high-speed trip into the future. My method is to paint a broader picture through specific examples, using analogy. Let's start with retail—confirmed by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (`BLS`) as the largest commercial job market.[[9]](#part0031.xhtml#bz177)

The `BLS` reports that 10% of all US workers, or nearly 14.5 million people, work in retail.[[10]](#part0031.xhtml#bz178) To analyze industry dynamics, let's treat salespeople as representative of the whole group. The `BLS` predicts this labor force (4.4 million in 2012) will grow by 10 percentage points over the next 10 years, reaching 4.9 million. But this prediction is based on current demographic trends, not a qualitative analysis of the industry's current situation.

To understand what's really happening, consider the impact of the shift from brick-and-mortar stores to online retail on employment. An effective method is to use a statistic called Revenue Per Employee (`RPE`). This is a standard metric for measuring corporate efficiency, or at least labor efficiency.

`Amazon`, the largest online retailer, has averaged $855,000 in revenue per employee over the past 5 years.[[11]](#part0031.xhtml#bz179) `Walmart`, the largest brick-and-mortar retailer, has revenue per employee of about $213,000—among the highest in retail. This means to achieve $1 million in sales, `Walmart` needs to hire 5 people; `Amazon` needs just over 1 person. So every time $1 million in sales shifts from `Walmart` to `Amazon`, roughly 4 jobs are lost.

Now, the two companies sell basically the same goods, and `Walmart` sells a fair amount online too, so the job losses from the shift to online sales are underestimated. And neither company is standing still; both will likely become more efficient in the future.

To determine the upper bound of job losses, imagine if all retail sales magically shifted overnight from stores like `Walmart` to sites like `Amazon`. The 10% of the workforce primarily working for stores would be replaced by the 2% working for online retailers. That's an 8% reduction in US jobs—higher than the unemployment rate for all of 2014. Are we in big trouble? Not necessarily. Of course, not all sales will move online—your favorite mall won't close, and change will take time. But how long?

Although it looks massive, only 6% of US retail sales currently happen online. This figure has been growing steadily at about 15% per year for the past 4 years.[[12]](#part0031.xhtml#bz180) If online sales continue at this rate for the next 20 years (unlikely), and if all retail growth flows to the online market (equally unlikely), online retail would at most capture half of total retail sales in 20 years. Based on the past 20 years, total retail sales will roughly double, but the jobs added accompanying sales growth would be only about 10%.[[13]](#part0031.xhtml#bz181) And in this scenario, brick-and-mortar stores don't grow at all, which is also unrealistic.

Meanwhile, what happens to the workforce? According to precise demographic projections, the `BLS` estimates the workforce will grow only 12% over the next 20 years.[[14]](#part0031.xhtml#bz182) In other words, this massive shift from brick-and-mortar to more labor-efficient online retail will likely only cause a 2% negative employment impact over that period (meaning 12% workforce growth only slightly exceeds 10% retail labor demand). That's only 0.1% unemployment per year for the economy to absorb, compared to an average annual agricultural job loss rate of 0.5% over the past 200 years. The story gets better. If retail sales double, new jobs will certainly be created in various industries—design, manufacturing, and logistics related to goods will generously compensate for losses elsewhere.

Did I just add logistics to that list? Sorry, logistics is a whole other story. In 2012, there were 1.7 million long-haul truck drivers in the US. Their job is to operate tractor-trailers and other heavy freight vehicles that frequently traverse interstate highways. The `BLS` predicts demand for these drivers will grow 11 percentage points over the next 10 years. Impossible.

You might think highway driving requires higher skills and more mature experience than city streets, but for autonomous driving technology, it's the opposite. This technology is a brilliant combination of synthetic intelligence and artificial workers. Highways are usually well-maintained, have fewer randomly moving obstacles (like pedestrians and bicycles), and are more predictable than the streets near your home.

### Autonomous Convoys with Zero Reaction Time

The technology for self-driving trucks exists today and can be retrofitted to existing fleets at very reasonable prices. Trucks equipped with this technology can "see" in all directions, not just forward. They can operate in total darkness or blackout conditions, instantly sharing road conditions, nearby hazards, and their own intentions. (Essentially, they rely on fine-grained 3D radar called "`LiDAR`," combined with detailed maps and `GPS`, so they don't need headlights.)

Even better, their reaction time is near zero. So autonomous truck convoys can travel safely with gaps of only a few centimeters between vehicles. Such convoys reduce road congestion and save 15% or more on fuel.[[15]](#part0031.xhtml#bz183) Deliveries become faster because they can run non-stop without pulling over to rest. They don't get tired, drunk, sick, distracted, or bored; they don't doze off, make phone calls, or strike for better pay and conditions. How many of the 273,000 large truck accidents (2011) that cost $44 billion and claimed 3,800 lives can be avoided in the future?[[16]](#part0031.xhtml#bz184) Let me point out that this single innovation could save more lives per year than were lost in the "9/11" World Trade Center disaster.

This system isn't a futuristic daydream; it's been tested on real highways and other sites. Not long ago, news reported: "`Rio Tinto` has unleashed a fleet of 150 autonomous trucks at its Pilbara iron ore mines—the world's first major deployment of autonomous trucks. During a two-year trial, from day one, the autonomous trucks operated 24 hours a day, moving 42 million tonnes of material in 145,000 cycles. They traveled over 450,000 kilometers. We control the trucks from an operations center in `Perth`, 1,500 km away. Truck routes are pre-defined, from loading zones to dump points, with `GPS` enabling autonomous navigation."[[17]](#part0031.xhtml#bz185)

Even if you're not a futurist, you should know what's coming. Will there be nearly 2 million long-haul truck drivers in 10 years? I suspect the `BLS` is wildly wrong this time—zero is more likely. But this is just one application of autonomous driving technology. Will over 5.7 million licensed US commercial drivers (2012) lose their jobs to variants of this technology?[[18]](#part0031.xhtml#bz186) In any case, I wouldn't recommend this career to my children.

## The Blue-Collar Labor Market Under Siege

Looking at the totals, drivers will likely lose their jobs in droves, while retail employees won't. But there's an unexpected twist—the raw data obscures a more profound truth. The real issue isn't the total number of viable jobs, but the skills required to do them.
