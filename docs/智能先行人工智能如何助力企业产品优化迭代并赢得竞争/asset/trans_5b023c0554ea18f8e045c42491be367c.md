## AI-First Aggregation

This creates an opportunity for vast amounts of freely flowing data worldwide: to aggregate it, manage it with intelligent systems, and distribute it widely, thereby generating more data, and so on. Figure 8-7 illustrates AI-first aggregation.

![](EPUB/images/image_086.jpg)

Figure 8-7 AI-First Aggregation

When we generate massive amounts of data, the opportunity for aggregation arises. Low transaction costs on the internet mean it is more convenient to promote the use of new products—people just need to enter credit card numbers online to use more products and generate more data. The internet also lowers distribution costs—just download an app—making it easier to sell products, attract more customers, and collect more usage data. Furthermore, the internet lowers marginal costs—cloud computing is relatively cheap—enabling cheap products to be offered to customers online. All this leads to more customers using more products and generating more data.

When something exists in large quantities, to gain more value one cannot simply keep producing it in bulk; instead, one must develop and manage it. Massive data is not a product, but information can be. The Big Data era gave us products that can manually store, manage, and discover data. AI can automatically manage all this data and help us discover content relevant to the decisions that must be made. AI turns data into products.

Below are the characteristics of AI-first aggregators:

- **Establish direct relationships with customers.** AI-first aggregators generate predictions that customers use daily for decision-making. Incumbent companies are the systems of record behind AI-first products, just as databases are behind software applications. Unless a failure occurs, customers never interact with the database. For example, you don't deal with the company that owns movie rights, catalogs them, and distributes them globally; you just click play on the next title Netflix suggests.
- **Serve customers at zero marginal cost.** With AI-first aggregators, production costs decrease as automation increases. Incumbent companies scale gradually by manually adding features to their products or providing more services. For example, offline publications require a translator to translate every page of a manuscript before printing, whereas online publications can use automatic language translation products to translate articles and deliver them to more customers worldwide.
- **Zero distribution costs.** AI-first aggregators integrate or piggyback their products with products customers already use, then deliver them to customers. For example, an AI-first application can retrieve identity tickets from the software program JIRA (developed by Australian software company Atlassian Corporation) and learn how to route these tickets to the right engineers. It can both aggregate data and learn from data sold through that company's marketplace. However, many incumbent companies have no product to piggyback on and must spend money on marketing to expand their market in order to sell products to customers.
- **Zero transaction costs.** AI-first aggregators run their systems on other vendors' infrastructure. In effect, many incumbent companies are the databases from which AI-first products obtain data. A good example is these companies plugging into Salesforce software applications. These applications can plug into the Salesforce system and run within it, extracting and processing data on Salesforce's compute infrastructure, effectively reducing the marginal cost of AI-first products processing data to zero.

Aggregation theory explains not only why companies like Netflix currently have massive market dominance, but also why AI-first companies may possess even greater market dominance. Streaming entertainment services possess a critical mass of customers (demand), incentivizing content production (supply); in a positive self-reinforcing loop, more content attracts more customers. AI-first aggregators have the potential to build a faster self-reinforcing loop because their customers are both the demand side and the supply side of this loop. Essentially, a critical mass of customers (demand) can generate enough data for the AI to produce increasingly accurate predictions. In turn, higher prediction accuracy encourages these customers to use the product more. Customers generate enough data to kickstart the AI flywheel; then, in a rapidly self-reinforcing loop, the AI automatically generates enough data to improve system accuracy, thereby increasing product adoption, allowing AI-first companies to quickly establish market dominance.

### Integrating Incumbent Companies

Software needs to integrate with existing workflows to function. AI-first aggregators reverse the direction of software integration, attracting incumbent companies' customers.

- **Incumbent companies integrate backward,** building a workflow tool for daily customer use, then putting the data from that workflow into a database.
- **AI-first companies integrate forward,** pulling data from the database and building an intelligent system for daily customer use.

AI-first aggregation differs from vertical integration: aggregation concerns data and aims to promote adoption; vertical integration involves data, services, infrastructure, interfaces, etc., and increases profits. Furthermore, AI-first aggregation promotes the development of new products used to aggregate data streams from the internet; these new products have advantages over incumbent products, can extract data from incumbent products, then integrate and sell through them, thereby subordinating the incumbent products. ## Positioning

In the history of software development, to prevent customers from abandoning their products for competitors', software companies typically deeply integrate their products with the customer's usage environment. This section draws lessons from the broader tech industry and applies them to AI-first product R&D, drafting a new framework to embed products into an industry at three levels: storage, standards, and ecosystems. Additionally, this section provides a list of items that must appear in vendor contracts for those purchasing AI-first products. Figure 8-10 illustrates product positioning.

The following are the ways AI-first products store core assets, set standards, build ecosystems, build brands, and capture value first.

### Store Core Assets

Embed data storage management into AI-first products: for example, provide powerful image search tools, but only to customers who agree to store all their images in your database, or who possess sensors that capture and store data. However, storing customer data requires negotiating data rights with them. To some extent, this is a choice: whether to acquire a large client on stricter terms, using a critical mass of data to train a high-accuracy model, then quickly onboard the rest of the market on looser terms; or whether to acquire many smaller clients on looser terms, whose aggregate data volume reaches a critical value, enabling the model to be trained to high precision. This choice depends on the market's data dynamics: whether one or a few clients possess a critical mass of data. If a single client's data volume already reaches the critical value and they agree to sign a valid data rights agreement, then pursuing that one client suffices; otherwise, pursue multiple clients.

For example, companies can also embed this in AI-first products by requiring clients to upload models to cloud services for storage. That is, models must be uploaded to the cloud. This is similar to how most cloud-based software vendors manage product storage and services.

### Set Standards

Vendors must adhere to product production standards, embedding the standard-setter's products into an ecosystem. This embedded technology approach differs from so-called vendor lock-in, because vendor lock-in revolves around a business arrangement with restrictive terms. Standard setting can be achieved in two ways: by adopting more advanced technology, or by occupying a dominant market position. Many great companies have embedded their technology into ecosystems, such as SAP, Oracle, and Microsoft.

Standards manifested as documentation and training lock in protocols, data, products, personnel, and procedures.

- **Protocol lock-in** comes from other vendors needing to adhere to a specific system architecture (e.g., data formats or model structures). This lowers switching costs for potential customers, making it easier to adopt standards-compliant products, but raises switching costs for current customers, because finding a vendor using that protocol or reformatting data can be costly. In the data world, the most famous example is probably Microsoft Excel. Microsoft's analytics product has powerful lock-in because its format is proprietary. Making protocols transparent and editable—for instance, open-sourcing data—is a primary way to drive industry adoption of a standard.
- **Data lock-in** may come from other vendors needing to send data through a specific system, allowing data collection in transit. If the intelligent systems trained on this data are better than competitors', data lock-in may increase. For example, SAP is the center of supply chain data; anyone developing new software to help understand supply chains must use its data protocols.
- **Product lock-in** comes from other vendors needing to run their software within a specific interface. Since differences between competing product interfaces are small, product lock-in increases. For example, Salesforce provides a feature-rich CRM system allowing vendors to build applications on top of it, provided those applications run on the Salesforce interface. This makes it hard for customers to see the added value of applications running inside Salesforce.
- **Personnel lock-in** comes from client employees who are accustomed to using products based on its architecture, data, or interface. This increases competitors' switching costs. For example, over the past decades, Oracle—directly or indirectly—trained many developers using its proprietary database query language. Today, these people can easily move between other companies that also use Oracle databases.
- **Process lock-in** stems from work partners adhering to operational procedures to control quality. This increases switching costs because current clients won't choose another vendor unless they reach an agreement with a partner—typically called a System Integrator (SI) or Independent Software Vendor (ISV)—who follows those procedures. For example, cloud computing and virtualization software company VMware trains system integrators to correctly install its hardware in compute infrastructure. Clients are unlikely to risk choosing a new integrator to build a brand-new compute infrastructure.

In determining a tech company's product and marketing strategy, when and how to pursue standardization to lock in customers may be one of the most critical questions. Computers are highly complex, requiring standardized coordination across the entire ecosystem to function. When to set standards depends on whether it is a new market or a mature one.

To broadly promote their technology, companies pioneering new markets are usually more willing to drive standardization. Standards not only allow customers to easily integrate existing systems—for example, by putting data into their AI-first products in standard data formats—but also open the door for third-party developers to build complementary features. For instance, a simple interface allowing customers to adjust parameters in AI models can expand the product market from technical to non-technical customers. With standards, companies can more easily position themselves as versatile players, using tools built on these standards to understand new technologies. Versatile companies with custom application capabilities grow rapidly at this stage because the product space is fragmented. We see, for example, that at this stage of the market lifecycle, startups delay focusing on vertical markets.

Dominant firms in mature markets tend to lower standardization levels, promote fragmentation, or implement highly detailed standards to reduce competition from new entrants. Standards facilitate new entrants entering mature markets, as they can use all specifications in the standard to quickly build products and offer them to customers already accustomed to the standard. For new entrants, lowering standardization levels hinders building a product that customers can easily switch to. Promoting fragmentation confuses customers, forcing them toward the dominant vendor with the most stable architecture. Detailed standards reduce new entrants' ability to produce differentiated products, because product functionality is dictated by the standard; this causes customers choosing the dominant vendor to value things beyond product features, such as after-sales service or good relationships. At this point, highly specialized companies can launch mature products compliant with every standard. Consequently, they thrive.

When to execute a standardization plan depends on who plays the dominant role in a specific market. These players may be at the bottom of the ecosystem chain, e.g., hardware manufacturers running software. Everyone hopes hardware manufacturers adopt relevant standards; otherwise, all software fails to run. Computer chip manufacturer NVIDIA holds such an advantage in the machine learning market. By multiple measures, this California-based company's chips offer the best performance for running various types of ML models. Developers learn CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture), the programming language developed by NVIDIA, to write instructions for NVIDIA chips. Once developers use CUDA, the company can showcase other new software it wants to sell to them. Figuring out who controls the key user interface and who controls the data pipeline bottleneck may be a critical factor to consider. For example, ERP software can collect and store data on product inventory. Dominance may also come from buyers rather than suppliers, as governments and other large purchasers can influence standard-setting to ensure industry standards perfectly align with their existing infrastructure.

Finally, alliances of companies and non-corporate organizations tend to set standards for the entire industry; thus, forming such alliances becomes a valuable strategic capability. The telecom industry is a classic example: the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), created by the UN, set mobile communication standards through long-term negotiations with major telecom providers, broadcasters, and handset/network equipment manufacturers. We see this in the software industry too, where one company sponsors others; e.g., with Google's sponsorship, Kubernetes (a way to write software deployable across multiple cloud computers) and Keras (a framework suite for building ML models) could consolidate internal development based on these standards, invest in the growing ecosystem, attract talent to build better products per these standards, and grow the ecosystem in a positive reinforcement loop, etc.

Ultimately, customers will reject features that lock them into a product unless those features generate significant value; therefore, promoting them prematurely before they demonstrate benefits is futile. However, promoting too late risks losing customers, who may then become interested in new entrants offering the same features. When technology is novel, adopting restrictive usage terms early is a way to protect IP; relaxing terms after competitors catch up is a wise move to expand the ecosystem around the product by allowing other products to integrate. This is both product strategy and financial consideration: product strategy concerns whether customers have functional alternatives; financial consideration concerns whether profit can be made by expanding existing customer usage or acquiring new customers—who may struggle to enter the field under relatively strict conditions and require deep integration.

### Build Ecosystem

#### Compatibility

For AI-first companies needing data from other applications to train underlying models, compatibility—introducing a new product that works with products customers already use—is a critical consideration. Truly novel, revolutionary, and vertically integrated products may be incompatible with existing products, but because the value they offer is distinct and complete, customers are more willing to adopt them, leading to holistic changes in compute, data, and staffing. In fact, revolutionary products may possess technology too novel to be compatible with existing tech. The iPhone is an example: for the first few years, Apple prohibited other developers from designing apps for it, preventing people from using Microsoft Exchange and other popular apps, but because the product was so revolutionary, it sold millions. However, creating a truly novel product and vertically integrating everything a customer might need into it from the start is very difficult. Therefore, when formulating go-to-market strategies, companies usually need to consider product compatibility. Less novel horizontal products resulting from improvements must be compatible with existing products, because their value relates to products customers are currently using. The key is for companies to objectively assess the product's relative novelty, degree of vertical integration with other market products, and dependencies. Some products may start as improvements, e.g., a system-of-record plugin; but after collecting data via the plugin to build a truly novel predictive system, they may become revolutionary products—analytics dashboards are a good example, having evolved into complete decision systems.

Not all companies must strive to make their products compatible with others at all costs. Incumbents' focus may be on building integrations, because they need the incremental functionality new products provide. Technically, incompatibility can be resolved via middleware or granting IP to key partners, supporting and incentivizing them to build integrations. There are many ways to achieve compatibility, but providing too much may let competitors steal customers. When to let other developers build on your product is a question requiring careful consideration.

#### Complementarity

Companies investing in complementary products, whether by building them or letting a developer ecosystem build them, increase customer value. SAS and Tableau are two companies producing analytics products; their products enhance other analytics products with additional statistical and visualization capabilities, respectively. When investing engineering resources in complementary product development, companies must balance whether it increases core product competitiveness against its value to customers. Encourage others not to develop complementary products that compete with the core product, but ideally develop those that might evolve into highly competitive complements.

The predecessors of AI-first products were non-intelligent workflow products that consolidated data into one place—a system of record. For example, a product predicting inventory orders needs inventory data from the system of record to train the prediction model. If there is no predecessor product—a system of record storing data—building that workflow product and offering it free to users to acquire data can embed the AI-first product more deeply.

### Capture Value First—First-Mover Advantage

Despite controversy in management strategy, entering the market first is undoubtedly a way to build competitive advantage, especially for AI startups: prominent advantages, no disadvantages. First movers can acquire scarce assets, build technology leadership, and increase switching costs before competitors. AI-first companies are exactly this. First movers collect scarce assets before competitors; AI-first companies use one of the multiple methods outlined in Chapter 3 "Acquiring Data" to collect data before competitors (and secure exclusive access rights). Google collected and stored massive search data before competitors, making its search product the best today. First movers establish technological advantage over competitors through leading R&D; AI-first companies build better intelligent systems through data collection and intelligent system design methods. Importantly, before new AI development, first movers need data for experimentation. Google long ago collected data by driving cars in the field before competitors and invested in autonomous driving research; it can be said Google has the most promising autonomous driving tech today. First movers create switching costs by integrating with existing systems and benefiting from uncertainty surrounding new entrants; AI-first companies increase switching costs by centralizing customer data in their own databases and launching highly accurate models. Google centralizes and protects customer data via emails, locations, and document collections, providing good suggestions on what to buy, where to go, even phrases to write. AI-first companies are first movers.

Sometimes, moving first can damage a company's competitive position, because potential competitors benefit from the first mover's developed technology. Increased certainty around base standards also gives customers more understanding of how to use new technology. However, AI-first companies are not affected by the potential downsides of moving first. Free-riders cannot benefit from the first mover's technology because they lack the data AI-first companies possess. For instance, there are many promising AI-based methods to improve search, but developing a search better than Google seems hard because without massive search data to train models, identifying the meaning of phrases—especially uncommon ones—is very difficult. Free-riding imitators need both technology and data. Compared to tech companies providing standard services, AI-first companies have less relevance to customer certainty generated around base standards, because AI-first companies gain technical returns through prediction accuracy, not interoperability with existing systems. Customers won't buy a system that predicts the next sales lead just because it integrates with most CRMs; they just need the lead to integrate with their current CRM and get good predictions. AI-first companies enjoy more benefits from customer certainty because their system accuracy improves with increasing real-world data. Finally, as latecomers adapt faster to market demand changes, first movers sometimes see their initial lead threatened. It must be emphasized again that this first-mover disadvantage does not apply to AI-first companies, because intelligent systems can automatically adapt to continuously incoming data. Intelligent systems are inherently dynamic, able to adjust when competitors enter the market.
