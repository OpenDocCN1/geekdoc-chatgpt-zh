
The main point of this chapter is that the data learning effect is a dynamic circular motion, but its mode of motion differs from that of a moat. They can be viewed as loops, forming the basis for selecting the right product, measuring competitive advantage, assessing risk, and generating profit. This is a guide that, when the loop breaks, can be used to light up the entire loop and gain a better understanding of it.

We start by defining loops, contemplating the nature of their motion, and comparing them with the outdated concept of moats. Then, based on the returns from solving specific problems, the data required to make AI work, and the possibility of AI drift, we provide methods for selecting the right problems and solving them with AI. After the conceptual discussion, we devote two sections to technical issues. One section introduces common methods for measuring the accuracy of machine learning models, while the other explores a method for calculating the various costs of building an AI-first company.

## Loops

This book begins by defining the data learning effect. Let's add some momentum to this definition to describe the motion characteristics of the data learning effect: circular motion.

Remember, the data learning effect has three components.

1.  **Input**: Access to unique data.
2.  **Processing**: The ability to process data.
3.  **Network**: Models that learn from processed data.

The first two parts can have their own internal loops—for example, robots roaming the world to collect data, or using machine learning techniques to process data. However, the higher-level loop of the data network effect only begins after the first two parts are completed and the model creates a data network effect, thereby completing the entire process of building the data network effect.

These loops are self-reinforcing and functionally powerful because they continuously generate their own data, serve predictions, and collect new data from observations. This is the power of the data network effect; the data learning effect thereby becomes self-sustaining and grows rapidly.

Repetition is an inherent characteristic of loop motion; loops cycle continuously. Without repetition, competitive advantage would not compound. The data learning loop cycles with every batch of feedback data. Each cycle generates a prediction, the customer takes it into the real world and acts upon it. Then, the model performs some learning and generates a new prediction. Loop stacking is shown in Figure 7-1.

![](EPUB/images/image_058.jpg)

Figure 7-1 Loop Stacking

For example, an AI-first product generates a prediction: the pasta on the supermarket shelf will run out in 15 minutes, so it alerts the clerk to check the shelf after lunch. The clerk verifies and confirms the prediction: there is no pasta on the shelf. He makes a note in his application and restocks. The camera monitoring the shelf sees: initially there was pasta, then there wasn't, then pasta appeared again. The AI receives information from the camera, the application, and the clerk's notes, learning that the prediction was accurate, and that the pasta reappeared because the clerk restocked. This proves the final stage of the loop is functioning correctly, which increases confidence in the next prediction—the next one might be sent to the clerk hours in advance. Consequently, restocking time shrinks from hours to minutes.

### The Physics of Loops

There is a fascinating physics behind loops. They can also be abstractly viewed as a kind of engine: first a steam engine, then a data engine.

1.  Two things must interact to produce a third. Think of water boiling in a boiler to produce steam, thereby generating heat.
2.  There must be more than two things interacting, or energy will dissipate. Steam produced by burning fuel is useless if not converted into motion. Before proceeding to the next step, two additional things acting somewhat like ballasts are needed to control input and output. Think of the cylinder and piston in a steam engine.
3.  These components need to act in concert so the reaction isn't too violent and doesn't consume excessive energy. The steam produced just fills the cylinder, and the piston just manages to drive the crank.
4.  There is a middle, somewhat magical step—entropy: for a period, moving objects cannot be converted into energy because they are busy forming something else. This happens when the crank almost stops... then, when a critical amount of steam forms, the crank moves again.
5.  They must do something in the real world. The piston pushes the crank (which usually pushes other objects).
6.  They must return to the initial state to start over. After every push of the crank, the piston returns to its starting position.

Now let's map the above example onto the data learning loop.

1.  Data and model react to generate a prediction.
2.  The model outputs predictions to people, but before these are applied to the real world, people process these predictions. So, the four factors are data (fuel), model (air), people acting on predictions (cylinder and piston), and the object of action (crank).
3.  Data, model, and human action are consistent, mainly because nothing acts faster than humans.
4.  Entropy occurs when humans think (cylinder) and their actions take effect in the real world (crank turning).
5.  People act on predictions, and their behavior is observable.
6.  Observations are data; the model learns from them, generates new predictions, and the next loop begins.

Ideally, all this runs efficiently without wasting energy or inputs. For a car engine, the input is gasoline. For an AI-first product, the inputs are data, engineer time, and customer attention.

Thinking in this way helps us distinguish good loops from bad ones. Good loops have ample inputs and ever-increasing entropy. Ample inputs mean more data. Increasing entropy allows humans to do many things with predictions and produce effects in the real world that match model predictions. Entropy also increases when people provide substantial feedback. By observing all impacts in the real world, digesting feedback, and integrating it to restart the loop, the model eventually solves the problem of disorder. Otherwise, there is nothing but a large amount of useless sensor output or manual feedback. This is just an abstract analogy that may help us understand how loops operate. Now let's talk about moats, a different metaphor, but one from which the loop concept arose.

### Moats vs. Loops

Traditionally, the moat surrounding a castle is considered a competitive advantage. However, a moat is a static concept. Once built, its size may increase or decrease, making it harder or easier for enemies to breach the castle, but it does not deepen automatically. The moat is shown in Figure 7-2.

## Lighting the Right Loop

Data learning loops are not just about writing code; they require significant investment, but these investments pay off. Customers are willing to invest in building data learning loops, but whether they continue to invest depends on the loop's performance, whether there are risks in obtaining results from the cycle, and whether the loop will keep running. The following content can help you judge whether customers will participate in the loop and how to keep the loop cycling.

### Product Returns

Machine learning relies on probabilistic methods to model uncertainty. Probabilities are not always accurate, so models are not always effective. When building machine learning products, keep this in mind: maximize customer expectations while avoiding unrestricted use of machine learning in high-risk situations.

### Returns (Payoffs)

Modeling the shape of the returns from product usage can determine whether the product is valuable to the customer. These returns (payoffs) are usually curve-shaped—convex curves or concave curves (as shown in Figure 7-5).

![](EPUB/images/image_062.jpg)

Convex curve returns indicate that using the product ultimately increases value (as shown in Figure 7-6). That is, the more it is used, the greater the benefit. Consumer applications are the most typical example: Google Search can be used almost anytime, and every result has some informational value. Even less-than-ideal search results cause no harm. The descending part of the convex curve and the negative value part represent the product cost in the early production stage.

![](EPUB/images/image_063.jpg)

Concave curve returns represent that using the product ultimately decreases value (as shown in Figure 7-7). That is, the more it is used, the smaller the benefit.

![](EPUB/images/image_064.jpg)

Figure 7-7 Concave Curve Returns

The keyword here is "potentially": the shape of the curve is based on probability. This seems a bit abstract, but it is very useful when considering where to apply machine learning—that is, when the expected value of using the product exceeds the cost. I remind you that expected value equals the payoff value multiplied by the probability of obtaining the payoff. We can also think of this in terms of interaction frequency. What is the product's average user experience? If it's a one-time interaction, like diagnosing a disease, the denominator of "average" in "average user experience" is 1, so the interaction must be perfect. This is a problem because machine learning systems are not 100% accurate. Below are examples of the two types of returns.

#### Convex Returns

**Marketing:** AI-based marketing products bring new leads. Even if these leads ultimately lead nowhere, the process of pursuing them—at negligible cost—can yield very high returns. For example, some companies can identify website visitors and generate rich profiles from them, used as marketing leads and for finding contact information for these leads. The low cost of such products is offset by the high return of acquiring new customers.

**Sales:** AI-based sales products prioritize leads by importance. These tools increase the likelihood of discovering leads. The low cost of using these products is again offset by the high return of winning new customers.

**Inventory:** AI-assisted inventory management can identify stock levels to enable timely replenishment, thereby retaining customers when they want to buy—for example, running computer vision algorithms on grocery store surveillance video to identify out-of-stock items, allowing staff to quickly restock so customers can buy more.

#### Concave Returns

**Scheduling:** AI-based personal assistants (undoubtedly) save time. However, for the customer, the machine-calculated ROI is somewhat biased (unbalanced). The gains from using an AI personal assistant are hard to calculate because the machine struggles to weigh the user's time and may incur large losses. For example, if a scheduling bot sets up some meetings, it has "completed the task." But the bot might double-book, causing the user to miss a meeting with an important person. This is a costly error, and the user naturally blames the bot.

**Customer Service:** AI-based customer service bots can answer questions quickly at low cost. But for the customer, the machine-calculated ROI is still somewhat biased (unbalanced). Because the bot answers questions in the most efficient way, the holistic experience of using the bot is affected—no surprises, no delight, no relationship building with the customer. However, if the bot merely creates more follow-up questions for humans to answer, or angers customers, the benefits of such products may be very low.

### Choosing What to Build

Founders, product managers, and designers must think carefully about the following question: Can a system with probabilistic assumptions meet customer needs, or is a deterministic system a better choice? The answer depends on how high the accuracy needs to be and how much it costs. Understanding what customers truly want and building a product to satisfy that need is often more difficult than building the technology itself.

### Sufficient Scale

Sufficient data scale is needed to reach the prediction usability threshold. The initial formula in Chapter 1 "Data Learning Effects" shows that output is a function of data:

`Output = Function{Data Network Effect[Data Processing Capability and Quality (Data Volume)]}`

This means output requires data support. Products without enough data to kickstart the data learning effect cannot provide sufficient value to customers, nor encourage them to provide more data to keep the data learning effect running. AI-first companies can acquire sufficient data through: receiving large amounts of customer data, partnering with supplementary data sources, and other strategies mentioned in Chapter 4 "AI-First Teams".

### Not All Data Is Useful to the Loop

Remember, not all data affects model output. Some data is used to build the model and then discarded; some serves as system input; and some is fed back into the system via feedback mechanisms. Of these three types, only the last one continuously contributes to model output.

Training data is discarded after use, so it does not accumulate value. Such data includes three independent datasets: training set, validation set, and designated test set. Training data gives machine learning its predictive properties. Validation data verifies predictions. After the training model has run for a while and been checked against reality, designated test data validates predictions generated by the trained model.

Input data for predictions flows in and out of the system. Validation data from the test set can be used as input data. This input data later trains new models, so it is a potential asset.

Feedback data changes the system, hopefully enabling better predictions. This is the most proprietary data. Design products to continuously collect feedback data; for example, ask customers if the prediction was correct, correct it or accept it. Feedback data is proprietary because it is what the customer observes from the real world after acting on the model's prediction. Competitors will not have these observations.

### The Other Side of Scale

As more data enters the loop, and the data learning effect works effectively, every run of the loop continuously improves. However, as seen in the data learning effect formula (taking logs of both sides), after a certain point, the effect of data decays. Note the last part of this formula: `log(Economies of Scale of Data)`. This means the output growth from having more data depends on the economies of scale of data.

`Percentage Increase in Output from More Data = log(Value of Data Network Effect) + log(Value of Processed Data in Data Network) + log(Economies of Scale of Data)`

When economies of scale of data are very low, it is difficult to have a strong data learning effect because more data makes output increase significantly. If a new entrant can achieve the same level of output without collecting too much data, it is either because the value of the data network effect is exceptionally high, or the value of the data network processing data is exceptionally high. For example, this is the case when the machine learning method used to generate predictions is effective on data, because it doesn't need much data to reach the prediction usability threshold. However, if economies of scale of data are very high, it may require significant capital or time to build a data network effect. This is a challenge, but also an opportunity to build enterprise defensibility.

However, this defensibility may not be sustainable in two cases: algorithmic breakthroughs or diminishing returns to scale. New entrants leverage algorithmic breakthroughs to achieve the same level of output with less data, surpassing incumbents. For example, modern Bayesian machine learning methods can effectively generate accurate predictions on small datasets. Scale effects also have limits: even with initial returns to data scale, after a certain point, returns on data may gradually diminish. More data may not improve the model; the data learning effect may stop. Monitor the ratio of returns to model improvement costs by recording costs spent on model improvement, acquiring more data, or designing more features for the model. Once the product generates diminishing returns, the company solves related problems by collecting data, thereby providing more value to customers.

### Minimizing Drift

When the data used to train the model no longer matches the real world, the model drifts. Models trained on high-persistence data seem to favor new entrants; however, in practice, incumbents with widely deployed systems may have the easiest access to large amounts of new data. The problem AI-first companies face is whether they can obtain enough new data from customers or other sources to minimize model drift.

Compared to others trying to model the same system, managing to hit the target in a highly dynamic system, and then obtaining enough new data to maintain that accuracy, creates a significant advantage. Even if you have an accurate prediction for delivery time, without the latest data on package location, weather, traffic, and other inputs, it is difficult to guarantee prediction accuracy. Without AI, it is almost impossible to master the dynamic changes of these systems to predict accurately; for AI, the learning curve has no end, and the data learning effect is crucial.

### Questions to Think About When Predicting

Good predictions should have the following characteristics: ① The prediction poses little risk when used in the real world; ② It generates convex returns when used; ③ It reaches the prediction usability threshold using available data; ④ It has some returns to data scale.

The following listed situations may help you think.

- How high are the stakes? How much money is lost if the decision is wrong? If something goes wrong, will anyone get hurt?
- How strong is the reinforcement effect on resources? Can it help customers do more with existing labor, or reduce their current headcount? Can it incentivize customers to invest money in this activity?
- Is there data to train the model? Are there sufficient resources to train the learning algorithm?
- How quickly can a new entrant reach a substantial data scale?
- Are data returns diminishing? Is the model continuously improving, or is there a performance ceiling?
- Is there enough new, real data to prevent model drift? If there is no new data yet, is there a pipeline to collect it, or does it require heavy investment in data collection methods (like deploying sensors)?
- Is the underlying system constantly changing so that modeling is impossible without new data? If so, is new proprietary data available?

Domains with low returns to data scale and high drift probability are unreliable. On one hand, new entrants can easily accumulate enough data to surpass existing companies. On the other hand, companies can build moats by acquiring new data faster than competitors and continuing to acquire new data, achieving high returns to data scale and low drift probability. ## Model Measurement

Loops are powered by underlying prediction models, and the performance of these models can be quantified. Some of the main metrics used in machine learning are shown below. There are many other models, each generation with its own measurement standards, but the following are basic metrics applicable to various models.

### Accuracy

Accuracy measures how many predictions are correct out of all predictions. To figure out what is "correct," one needs a reference to the real-world ground truth, and obtaining ground truth data can be highly challenging. Especially when first-hand data must be obtained from customers to assess model accuracy, because they are already dissatisfied with the prediction accuracy. This is the full meaning of accuracy mentioned in this book. Usually, you don't need to be a math expert—just common sense—but you might need to know a little statistics or calculus.

### Binary Classification

Outcomes are often binary, such as true or false. However, accuracy is measured differently. The real world has true and false, while the model world has positives and negatives. The real world only sees certain things as true (it exists/happened) or false (it doesn't exist/didn't happen). For example, based on verified ground truth, like a cat in the frame or no cat in the frame. The model world, however, says some things are judged as positive, or judged as negative.
