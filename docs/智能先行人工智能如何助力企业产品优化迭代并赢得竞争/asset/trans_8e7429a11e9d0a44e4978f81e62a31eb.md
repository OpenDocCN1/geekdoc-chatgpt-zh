In a market, if only one company has accumulated the most resources (such as investment, ecosystem, etc.), then that company is the only one that can invest in producing a variety of products for that market's customers. The company with the largest scale can offer the most variety of products, and after this tipping point, it will dominate the entire market.

Data learning effects have very high data economies of scale because they require large amounts of data to get started. Data learning effects can make all sorts of predictions because models continuously generate different predictions based on feedback data. Furthermore, customers of AI-first products inherently have a high demand for variety because every customer needs a model that can make predictions specific to their business. Therefore, data learning effects tend toward winner-take-all markets.

## Data Learning Effects Make Products More Useful

Data learning effects often work behind the scenes rather than front and center. Some products demonstrate their utility in the foreground; others demonstrate it in the background. Foreground demonstration means the increase in utility is obvious to the end user: they see that adding data generates more accurate predictions for them or triggers new insights immediately. For example, adding their own sales data lets them immediately see their sales performance compared to competitors. Background utility demonstration means the utility increase is not obvious to the user. Users don't see that adding data generates better predictions for them, nor do they see any information they didn't already have.

Through the use of data learning effects, Square Capital became a company that performs well in the foreground. The company issues loans to its point-of-sale (POS) system customers. Merchants, such as restaurant owners, simply add data to their POS system and can almost instantly obtain loans matching their revenue situation. Square Capital can offer loans to them by comparing the data uploaded by merchants with that of other merchants who previously received loans from Square Capital. The user (merchant) sees the immediate benefit of adding data (getting a loan) because the product uses data learning effects to run a loan prediction system that provides a product: a lending rate predicted based on the merchant's ability to repay. Without adding data, you can't get a loan; you qualify for a loan *because* your data can be compared with other data to generate a prediction. This product performs relatively well in the foreground based on data learning effects.

A product that uses data learning effects and performs better in the background is Cloudflare, a product focused on website performance optimization and security management. Customers—such as news sites—can add Cloudflare's data collection mechanism to their networks; this mechanism serves page requests to legitimate visitors while blocking requests from bad actors. Cloudflare provides this protection by distinguishing which requests are detrimental to other customers. "Detrimental" means a request attempts to flood the site with traffic or exploit other security vulnerabilities to shut it down (a Denial of Service, or DoS attack). After adding the Cloudflare data collection mechanism, the customer (site owner) doesn't immediately see an alert rejecting a particularly dangerous request, but the product continuously learns and alerts on potential malicious requests. If you don't allow Cloudflare to see your network requests, you won't get these alerts. This product gets better in the background based on data learning effects.

## Data Learning Effects Compound Faster Than Network Effects

When data learning effects acquire data from customers, they generate a faster compounding effect. The fuel for network effects is data; customers supply fuel to the system, ultimately benefiting themselves.

Products with entry-level data network effects typically acquire data customers, sometimes called a "give-get" model. Every customer provides data that makes the product more useful, attracting more customers. High-quality data makes the product better, and customers can contribute some of that data.

Products with next-level data network effects can acquire data from customers, but this data is typically provided *as* customers use the product, sometimes called feedback data—but, crucially, this data goes into a model that transforms it into predictions, multiplying its value.

For example, customers submit data every time they get a credit score; credit bureaus use this data to provide more accurate scores (then sold to third parties). This is an entry-level data network effect. A next-level data network effect might be a loan application where customers get a loan by allowing ongoing access to their spending habits. Next, the lending company can predict whether or when the loan will be repaid to determine the customer's creditworthiness, and issue larger loans or cut off credit accordingly. The difference between these two data network effects lies in feedback data and prediction models.

## One Flywheel Drives Another

Automating the process of collecting data and generating information is the beginning of the next level, where the shared brain starts comparing what it has learned with other things it has learned. This is the "flywheel" that pushes one network effect into another, more powerful one.

Amazon did exactly this. First, it collected massive amounts of product data, then put that data into product listings while providing comparison tables with structured product information to help customers make better purchasing decisions. More information meant better comparison and decision-making. Subsequently, Amazon invested in a team to build machine learning search and recommendation systems: A9. This team effectively acquired product data and matched it with purchase data to understand which products customers wanted to buy, so Amazon could recommend similar products to these customers on listing pages and in search results. Collecting massive data was the start of the entry-level network effect: for consumers, Amazon used to be one of the most useful shopping sites because it had the most product information. Learning from this data launched the next-level network: for consumers, Amazon is still one of the most useful shopping sites because it offers the best recommendations and best search experience. ## Data Learning Effects Create Cost Advantages

Data learning effects can allow companies to automatically lower product production costs. Cost advantages are achieved by reducing fees or keeping fees constant while providing more value.

The initial cost of building products using data learning effects is amortized across each new customer added. Building intelligent systems is expensive: it requires investment to purchase, collect, clean, and store data. Hiring data scientists and machine learning engineers also costs a lot. Typically, a company must spend a lot of money before customers see the value of predictions. However, once customers start using these products and providing data, costs decrease. They may label or store their own data, reconfigure model functionality through interfaces, and effectively bear the cost of collecting and processing data through feedback systems, lowering production and maintenance costs, thereby creating a cost advantage.

Data learning effects make products more valuable. More data enables customers to make more accurate predictions and better decisions. Improvements in base model performance create value for customers, meaning they get a higher return on their investment. The denominator (price) stays the same while value rises.

## Data Learning Effects and Price Optimization

Data learning effects enable more accurate pricing. Reasonably priced products sell more volume; more customers collect more data, fueling data learning effects. Cost advantage differs from price optimization; better pricing helps propel data learning effects.

Pricing is an information game, won by accurately calculating how much someone will pay for something. Prediction systems are used for pricing experiments to generate test prices. One way to run pricing experiments is to pick prices based on heuristics, guesses, or other factors; another is to use data from previous experiments to predict what prices might be accepted. The second method is more likely to find the optimal price.

E-commerce sites often engage in personalized pricing. A price one shopper sees for a product is often different from what another shopper sees on the same site, or the same product might show a different price on a shopper's phone versus their laptop. This is because AI constantly runs experiments to determine how much a customer might pay for an item based on who they are (whether logged into a profile containing past shopping and demographic data), where they live, what they just clicked, etc. Sophisticated shopping sites use hundreds of data points per person to price products, from customer preference datasets to billions of data points across different demographics. These sites often run promotions that may lose money—selling products below cost—but this ultimately generates useful data about customer willingness to pay.

A manual personalization process should include observation, using observations to determine the next price for an experiment, then running the next experiment, and more observations, and so on. An automated personalization process might use machine learning by feeding data into a system that generates a price to test based on the gradient of the curve it believes leads to the optimal price.

Airlines developed some of the earliest price optimization systems, often called yield management systems, which price each seat based on various variables: number of seats on the plane, cost to fly the plane on a given route, seat location, expected demand at a specific time of year, etc. These systems relied on linear optimization mathematical concepts, which are the basis for some machine learning systems. Today, these systems price seats and change prices frequently, distributing those prices to countless places where tickets can be bought.

There are also examples of predictive pricing for software products sold to businesses. The challenge is that there is usually less data on how much a business customer will pay for a product, not only because they make large purchasing decisions less frequently, but also because their behavior when making these decisions is not as easily observed as consumers browsing shopping sites.

If profits are reinvested into data learning effects, better pricing results, which brings more profit while improving data learning effects.

- Better pricing generates profits, funding ML R&D.
- Better pricing attracts customers, yielding more data (at no cost), creating more profits, increasing R&D investment.
- Better pricing means less sales and marketing spend, creating more profits, increasing R&D investment.

## Limitations of Data Learning Effects

Network effects have limitations; people have questioned the assumption that utility increases as the network scales. Sometimes these limits are external. For example, when a network-covering product achieves monopoly status in the market, governments take regulatory action against the network owner. Sometimes limits are internal. For instance, the technology serving the network product crashes at a certain scale. Sometimes, products generating network effects don't become more useful beyond a certain network size. For example, your 510th friend on Facebook isn't necessarily better than your 50th; once the network grows to a certain size, you stop interacting with them or the product.

Data learning effects face similar limitations. Sometimes these are external, e.g., governments taking regulatory action against data owners to break their market monopoly. Sometimes limits are internal. For example, data becomes too expensive to store and manage. Adding data points increases the cost of data extraction, transformation, preparation, cleaning, loading, and storage, and can even lower the return on data scale. For example, adding more data about each customer's preferences to a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system requires more granular labels to categorize data meaningfully. Adding data can also increase the number of output dimensions required—in other words, what the prediction must display. Sometimes, products generating network effects have no marginal utility as information increases. For example, the 300th review of a coffee shop on Google Maps or Yelp isn't necessarily more useful than the 3rd. The aggregate score might be more reliable, but no one reads the 300th review. More data is not always better.

Data learning effects have limitations in acquiring new data, which increases product manufacturing costs and fails to generate new information. The value of acquiring more data must be considered from both technical and practical perspectives: the additional data must make the model more accurate, and that higher accuracy must be useful to the customer.

## Key Takeaways

- **Accumulating information from automatically synthesized data is a data learning effect.** The steps to build a data learning effect are: ① Capture a critical mass of data; ② Develop the capability to turn data into information; ③ Feed this information into a computer to run the data and learn from new data points.
- **Combined with data processing capabilities and data network effects, data can produce marginal output.** Data learning effects clarify the value chain around data.
- **Data learning effects are unique.** They start with a supply-side competitive advantage, trigger a demand-side competitive advantage, and combine resource access privileges with the ability to transform that resource into a valuable asset.
- **Data network effects differ from ordinary network effects.** The difference between network effects and data networks lies in what is added to the network. With network effects, the connections (edges) between network nodes function and communicate. With data network effects, edges become informative and computable.
- **Data scale effects are more than just data economies of scale.** More data doesn't automatically create data network effects but may lead to scale effects where the cost of processing or acquiring data decreases as scale increases.
- **There are two types of data network effects: entry-level and next-level.** Entry-level sees positive effects from adding data; at the next level, adding data *plus* AI creates positive effects. The first is easier to build, but the second grows faster.
- **Next-level data network effects generate free data.** Entry-level effects require data from outside the network. Next-level effects generate data from inside the network (feedback data), producing data value with a higher compounding effect.
- **Better forms of intelligence come from self-generating data networks.** Entry-level data network effects improve our own intelligence. Next-level data network effects accelerate our learning speed.
- **With data learning effects, the winner can take all.** AI-first products have very high data economies of scale, and customer demand for variety is also high; therefore, these products can dominate an industry after a tipping point.
- **Data learning effects make products cheaper.** Data learning effects achieve cost advantages by lowering the cost of acquiring and processing data while providing more value to customers.
- **Data learning effects make product pricing easier.** Build an independent intelligent system to run price experiments across different customer segments and optimize pricing.
- **Data learning effects have limitations.** They are constrained by surrounding technology scale, data storage costs, lack of marginal utility for customers, and regulatory measures.

Understanding customer needs can be inductive: ask questions, listen, infer what is needed, then build it. Entrepreneurs without much experience in an industry often inductively determine customer needs by asking many questions before starting up. The focus of the inductive process is to get information from a group of potential customers, discover a needs trend, and propose a set of features to meet that need. This usually includes surveys, proposing a design, testing with different potential customer groups, and iterating on the design. This is a centrifugal process: analyze large amounts of data about how people shop (supply) before determining what customers need (demand). Many consumer products are designed this way because the potential customer base is huge.

Figuring out what to offer customers can be deductive. Contemporary entrepreneurs often deduce what to offer by observing the past and adding new products. The focus of the deductive process is to get information from a single customer to judge supply trends and formulate a product feature list. This usually includes calling customers, proposing designs, holding many meetings to refine designs, and gathering feedback after product launch. This is a centripetal process: gradually narrowing product specifications to meet customer needs. Many B2B products are designed this way because the number of potential customers is small, making it possible to talk to all of them.
