## The Automation Revolution and Trust Crisis Brought by Deep Learning

I am very pleased to have the opportunity to share our understanding of the trustworthiness issues in AI. Deep learning has brought an automation revolution to many fields, such as computer vision, natural language processing, speech recognition, etc. Our research work mainly focuses on deep learning and its applications in computer vision.

We can see that with deep learning, many computer vision tasks that we previously found very difficult have now become very easy. Deep learning algorithms are very stable and efficient, meeting the needs of many practical situations to a certain extent. However, in many scenarios with high requirements for AI algorithm safety, a tiny error could lead to a fatal disaster.

Due to the lack of suitable theoretical foundations for existing algorithms, we do not know why these algorithms succeed, when they will fail, or how high the probability of failure is. Coupled with some challenges encountered in application domains, our trust in AI continues to decrease. So, how to solve this problem? This requires us to establish a complete theoretical foundation for AI.

## Progress in Current AI Foundational Theories

First, let's discuss the progress in current AI foundational theories, focusing mainly on the deep learning part. There are two main reasons: first, the foundational theories of traditional machine learning are relatively mature and complete; second, deep learning has dominated the third AI boom and driven many practical applications, but currently, the foundational theories of deep learning are still in their infancy. The success of deep learning is mainly built upon experiments, and it currently lacks a solid theoretical foundation. So, why are we so confident in using deep learning?

### Why Theoretical Analysis of Deep Learning Algorithms is Difficult

Here I quote a statement by Professor Tomaso Poggio from MIT: "We once needed more training data than model parameters to get a meaningful fit." What about deep learning? Let me make a comparison, see the figure below: the left side shows the best-performing neural network in 2017, with tens of millions of parameters; the right side shows a commonly used dataset now. The first is the CORD-19 database, with only 6,000 data points; most datasets only have tens of thousands of data points. ImageNet is considered a relatively large database, but it only has over 10 million data points. So, even for a database like ImageNet, the data volume is very small relative to the parameters of deep learning.

Then, why does deep learning perform so well under such over-parameterization? Some works analyze neural networks from the perspective of model capacity and complexity. We know that if a model's capacity or complexity is small, its generalization ability is guaranteed. This is what traditional statistical learning theory tells us. If we can prove that the capacity or complexity of a deep learning model is not directly related to the number of parameters, then to a certain extent we can clearly explain why deep learning is so successful.

## Analysis Based on Model Capacity and Complexity

### VC Dimension Analysis

First, let's look at VC Dimension. VC Dimension is a common tool for measuring complexity in traditional machine learning. In 2017, Harvey et al. analyzed the generalization performance of neural networks from the perspective of VC Dimension and obtained two upper bounds[^1]. We found that the upper bound of VC Dimension is directly related to the number of model parameters, which makes the upper bound of generalization error very large, even larger than the maximum value of the loss function; such an upper bound loses its meaning. This work shows that the VC Dimension path might be arduous, but we think this work is still very valuable. It tells us that we need to further explore the foundational theories of deep learning.

### Rademacher Complexity

How about Rademacher Complexity? In 2018, Golowich et al. derived a generalization error upper bound from the perspective of Rademacher Complexity. This upper bound is not directly related to the model scale, but it is related to the norm of model parameters and the network depth. This also makes the upper bound of generalization error relatively large[^2].

### Fisher-Rao Norm and Matrix-1 Norm

In 2019, Liang et al. explored the Fisher-Rao Norm. Their work showed that the Fisher-Rao Norm has many good properties, such as invariance under certain reparameterization processes and some special advantages over other complexity measures[^3]. In our work at the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) this year, we also used the Fisher-Rao Norm and Matrix-1 Norm to prove the upper bound of the generalization error of RNN networks[^4]. The Fisher-Rao Norm and Matrix-1 Norm can effectively help us control the scale of the upper bound. The term related to model scale in our upper bound is `the inverse of the minimum eigenvalue of the product of x transpose and x`. This term gives us an inspiration — adding some noise to the training samples can improve the model's generalization ability. At the same time, we cannot add too much noise, because this term would make the training error relatively large. Our Fisher-Rao Norm relies on a structured lemma about gradients, which states that the norm of parameters can be controlled by the magnitude of gradients. This lemma and the Fisher-Rao Norm help us effectively characterize the impact of gradients on the generalization ability of deep learning models, helping us understand the relationship between generalization ability and training ability, and further helping us understand the impact of weight decay and gradient clipping on generalization ability.

### The Paradox of Over-parameterization and Generalization Ability

Generalization error upper bounds based on model capacity and complexity usually indicate that small models have better generalization ability, but deep learning experiments and performance are not entirely consistent. Neyshabur et al.'s work in 2015[^5], and Novak's work in 2018[^6] both used experiments to show that large networks not only have good test performance, but generalization performance also improves as the network grows larger. This point also supports the current trend. Companies are trying every means to increase the depth and scale of networks. Canziani et al. statistics on some competition model scales found that the depth of winning models is increasing year by year[^7], and students who often participate in competitions probably feel the same way.

## Generalization Error Upper Bounds Based on Information Theory

Currently, our work starts from the perspective of information theory and obtains a generalization error upper bound. Our theoretical analysis also supports previous empirical observations. It is mainly based on the following three aspects.

First, unlike traditional shallow learning models — such as Support Vector Machines — deep neural networks possess a hierarchical feature mapping structure. Is this the key to helping us avoid overfitting under high complexity? Our work answers this question affirmatively[^8]. The hierarchical structure is the key to guaranteeing the generalization ability of deep neural networks.

Second, traditional generalization error upper bounds are estimated through the complexity of the model's function space, such as VC Dimension and Rademacher Complexity. This estimation ignores the data distribution itself and considers the worst-case scenario in the model's function space. Therefore, the estimation of the generalization error upper bound becomes relatively loose for deep models with large function spaces. In practice, however, the model's generalization ability is related to the data distribution. Inspired by recent work in information theory and adaptive data analysis, we can use the Mutual Information between the features learned by the neural network and the parameters of the last layer as the generalization error upper bound. Intuitively, the less the network output depends on the input, the stronger its generalization ability.

Third, inspired by the Strong Data Processing Inequality in information theory, as long as the mapping of each layer of the network is irreversible — for example, using ReLU activation functions, convolution, and pooling — the Mutual Information between the features learned by the network and the parameters of the last layer will decrease as the number of layers increases.

Therefore, the deeper the network, the stronger the model's generalization ability. However, as the number of network layers increases, information useful for data fitting may be lost during the mapping process. In such cases, the training dataset fitting error will increase. Therefore, although the generalization ability of deeper networks gradually increases, to ensure the overall network performance is good, it is still necessary to guarantee that the training fitting error is sufficiently small.

## SGD Optimization Process and PAC-Bayes Theory

From the current progress, the path based on model capacity is still very difficult. Because the capacity of neural networks is huge, they even have universal approximation capabilities. Three independent works in 1989 simultaneously proved that the Universal Approximation Theorem holds for infinitely wide multi-layer networks[^9][^10][^11]. Hornik et al. proved in 1993 that infinitely wide single-layer networks also have the Universal Approximation Theorem[^12]. In 2017, Lu et al. proved that finite-width networks also have the Universal Approximation Theorem[^13]. In 1993, Funahashi and Nakamura proved that RNNs can approximate arbitrary sequences[^14]. Lu and Lu recently proved that neural networks can fit arbitrary distributions[^15]. These examples all show that if parameters can be chosen arbitrarily, the capacity of neural networks would be huge, but the question is: can parameters be chosen arbitrarily?

Currently, neural network parameters are basically learned through the Stochastic Gradient Descent algorithm (SGD), so obviously the parameters are not completely random. Therefore, SGD might be the key to solving this problem. The hypothesis space of the neural network itself is large, but SGD only explores a part of the space, which makes the part actually output to the hypothesis space not that large. Recent articles, including our paper at the 2019 Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS), pointed out that the SGD optimization process and the formed path can be represented by Partial Differential Equations (PDEs)[^16]. The SGD optimization process — the parameters at time t+1 minus the parameters at time t, equals the learning rate times the gradient — is a PDE.

Since the "batch" is random and initialization is random, modeling the gradient introduces a lot of noise. That is, the gradient equals the average gradient over the entire dataset plus an uncertain noise, which is usually assumed to be Gaussian distributed. Recently, people have also been exploring what distribution is more suitable for modeling. Therefore, the PDE becomes a Stochastic Partial Differential Equation. So the stochastic PDE of parameters with respect to time t can be represented by an O-U process. We know that the SGD optimization path can be modeled by an O-U process.

Specifically, the stationary distribution of this stochastic process is used to model the model learned by the SGD algorithm, and then we can use PAC-Bayes theory to obtain a generalization error upper bound. Our conclusion also gives a positive correlation between generalization ability and the ratio of learning rate to batch size. This relationship also shows that hyperparameter tuning follows patterns. We trained 1,600 models on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 based on the network structures ResNet-110 and VGG-19 to verify this positive correlation. We collected the test accuracy of all models for analysis, where each small plot is drawn from 20 models. We use test accuracy to represent generalization ability because training accuracy is almost 100%. The left side shows the relationship between test accuracy and batch size under constant learning rate conditions — that is, as batch size increases, test accuracy decreases; the right side shows the relationship between test accuracy and learning rate under constant batch size conditions. We found that as learning rate increases, test accuracy improves. These two plots show the relationship between test accuracy and the ratio of batch size to learning rate, each colored function drawn from 1,000 models. Obviously, as the ratio increases, test accuracy decreases.

Modeling the SGD optimization trajectory using PDEs is very valuable for our understanding of deep learning, and it also requires us to deeply understand the geometric properties of the loss landscape. The geometric properties of the loss landscape determine the trajectory of the stochastic PDE on the loss landscape, i.e., the phase portrait. The curve of neural network parameters changing with time t is the phase portrait.

This is mainly for two reasons. First, the stochastic PDE contains the loss function and its derivatives of various orders, so the geometric structure of the loss landscape directly affects the structure of the stochastic PDE. Second, the loss landscape also determines the boundary conditions of the stochastic PDE; therefore, the loss landscape determines the nature of the solutions output by the SGD algorithm. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the geometric structure of the loss landscape is crucial for our research on deep learning optimization and generalization. In 2018, Novak et al. experimentally showed that the generalization performance of neural networks is related to the geometric structure of the loss landscape[^17]. Specifically, the generalization performance of neural networks is related to the number of linear regions in the input space. Nonlinear activation functions make the loss landscape extremely non-convex and non-smooth. This chaotic situation makes the theoretical analysis of existing optimization algorithms very difficult. For example, why can convex optimization algorithms optimize such non-convex problems as neural networks? But on the other hand, deep learning models have achieved world-renowned results in practical problems. This also hints that beneath the chaotic surface, there may exist some undiscovered order.

## Debates and Discoveries on the Geometric Structure of the Loss Landscape

Regarding the geometric structure of the loss landscape, there is currently a very interesting debate. For linear networks — networks where activation functions are all linear — there are already some conclusions. Kawaguchi's work in 2016[^18] and the work by Lu and Kawaguchi in 2017[^19] pointed out that for linear networks under squared loss, all local minima are equally good, meaning they are all global minima. If similar properties can be found for nonlinear networks, then the excellent performance of neural networks would get a good explanation, at least we would understand some theoretical properties of deep learning to a certain extent. This is because once the SGD algorithm finds a local minimum, it has found a global minimum.

Several works have pointed out that nonlinearity might not be important because nonlinear networks might inherit some important properties of linear networks. But other papers have proven that the reality is not so. In 2019, Yun et al. proved that the loss landscape of a single-layer neural network has infinitely many suboptimal local minima[^20]. This conclusion requires 4 conditions.

First, only one hidden layer; second, squared loss function is used for training; third, the output is one-dimensional; fourth, the activation function is ReLU. Our paper at the International Conference on Learning Representations this year significantly expanded these conditions, covering almost all practical situations[^21]. Specifically, we extended these 4 conditions to: first, arbitrary depth; second, arbitrary differentiable loss functions; third, arbitrary output dimensions; fourth, arbitrary piecewise linear activation functions.

So what might the previously conjectured order be? Soudry and Hoffer pointed out in 2018 that the loss landscape of ReLU networks is divided into several smooth multi-linear regions[^22]. We further proved in the paper that: first, in each region, every local minimum is a global minimum; second, in each region, all local minima converge into a minimum valley; third, all points on the valley constitute an equivalence class; fourth, there exist "parallel valleys" for this equivalence class; fifth, all these equivalence classes and parallel valleys constitute a quotient space. The second property here explains "Mode Connectivity"[^23] — that near the local minima found by SGD, there exist some points with very small differences in empirical risk, and these points connect into a line, which is equivalent to one of these minimum valleys. But unfortunately, currently these geometric properties only hold for single-hidden-layer neural networks. For the analysis of multi-hidden-layer neural networks, we still need further efforts.
