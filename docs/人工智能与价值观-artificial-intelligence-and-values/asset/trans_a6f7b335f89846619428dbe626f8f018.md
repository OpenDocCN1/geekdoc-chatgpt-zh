

## Algorithm Transparency and Fairness: The Case of Facebook

### Facebook's Algorithmic Black Box

In reality, however, even well-intentioned companies that deliberately wish to disclose their algorithms face obstacles due to technical considerations and trade secrets. Take Facebook as an example. In the past, when encountering individuals exhibiting suicidal tendencies in real life, we naturally lend a helping hand, inferring from low moods or abnormal behavior. Yet some people behave normally in daily life but have long accumulated expressions of depression online. When social media platforms become tree holes for people to dump their privacy, social media can judge suicidal tendencies faster and more accurately than humans through artificial intelligence. Facebook's researchers not only analyzed the language, vocabulary, and emojis accompanying suicidal tendencies but even conducted retrospective analyses of the lifetime data of Facebook users who had already committed suicide to predict similar situations. In November 2017, Facebook announced the deployment of this new AI: when a user exhibits suicidal tendencies, the AI alerts staff members, who then conduct "wellness checks" on the user and intervene in their self-harm behaviors. But when asked about algorithmic details, the product's deputy manager, Guy Rosen, remained tight-lipped, saying only that inferences could be made from common times of low mood, concerned comments from friends and family, and text content in user posts, but refusing to discuss the algorithm itself. Simultaneously, Facebook repeatedly emphasized its close cooperation with traditional suicide and self-harm prevention organizations, attempting to speak through offline results.[9](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_9)

In actual operation, Facebook's system may have various problems. For instance, the AI may falsely flag users, who then have no way of knowing or reporting the error to the system; when flagged users receive messages like "You might need extra help right now, please reach out to us," they themselves may not know they have been placed under surveillance, monitored not by machines and platforms, but by living, breathing Facebook employees. Furthermore, this algorithm can be weaponized by malicious actors to harass and discriminate against netizens in poor mental states. Notably, even Facebook's official partner, the "Crisis Text Line" (CTL), did not know Facebook had developed such a system in the backend. Although CTL stated they also used algorithms to predict suicidal tendencies in messages users sent them (e.g., the word "ibuprofen" signaling a spike in suicide risk), Facebook remained opaque even with partners while testing AI on such a massive user population, inevitably raising doubts.[10](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_10)

Facebook's practice of using AI as a shield is not an isolated case. In the spring of 2018, Facebook was deeply mired in the scandal of foreign powers infiltrating its platform to interfere in US elections. CEO Mark Zuckerberg mentioned AI dozens times during congressional hearings, dumping many inexplicable technical and ethical issues onto AI. The 2016 presidential election rapidly intensified long-standing social contradictions in the US, and the rapid growth of social media like Facebook provided a fast lane for external forces. The election's aftermath did not end with the new president's inauguration; Facebook's mismanagement became an enduring hot topic—fake accounts, hate speech, terrorist propaganda, click farms, alleged Russian interference, privacy security... Faced with these issues, Zuckerberg uniformly evaded with AI. Admittedly, AI does possess the ability to search keywords and analyze content, alleviating the chaos of the online information space to some extent, but natural language processing remains insufficient, especially regarding the judgment of "fake news," which involves the value system of the judging subject. Facebook is unwilling to elaborate on its judgment methods. Tech media *The Verge* commented on Zuckerberg's congressional performance: frequently using the term "AI" before a group of older laymen was essentially evading the problem. When asked by Michigan Senator Gary Peters about AI algorithm transparency, Zuckerberg only said "This is a very important issue," Facebook takes it very seriously and has a dedicated team, but revealed absolutely no willingness to make its algorithms public.[11](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_11)

Regarding Facebook, critics argue: although Facebook often discusses government and corporate transparency, what it truly wants to advance is individual transparency, the so-called "radical transparency" or "ultimate transparency."[12](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_12) Facebook uses inductive, paternalistic methods to guide you toward content and people you might like, helping you find political like-minded allies, forming self-satisfying online echo chambers, continuously providing positive feedback to users, and enhancing the excitement of political participation. However, the flaws of this practice were fully exposed in the Cambridge Analytica case—when this data was sold through layers of outsourcing, once it fell into the hands of malicious actors midway, it could not only be used to interfere in US domestic affairs but also pose a universal threat to civil rights. In the Cambridge Analytica case, Facebook platform data was sold, violated, and abused in the process of changing hands, while Facebook remained oblivious and helpless. This shows that when the principle of "openness" is applied to social media of Facebook's scale, besides algorithmic transparency, there needs to be continuous disclosure of data flows and long-term oversight. For a company that profits from data, this is tantamount to negotiating with a tiger for its skin.

### Multi-stakeholder Participation as a Path to Openness and Fairness

Closer to livelihood than reading political news are bank loans and welfare distribution. Today, AI has begun to be widely used by banks and governments to assess personal credit and economic status, yet why some applications are rejected and others accepted often cannot be given a fully convincing answer even by the users of AI, let alone the developers. This is because many AI designs, especially neural networks, operate in an untraceable manner. Professor Anupam Datta of Carnegie Mellon University's Computer Science and Electrical Engineering departments developed a Quantitative Input Influence (QII) system in 2016, attempting to reveal the respective weight of each factor in the final lending decision, thereby observing whether race or gender played a discriminatory role. Datta pointed out that some companies have released algorithmic transparency reports, but these reports disclose limited computational foundations; QII's goal is to form truly transparent reports. The QII method does not merely examine which single variable has the greatest influence but quantifies the combined force of a set of inputs.[13](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_13) For example, when a bank decides on lending, if high-income women and high-income men have extremely similar approval probabilities, it is hard to conclude the bank is suspected of gender discrimination; but if low-income men obtain loans more easily than low-income women, the revealed issue is more complex than simple gender discrimination—it involves gender division of labor among low-income groups, differences in career development potential that may not be as significant in high-income groups.

Pressure to disclose algorithms and open source comes not only from within US society but also from external competition, such as Chinese peers. In the second half of 2018, several major US tech companies launched tools to optimize AI fairness, including Facebook's Fairness Flow, Microsoft Research's data preprocessing and output prediction, Google's "People + AI Research" (PAIR) initiative, IBM's AI OpenScale tools and AI Fairness 360 toolkit. One background for this series of actions is competition from Chinese peers. In January 2018, the China Electronics Standardization Institute released the *Artificial Intelligence Standardization White Paper*, with many industry-related enterprises participating in the writing, showing China's AI moving from savage growth into orderly development.[14](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_14) Compared to administrative constraints, US leading enterprises view open-source sharing and bottom-up industry self-regulation as the best governance means. If enough programmers and developers can jointly witness and supervise the making of algorithms, it can ensure that the models and algorithms developed by major tech companies are relatively transparent.[15](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_15)

High-quality, high-transparency fairness and justice can only be effectively maintained by inviting more subjects to participate, a participation unrestricted by nationality or industry. As AI deepens in the lives of ordinary people, the trust relationship between AI users and the public will be indispensable. Of course, this must be based on a benevolent premise: that when enterprises or states use AI, they will publicly announce it. In this sense, AI algorithms' action on values is a double-edged sword: on one hand, it may expose more and more historical value maladies, providing an opportunity to face and solve them; on the other hand, it may prompt more and more enterprises in more and more scenarios to choose not to disclose the presence of AI tools. If the social effects of AI are shielded as a whole, this brings variables to a value orientation toward good.

## Safety and Accountability: Ethical Challenges in Human-Machine Integration

Gun proliferation is one of the most troubling social issues in the US today, but also the most unique embodiment of its values. When the Second Amendment to the US Constitution was established, its primary consideration was safety: universal gun ownership could both resist foreign invasion and deter the domestic government's intrusion into private space; it was an assertion and praise of private rights, and the Fourth Amendment's prevention of power searching private space follows the same logic. After nearly three centuries of development, the balance between these two considerations (external and internal) has gradually tilted—guns remain a cultural symbol of freedom and safety for ordinary Americans, but privacy has become increasingly less important. The same applies at the state level: US military values externally increasingly emphasize strict control, while the public does not mind their own government's overreach, even enjoying the exchange of data privacy for personal safety.

### Contemporary Continuation of the "Closed World" Cold War Military View

In fact, this imbalance can be traced back to the late 19th century. At that time, the wealth and social status of engineers and the managerial class were rising, and vertical integration prevailed among enterprises. This measure placed the main production processes under a single organizational structure, reducing costs and increasing efficiency through control and scale, seizing shares in emerging urban markets through high-volume, low-margin tactics. This value pursuit of safety and stability incentivized enterprises to further merge upstream and downstream companies to ensure data remained long-term visible, accessible, and controllable. By 1911, when *The Principles of Scientific Management*, advocating quantitative management of labor, was published, author Taylor could explicitly state: "The emerging management doctrine applies not only to enterprises but also to families, farms, churches, charitable organizations, universities, and even government departments."[16](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_16)

The tense international situation of the Cold War created conditions for the application of this value in the military field. Whether it was the 1950s SAGE Semi-Automatic Ground Environment air defense system against Soviet aerial nuclear strikes, or the 1980s Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI, dubbed "Star Wars"), both aimed to achieve seamless human-machine interaction. Technology historian Paul Edwards pointed out that post-war America's prevention-oriented diplomatic view and control-oriented technological view converged, forming the unique "Closed World" concept of the Cold War, pursuing the datafication and computability of all information within the scope of control, integrating computer data processing capabilities into equipment actions and decision-making processes, completely eliminating the uncertainty and irrationality brought by humans in human-machine integration, constructing an absolute, automatic, rational system.[17](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_17) It can be said that after the value concept of safety matured and was successfully applied in the industrial and military fields, its connotation was no longer human resistance to power intrusion; the American public's pursuit of safety increasingly shows individualized, passive, consumerist trends. Correspondingly, the US state's pursuit of safety increasingly approaches the "Closed World" dystopian vision. AI's action on the value of safety unfolds simultaneously on these two levels, and what joins these two seemingly opposite demands is the technical proposition of human-machine integration.

In the civilian sphere, autonomous vehicles are the contemporary continuation of the "Closed World" value, because their purpose is to eliminate the negative impact of human decision-making errors and behavioral fluctuations on output. At this moment, "safety" is no longer a synonym for "no accidents"; its connotation has metamorphosed into: eliminating unstable performance in a statistical sense, which must rely more on machines and less on humans. In 2018, a mistake in ride-hailing company Uber's autonomous vehicle test once made AI safety a focus of media and legal circles. On the night of March 18, 2018, at 10 PM, Elaine Herzberg was walking her bicycle on a highway in Tempe, Arizona, when she was struck and killed by a test Uber autonomous vehicle. Although there was a safety driver in the car, because driving control was completely handed over to the AI, the driver, who was watching a variety show on a mobile device, failed to prevent the tragedy in time. However, this accident did not shake market confidence in autonomous vehicle development, because statistically, autonomous driving technology is undoubtedly safer. Predictions suggest that by 2050, the autonomous vehicle market will grow to $7 trillion, saving 250 million work hours annually, avoiding $234 billion in economic losses caused by human error, preventing 90% of traffic casualties, and saving 1 million lives.[18](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_18)

In the military, drones are a hot field for AI application. This is because drones with built-in visual systems need to process massive amounts of data; if they can be given the ability to learn, judge, and adjust actions on top of existing vision, their combat power can be greatly enhanced and failure rates reduced. According to new media DroneII.com, which tracks drone industry dynamics, machine learning, deep learning, and motion planning are the three AI technologies most closely related to drones. Newly emerged image processors (GPUs) have greatly enhanced drone computing power; although becoming fully autonomous drones still requires long training and feeding massive images and information, deep learning seems to provide a relatively fast shortcut, creating technical conditions for further improvement of drone AI levels. Currently, such technology has been applied to drones detecting rail and metal corrosion, with side measurement accuracy reaching 70%-80%.[19](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_19)

The primary goal of the US Air Force in developing and applying drones is to achieve combat capability endurance under unmanned conditions. The Obama administration's promotion of the drone industry was evident; just three days after taking office, he authorized two drone strikes on Pakistan, killing over 20 innocent civilians, simply because they "looked like terrorists." Obama expressed dissatisfaction with intelligence briefings but did not stop drone operations, showing that the US military's requirements for visual recognition were merely so-so; decisions and final actions still needed to be completed by humans. By 2013, the US Navy could already use demonstrator drones to perform unmanned takeoffs and landings on aircraft carriers, an action that even experienced pilots require long training to accomplish, demonstrating the bright prospects of AI in this field. The US military has also clearly shown its positioning of drone capabilities: fearless against enemy information and electronic warfare interference, achieving combat decision-making using onboard computers in completely communication-denied environments.[20](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_20)

In this sense, the application of AI in the drone field contains a dual safety value: first, drones are light and fast, with low strike costs, avoiding friendly casualties; second, as AI application in drones continuously upgrades, fully autonomous drones requiring no human operation will appear in the future, capable of independently judging and deciding next actions on the battlefield, maintaining combat power, and even achieving safe return. In these two meanings, the latter is closer to the true connotation of "safety" in the future era.

Such technological direction and pace have aroused prudent thinking on related ethical challenges by some: if an AI-carrying drone kills/miskills a person, who is responsible? A non-governmental organization named the "Campaign to Stop Killer Robots" proposes that the act of robots killing must be stopped under the joint efforts of the international community. Since its establishment in 2012, the organization has gained support from an increasing number of world leaders and local US legislators.[21](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_21) In the 1950s, sci-fi writer Asimov proposed the influential Three Laws of Robotics: do not kill, obey orders (unless it harms/kills), seek self-preservation (unless it harms/kills), which seem to share common ground with contemporary AI safety considerations.

To address such calls, the US Department of Defense released the "AI Principles Declaration." Reportedly taking 15 months to form and widely consulting experts inside and outside the industry, it finally proposed five principles: Responsible, Equitable, Traceable, Reliable, Governable. The DoD stated they would ensure operators understand new technologies, ensure data used by AI is transparent, whether data or process design is auditable; AI use in a specific domain is explicit, clear, safe, and effective, requiring repeated testing for confirmation; when AI produces unexpected consequences, humans have the capability to immediately intervene and stop its use.[22](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_22) Apart from "Equitable," all principles essentially point to one question: who is responsible when things go wrong? The implication is that the act of killing itself has no moral flaw; the ethical controversy focus shifts to: exactly what algorithm made that judgment for what reason? This ambiguous stance actually shifts moral criticism away from the behind-the-scenes executors and decision-makers, making the machine and algorithm the new focal point of contradiction.

Additionally, new AI applications in various new fields have created objective conditions for the further development of the "Closed World" safety view, such as AI image recognition technology. Over the past few years, changes in the planet's surface captured by commercial satellites have gradually accumulated to a commercially viable level, even enabling updates of the entire Earth's imagery every 24 hours. Silicon Valley companies processing geospatial big data emerged, such as DigitalGlobe, Spaceknow, and Orbital Insight; they use machine learning tools to search and extract valuable information from massive images. For example, image recognition can make extremely precise calculations of tree counts in millions of hectares of forest; compared to previous sampling estimation methods, AI can pinpoint tree counts to the individual tree. Another example: Facebook purchased large amounts of satellite images from DigitalGlobe and, through its facial recognition technology, located where potential users without internet access were. [23](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_23) Google has also developed a deep learning tool called PlaNet, aiming to give Google Search the ability to locate any input satellite image. [24](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_24) If such technology is applied in the military field, its intelligence value cannot be underestimated. Assuming a satellite imagery data company can conduct all-weather tracking observation of the operation of a country's industrial facilities, these data results could help the US bypass official economic data released by that country's government and independently estimate its economic condition.

In the US, the content contained in the value of safety has been successfully rewritten. Rejecting error-prone human-machine collaboration and comprehensive surveillance, whether in the civilian or military/political spheres, have replaced the protection of private rights as the true pursuit of the safety value. AI, as a tool, increases the possibility of achieving this goal, while the new safety value in turn paves the way for technological development. Since AI has become related to human life and death, discussions on whether AI possesses moral agency are inevitable. The public often likes to use the dilemma of the "Trolley Problem" as a standard to measure whether AI possesses higher morality, as if AI, as a more powerful "intelligence," should make more reasonable choices. Scholars point out that using such extreme cases as a moral guide for AI development is not conducive to regulating and guiding it in real life.[25](#txt003.xhtml#footnote_content_txt003_25) Considering AI's silent transformation of American social life, the exploration of AI's moral agency should also focus on seemingly ordinary yet profoundly meaningful topics and instances in daily life.
