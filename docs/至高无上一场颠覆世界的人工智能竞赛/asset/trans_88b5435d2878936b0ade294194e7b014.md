
Public companies typically have boards of directors representing shareholder interests. Directors meet quarterly to review the company's conduct, ensuring it acts correctly to drive the stock price up rather than down. Suleyman told his partners that to address a transformative technology like artificial intelligence, DeepMind should establish a different kind of board. Its mandate would not be to focus on making money, but to ensure DeepMind developed AI safely and ethically. Hassabis and Legg initially disagreed, but Suleyman was persuasive, and they eventually agreed to the proposal.

Hassabis approached Zuckerberg and told him that a precondition for selling DeepMind was the establishment of an Ethics and Safety Board. This board should possess independent legal status so as to govern all superintelligent AI ultimately developed by DeepMind. Zuckerberg hesitated over this demand. He wanted to grow Facebook's advertising business and "connect the world" through its various social media platforms, but he did not want to run an independent AI company—especially one that came with a grand mission and a stack of ethical protocols. Ultimately, the negotiations collapsed.

Publicly, Hassabis told employees that DeepMind would remain independent for the next 20 years. Privately, however, he had grown weary of fundraising and was frustrated by how little time he had left for actual research. After turning down Zuckerberg's massive offer, he couldn't help wondering how much money he could make by selling the company to a Silicon Valley firm, especially at a time when large tech companies were salivating over AI. Executives from Silicon Valley tech giants, including a billionaire or two, were now regularly calling DeepMind's researchers, attempting to poach them. Many of DeepMind's employees were experts in deep learning, a field that had stagnated for years until recently.

The turning point arrived in 2012. Previously, Stanford AI professor Fei-Fei Li had created an annual academic challenge called ImageNet (a large visual database for visual object recognition software research), which required researchers to submit AI models with visual recognition capabilities—models that could identify images of cats, furniture, cars, and the like. That year, a research group led by scientist Geoffrey Hinton used deep learning to develop a model that was more accurate than any before it; their results stunned the AI world. Suddenly, everyone wanted to hire experts in deep learning AI theory—theory inspired by the brain's pattern recognition abilities.

Legg noted it was a niche field with only a few dozen experts. "We hired a good number of them." Hassabis was paying them around $100,000 a year, but tech giants like Google and Facebook were offering multiples of that. Legg recalled: "Some very famous people were calling our researchers and offering three times the salary." According to a former DeepMind employee, Zuckerberg was one of those very famous people. "We had to sell the company, or we would have been torn apart." Hassabis yearned to be the first to build Artificial General Intelligence (AGI); he could not stand by and watch better-resourced tech companies beat him to it.

Unexpectedly, investor Elon Musk also suddenly proposed acquiring DeepMind. According to insiders, the billionaire wanted to pay with equity in Tesla (the electric car company he had been running for five years). As an investor, Musk never interfered in company affairs, only occasionally touching base with Hassabis. Although the billionaire was increasingly worried about the risks of AI, commercial objectives remained paramount in his mind. He wanted Tesla cars to be the first in the world to successfully deploy self-driving technology, which naturally required more top-tier AI experts. Now, he could build an elite team by acquiring DeepMind.

But DeepMind's founders remained wary. Tesla equity held little appeal. They were also uneasy about someone like Musk taking the reins of AGI. Although the mainstream had begun to acknowledge Musk's foresight, he was notorious in the tech world for his volatility—abruptly firing employees, even ousting Tesla's co-founders.

The founders admired Musk's investment acumen and network just as much as they feared his mercurial temperament. They rejected Musk's offer, too, but failed to realize just how much the thin-skinned Musk disliked being turned down, nor how much this decision would come back to haunt them. Soon enough, however, Hassabis received another email. It came from Google.

## 5 For Utopia, and for Money

The email traveled over 5,000 miles from sunny Mountain View, California, sent by an executive at Google headquarters. Hassabis, in London, opened it to find an invitation to meet with Google CEO Larry Page. In 1998, Page had co-founded Google with Stanford PhD student Sergey Brin. They wanted to improve internet search, so they designed an algorithm called PageRank to categorize web pages by relevance and interconnectivity. Starting from a friend's garage in Menlo Park, California, they eventually built one of the world's largest tech companies.

But by today's standards, Google's money-making method isn't exactly high-tech or innovative, because it has turned into an advertising sales behemoth, much like Facebook. The vast majority of Google's profits and revenue come from tracking users' personal information and serving them ads through Search, YouTube, Gmail, and millions of websites and apps using the Google Display Network.

For someone like Hassabis, who wanted to use AI to change the world, the invitation was unsettling. But he also knew that if he declined, Google might simply poach his staff and build AGI without him. Google had already hired hundreds of engineers to work on AI, so Hassabis felt he could not refuse the meeting request from California.

Meeting Page, Hassabis found a kindred spirit. Before him sat an introverted math graduate with thick, dark eyebrows, wearing a casual shirt and shorts. Throughout Google's founding, Page had harbored a dream of building powerful AI. Hassabis recalled: "He told me he always thought of Google as an AI company, even back in 1998 when they started in the garage."

This was partly personal; Page's father was a professor of AI and computer science who died in 1996. This made him "AI royalty." Page admired Hassabis's seriousness about building AGI and thought the idea wasn't crazy at all. He had also launched an internal project at Google to develop human-like AI, a project that would eventually ignite Hassabis's fierce competitive drive.

Page's project was called "Google Brain," though Hassabis knew little about it at the time. It was proposed by soft-spoken Stanford professor Andrew Ng, who wanted to build more advanced AI systems within Google. In 2011—years before Google approached DeepMind—the professor sent Page a four-page document titled "Neuroscience-Inspired Deep Learning." Professor Ng hoped the Google CEO would approve a project to develop a "general" AI system—precisely what Hassabis was researching in the UK.

It turned out that both Ng and Hassabis employed a similar approach to achieving their goal: using neuroscience as the foundation for building AGI. The Stanford professor told Page in his proposal that he would build "increasingly accurate simulations of the various parts of the mammalian brain."

Even though Ng was a leading figure in AI working at one of the world's most prestigious universities, the idea of building AGI was highly controversial at the time. Ng recalled: "Friends told me it was a bit weird. They said, 'It's not good for your career.'"

In a sense, they were right. Scientifically speaking, Ng and Hassabis's obsession with the human brain had issues. Theoretically, using the human brain as a blueprint for AI makes sense, but slavishly copying biology is not a panacea. Think of the early pioneers who tried to build flying machines by mimicking bird flight. They piloted contraptions with heavy wings and all ended up crashing. Other computer scientists kept hitting walls trying to replicate the brain. In a 2013 TED Talk, neuroscientist Henry Markram claimed he had figured out how to simulate the entire human brain on a supercomputer and would achieve it within 10 years. A decade later, his Human Brain Project had cost over $1 billion and essentially failed.

Over time, AI scientists like Ng and Hassabis would realize that simulating the brain is fraught with difficulty while our understanding of it—from the function of neurons to the operation of brain regions—remains fragmentary. Although we know the human brain has roughly 90 billion neurons constantly firing, we still don't know how this information is processed.

"In retrospect, pursuing biologically realistic fidelity too aggressively was a mistake," Ng said. But on another scientific front, Ng was spot on: scaling up neural networks.

A neural network is a piece of software built by training on vast amounts of data repeatedly. Once trained, it can recognize faces, predict chess moves, or recommend your next Netflix blockbuster. Neural networks, also called "models," typically consist of many different layers and nodes that process information in a way loosely similar to the brain's neurons. The more a model is trained, the better its nodes become at predicting or identifying things.

Ng discovered that the more nodes, layers, and training data used, the more capable these models became. Years later, OpenAI would make a similar discovery: that "scaling" these key ingredients was critical. During his experiments at Stanford, Ng noticed that the larger the deep learning model, the better it performed. These results thrilled him, prompting him to send that four-page memo to Page, suggesting he might achieve "large-scale brain simulation" and progress toward "human-level AI."

Page loved the idea. He not only agreed but put Ng in charge of Google's most cutting-edge AI research project to date. But years passed, and "Google Brain" didn't seem to be on track to build AGI. Instead, it helped Google improve its targeted advertising business (making ads more relevant by better predicting what users would click), thereby boosting company revenue. Ng admitted this wasn't what he had envisioned when he sent the proposal to Page. He said: "It was a bit disappointing compared to the work I had done."

What Ng truly wanted to achieve scientifically was to free humanity from mental drudgery, just as the Industrial Revolution freed us from sustained physical labor. He believed more powerful AI systems could do the same for professionals, "so we can all go pursue more exciting, higher-level goals."

Ng's modus operandi differed from Hassabis's. The British entrepreneur wanted to stay as far away from the advertising behemoth as possible; Professor Ng was happy working at Google. In that sense, Ng did Hassabis a huge favor. From the deck of the Google mothership, Ng's research had already veered onto a track of boosting the company's ad performance, meaning DeepMind didn't have to contribute immediately.

By late 2013, when Google first approached DeepMind about an acquisition, Ng was already leading a team building complex AI models to enhance Google's ad tools—diverging from his lofty goal of building omnipotent AI to liberate humanity from mental drudgery. Now, Page flew to London to negotiate the acquisition of DeepMind, knowing he could spend Google's money on more forward-looking research projects.

According to *Genius Makers* by New York Times writer Cade Metz, DeepMind's founders hosted the Google billionaire in their London office and briefed him on the company's current research. Hassabis described how his team had developed a new technique called reinforcement learning and trained an AI system to master the Atari classic *Breakout*. In the game, you slide a paddle back and forth to bounce a ball at a wall of bricks. Within two hours, the system learned to hit the ball to the right spot, consistently knocking out a few bricks to carve a tunnel through a narrow gap behind the top row. Page was amazed.

As a technology, reinforcement learning isn't so different from rewarding a dog with food for obeying commands. In training AI, you can use a similar reward model—numerical signals like "+1" to indicate a good outcome. Through endless trial and error and repeated gameplay, the system learned what worked and what didn't. Highly complex computer code wrapped an elegantly simple idea.

Next, Legg outlined the future direction: applying these techniques to the real world. Just as they trained models to master video games, using the same methods, they could teach robots to navigate homes or develop an autonomous agent fluent in English. This was where DeepMind's discoveries—and AGI itself—would ultimately have the most profound impact. Page and his team were convinced.

Page knew Hassabis and his co-founders had rejected Facebook's massive offer, so he led the deal negotiations personally. He quickly learned the details. Hassabis set two conditions for the sale. First, he and his co-founders wanted Google to promise never to use DeepMind's technology for military purposes—whether controlling autonomous drones or weapons, or supporting soldiers on the battlefield. They considered these moral red lines Google must never cross.

Second, they wanted Google's leadership to sign an ethics and safety agreement. Drafted by London lawyers, this agreement would place all future AI technology developed by DeepMind under the control of an Ethics Board, co-chaired by Hassabis and co-founder Suleyman. They hadn't finalized who else should sit on the board, but they wanted it to have full legal standing to govern the powerful AI they would eventually build.

"If we succeed, we need to treat AGI with caution," Hassabis said of the board he and his co-founders wanted to establish. "Because this is a general-purpose technology, perhaps one of the most powerful technologies ever, we want to align with people who take it just as seriously."

Sure enough, it took months of tough negotiations before Google agreed to the two conditions that had sunk the Facebook deal. Acquiring DeepMind meant Page would own the first company building AGI. He knew that if an ethics board had legal control over the technology, Google would struggle to profit from it, but ultimately his idealistic vision won out. They would find a way to solve that problem. Page agreed to DeepMind's demands, incorporating the establishment of the ethics board into the acquisition process.

AGI needed to be handled with care not just because a large corporation might dictate its future, but because it attracted several nascent ideologies that could steer the technology in different directions. Hassabis had already learned this from investors like Peter Thiel and Jaan Tallinn—Thiel wanted AI to develop faster, while Tallinn feared this young British entrepreneur might trigger a catastrophe.

For those with strong convictions about AI's use, its incredible potential added an almost religious dimension. In the coming years, these ideologies would battle innovators and monopolies for control of AGI, becoming an unpredictable risk for the technology. For instance, they would oust Sam Altman from OpenAI, absurdly push AI commercialization, paint a doomsday portrait of AI's power just to attract more corporate attention to the software. In a world chasing commercial success and profit, a growing number of AI creators found themselves surrounded by competing dogmas—whether to build AI as fast as possible to achieve utopia, or to stoke fears that it might bring about the end of the world.

People who know Hassabis say that as a strategic thinker who likes to hedge his bets, he largely stayed above these fray, partly due to his own unique goal: using AGI to make major, even sacred, discoveries. Suleiman was more concerned that AI would inevitably cause societal problems. A former colleague of Shane Legg revealed that among the three co-founders, Legg was the most sympathetic to the extreme ideologies pursuing AGI, one of which had been brewing for decades. It is the ever-controversial transhumanism, which helps explain why AI creators sometimes ignore the technology's downsides.

The basic premise of transhumanism is that humans are currently second-rate. With the right scientific discoveries and technological support, perhaps we will one day transcend our physical and mental limits, evolving into a new, more intelligent species. We would be smarter, more creative, and live longer. We might even succeed in merging the human brain with computers and exploring the galaxy.

Its core ideas trace back to the 1940s and 60s, when evolutionary biologist Julian Huxley joined and became president of the British Eugenics Society. The eugenics movement advocated improving humans through selective breeding, and it flourished in British universities, intellectual circles, and the upper classes. Huxley himself came from an aristocratic family (his brother Aldous wrote *Brave New World*) and believed the upper classes had superior genes. The lower classes needed to be weeded out like failed crops and forcibly sterilized. "They breed too fast," Huxley wrote.

After the Nazis co-opted the eugenics movement, Huxley decided to rebrand it. In an essay, he coined the new term "transhumanism," arguing that beyond sensible breeding, humans could "transcend themselves" through science and technology. By the 80s and 90s, the movement peaked because the advancing field of AI offered a tantalizing new possibility: perhaps scientists could merge the human brain with intelligent machines, thereby enhancing the human mind.

This proposition was concretized in the concept of the "Singularity." The Singularity is a future moment when AI and technology become so advanced that humanity undergoes a sudden, irreversible transformation, optimizing itself by fusing with technology and machines. Legg, who read related books in his youth, and DeepMind's wealthy investor Peter Thiel were both captivated by this proposition. Tech experts craved this utopia so desperately that Altman, Thiel, and others have signed agreements with various companies to have their brains or entire bodies cryogenically preserved, just in case they don't live to see "mind-machine merger." "I don't expect it to necessarily work," Thiel said on journalist Bari Weiss's podcast, "but I think we should try to do it."

The problem with some of these views is that over time, adherents become increasingly fanatical. For example, certain so-called AI accelerationists believe scientists have a moral obligation to build AGI as fast as possible to create a post-human paradise—a techno-rapture. As long as they succeed in their lifetime, they can live forever. But accelerating AI development might also mean cutting corners, risking harm to certain groups or losing control.

Consequently, others take the opposite stance, viewing AI as a future demon that must be stopped. Bearded libertarian Eliezer Yudkowsky, sipping coffee, sparked Jaan Tallinn's precautionary instincts; he is the leading figure of this intellectual movement, constantly fueling it through his website LessWrong. By the time Google acquired DeepMind in 2014, several hundred people, including AI researchers, had gathered on the site to philosophically debate how to prevent a future superintelligence from causing destruction. LessWrong became the internet's most influential AI doom hub, with some reports noting it bore all the hallmarks of a modern doomsday cult. If a member suggested a future AI might destroy humanity in a different way, Yudkowsky would publicly denounce them and kick them out.

Over time, these so-called AI doomers gained enough support among wealthy technologists to fund companies and steer government policy in their favor. Yudkowsky's site grew so influential that many devoted readers eventually joined OpenAI.

But perhaps the most unsettling ideologies emerging in the AGI field are those focused on digitally creating a near-perfect human species. Bostrom's *Superintelligence* partly fueled this view. The book had a contradictory impact on the AI field. It made the public fear AI's potential destruction more with the "paperclip maximizer" example, but it also prophesied that creating the right kind of powerful AI could usher in a bright utopia. Bostrom argued one of this utopia's most alluring features would be "post-humans," possessing "capabilities far exceeding those of current humans," existing in a digital world. In this digital utopia, humans could experience environments defying physical laws—like death without assistance or exploring fantasy worlds. They could choose to relive cherished memories, create new adventures, even experience different forms of consciousness. Interactions with others would become more profound, as these new humans could directly share thoughts and feelings, forging deeper connections.

These ideas are intensely seductive to parts of Silicon Valley, who believe the right algorithms can realize such wondrous lifestyles. By painting a future that could be either heaven or hell, Bostrom triggered a pervasive belief that ultimately drove AI creators like Sam Altman in Silicon Valley to race ahead of Demis Hassabis in London to build AGI. That pervasive belief is: they must build AGI first because only they can do it safely. Otherwise, someone else might build an AGI that violates human values, which would not only wipe out the billions living on Earth but also destroy the trillions of perfect future digital humans—costing us all the chance to live in paradise. Meanwhile, Bostrom's ideas have dangerous consequences because they divert industry attention away from how AI will harm humans *now*.

These contemporary tech ideologies, coupled with Google's acquisition of DeepMind, illustrate a hardening, brutal truth: for tech companies, finding a responsible "steward" for AI is becoming increasingly difficult. Driven by the dual forces of near-religious zealotry on one side and an insatiable hunger for commercial growth on the other, divergent goals are heading toward a head-on collision.
