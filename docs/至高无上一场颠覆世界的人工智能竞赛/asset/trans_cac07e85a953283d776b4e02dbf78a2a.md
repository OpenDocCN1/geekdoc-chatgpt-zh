
# Act One: Origins

Brockman later recalled on Lex Fridman's podcast: "We also knew our resources would pale in comparison to Big Tech." But if they really established such an organization, how could they choose an organizational structure to ensure the AI developed would benefit humanity? "Obviously, only a nonprofit lacks the competing incentives that could undermine its mission."

On the ride home in Altman's car, Brockman said that although it felt surreal, he agreed to join. After all, in Silicon Valley, even the most inconceivable ideas can find a way to grow and thrive.

Brockman immediately began preparing the necessary steps to found OpenAI, leaving a deep impression on Altman, a self-described workaholic. This was a man who replied to emails every five minutes on average; presumably, he would devote his entire being to the cause just like Altman. Altman later said: "He was all in." On the matter of founding OpenAI, Brockman became the point person.

Next, Brockman was responsible for poaching a batch of tech talent from Google, Facebook, and other companies, and made contact with Yoshua Bengio, a professor at the University of Montreal and one of the "godfathers" of the deep learning movement. Brockman didn't expect to hire Bengio; he wanted the professor to tell him who the most promising scientists in AI were. Bengio printed a list and sent it to Brockman.

Hiring these people wouldn't be easy. Some were earning seven-figure salaries at Google, Facebook, and the like, while Altman and Brockman couldn't offer comparable packages. All they had was an intense mission to change the world and two famous figures operating behind the scenes. Elon Musk was now a globally revered business titan, and leading Y Combinator had elevated Altman's status in Silicon Valley to someone everyone wanted to know. For AI research experts, even working briefly at this newly established nonprofit could yield extensive networks and potential career opportunities, making a pay cut worth it.

Several top scientists on Brockman's list decided to interview with him for the job. Besides the celebrity effect and the future vision, they valued the new organization's promise of "openness." According to former OpenAI employees, they would have the opportunity to publish their final research results, rather than secretly developing corporate products; some also believed that Google and DeepMind were striving to develop AGI for profit, and OpenAI could become a force to counterbalance that profit motive.

To seal the deal, Brockman took several scientists to a winery to negotiate. If Sutskever agreed to join, that would be the biggest coup. They discussed many things about building an AI lab: that the lab must be completely free from corporate constraints, that it must "open source" research results—making them freely available to the public—and how this would prevent large tech companies like Google and Facebook from exerting control as AI grew more powerful. Almost all the scientists agreed to join OpenAI, including that seemingly unsmiling genius scientist, Sutskever. He grew up in Russia and Israel, had worked with famous deep learning pioneer Geoffrey Hinton, and now he would leave "Google Brain" for OpenAI.

In December 2015, a team of a dozen scientists traveled to Montreal, Canada, for the annual AI conference "Neural Information Processing Systems" (NIPS, now NeurIPS), announcing the establishment of the new research lab. Amidst heavy snow falling outside the venue, team members introduced their new lab to other attendees. The real launch, however, happened online—OpenAI.com appeared out of nowhere, and Brockman and Sutskever jointly published an article on the website introducing the lab's project. They wrote: "Our goal is to advance digital intelligence in the way that is most likely to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to generate financial return."

The lab secured a staggering $1 billion in investment from Musk, Thiel, Altman, Hoffman, Jessica Livingston, and Amazon's cloud computing division, with Musk and Altman serving as co-chairs. Insiders revealed that Musk planned to fund OpenAI with Tesla stock, just as he had proposed for his acquisition of DeepMind years earlier. Hearing this news, hundreds of attendees at the NIPS conference were stunned. Many thought developing AGI was a pipe dream, but some were green with envy. Over the past 10 years, big tech companies had been poaching top computational science talent from universities, causing the best AI experts today to serve corporate interests. In reality, a pipeline had formed from elite universities straight to Google, Facebook, or Amazon. This was a chronic issue that had existed for years.

"No one can turn down a salary that doubles or triples. I couldn't, and neither could my colleagues," said Maja Pantic, a computer science professor at Imperial College London. She joined Samsung Electronics in 2018 as Research Director of their AI Center, later moving to Meta (formerly Facebook). Those outstanding scientists couldn't refuse either: Hinton now works for Google; Fei-Fei Li left Stanford for Google; Yann LeCun works for Facebook; Andrew Ng left Stanford for Google, then for China's Baidu. Even top schools like Stanford, Oxford, and MIT struggle to retain their star scholars, resulting in a hollowing out of the educational workforce. AI research has become increasingly secretive and more profit-oriented. This is why Musk and Altman's push for open research was so refreshing to researchers. Finally, someone was going to solve the problem of big companies monopolizing AI knowledge.

There are two reasons for the university brain drain. The first and most obvious is compensation. At the University of Toronto, where "AI Godfather" Geoffrey Hinton once worked, computer science professors earn about $100,000 a year. The university's highest earners make about $550,000 annually—that's the "ceiling." Hinton's star student, Sutskever, never even considered academia. After working at Hinton's startup for a while, he joined "Google Brain" directly. According to *The Deep Learning Revolution*, OpenAI offered Sutskever $2 million a year, but "Google Brain" offered three times that.

The second reason is that AI research experiments require data and computing power. Universities typically have a limited number of GPUs (Graphics Processing Units). GPUs are powerful semiconductors made by Nvidia; most servers used to train AI models today require GPUs. While in academia, Pantic managed to purchase 16 GPUs for her 30-person research team. But with so few chips, training an AI model took months. "It was absurd," she said. But shortly after joining Samsung, she had access to 2,000 GPUs. This increased processing power meant training an algorithm took only days, and research could accelerate.

For scientists who remained in academia, escaping the influence of big tech became increasingly difficult. A 2022 study showed that over the past 10 years, the number of academic papers affiliated with big tech companies had more than doubled, accounting for 66%. The study was conducted by researchers from multiple universities, including Stanford and University College Dublin. It showed that big tech's influence is growing "using the same strategies as Big Tobacco." This in turn affects how universities measure the success of AI research. Abeba Birhane, who led the study and is now a senior fellow at the Mozilla Foundation, said scholars no longer focus on values like human welfare, justice, and inclusivity, but tend to pursue better performance.

Birhane said welfare and inclusivity aren't just empty concepts. They are measurable. "They may be abstract, but so are efficiency and performance," she added. "People have found ways to measure fairness, privacy, and so on." Birhane pointed out in another collaborative study in 2023 that what's worse is that researchers, whether in universities or tech companies, are too focused on making their AI bigger and more capable, increasing the risk of these models producing racist or sexist results. "We found that as dataset sizes grow, hate content increases as well."

However, scale is critical to big tech's influence in AI. Google and Meta possess trillions of data points for training models, while managing massive computer clusters spanning hundreds of thousands of square feet ^([⁵](#fn5)).

To improve AI, the more data the better. The research Sutskever and his team initiated at OpenAI focused more on AI model capabilities than on fairness, privacy, etc. Simply put, this approach had a playbook. If you train AI models with ever more data, increase model parameters, and boost computing power for training, the models become more proficient. This is the striking correlation Professor Andrew Ng noticed during experiments at Stanford. It doesn't matter what the model is designed to do. Just turn all the knobs up, and it translates languages more accurately or generates text more humanly.

Sutskever once said at an AI conference: "If you have a very large dataset and a very large neural network, success is guaranteed." That second half became his signature quote among AI scientists, especially after OpenAI held a grand launch event. This new nonprofit, led by a brilliant scientist and several Silicon Valley heavyweights, ignited fresh enthusiasm across the field for its philosophy.

But before long, problems emerged. OpenAI didn't immediately receive the $1 billion funding commitment announced by Musk, Thiel, and others in December 2015. Tech news site TechCrunch scrutinized OpenAI's federal tax filings and wrote an investigative report. The report showed that over the next few years, the nonprofit actually raised only about $130 million in donations.

OpenAI not only lacked funds but also lacked a clear direction. The founding team of 30 researchers initially worked out of Brockman's apartment in San Francisco's Mission District—some at the dining table, some sunk into the sofa with laptops on their knees. A few months later, Dario Amodei, a senior researcher from "Google Brain," came for a visit. He asked pointed questions. For example, what exactly did OpenAI's commitment to developing friendly AI and opening its source code entail? Altman countered in a *New Yorker* column that they didn't intend to open all source code.

"But what is it for?" Amodei pressed.

"I'm not sure either," Brockman admitted. Their goal was to ensure AGI progressed smoothly.

Scientists harboring doomsday fears like Musk and Eliezer Yudkowsky were growing in number, and Amodei was one of them. About a year earlier, he was still at Google, which faced backlash because the visual recognition system in its smart photo management software classified Black people as gorillas. Google said it was "appalled" and completely removed the "gorilla" label from the software. "A system with such unpredictable failure modes is not a good thing," he said on a podcast discussing the incident.

But Amodei's concerns weren't limited to algorithmic racism and offensive decisions. He also worried about the AI technology DeepMind possessed—reinforcement learning. This technology was being used to control physical systems like robots, self-driving cars, and Google's data centers. He told the Future of Life Institute, led by Jaan Tallinn, in a 2016 interview: "Once you really interact directly with the world, and directly control physical objects, I think the probability of things going wrong... starts to increase." Amodei's research into AI harm showed him increasing possibilities for catastrophe. He later warned the media in 2023 that runaway AI had a 25% chance of causing human extinction. Staying at "Google Brain" couldn't solve these risks. So, a few months after that deep conversation in the OpenAI office, he joined the organization.

To develop AGI, OpenAI's founding team needed to attract more money and talent, so they tried to focus on projects that would generate positive media coverage. They early on created a computer that defeated top human players in the 3D strategy video game *Dota 2* (referred to as "Ancient Guardian" in text), and built a five-fingered robotic hand driven by a neural network that could solve a Rubik's Cube. These projects attempted to surpass the secret research happening in DeepMind's offices across the Atlantic, to keep Elon Musk satisfied.

Musk never hid his distrust of DeepMind. Initially, Musk visited OpenAI's office weekly; later, every few weeks. In 2017, OpenAI staff went to SpaceX headquarters for a meeting. Musk gave them a tour and held a fireside chat with about 40 newly hired AI researchers. At one point, Musk began talking about why he funded OpenAI: it was because of Demis Hassabis.

An attendee revealed Musk said: "I was an investor in DeepMind, and I was very worried that Larry [Page] was treating Demis as his subordinate. Actually, Demis only works for himself. I don't trust Demis either."

The researchers were shocked. To most, it sounded less like Musk was particularly worried about AI development and more like he had a personal vendetta against Hassabis. When asked why he was hostile toward Hassabis, Musk mentioned the British entrepreneur's past computer games designed around world domination.

During the chat, Musk recounted a conversation with another DeepMind investor. The investor said of an earlier meeting with Hassabis: "I felt like a moment in a movie where someone should step up and shoot the guy." In other words, someone needed to stop Hassabis from building omnipotent AGI.

Despite disliking Hassabis, Musk still reminded OpenAI staff that DeepMind was in the lead and they needed to match the British company's research output. Former OpenAI employees revealed that months flew by, and Musk grew increasingly worried that OpenAI's technology wasn't as powerful as DeepMind's.

To keep their biggest patron, Altman and Brockman led some researchers to mimic DeepMind's work. For instance, the *Dota 2* project researchers couldn't understand why they were studying game simulators if the ultimate goal was building AGI to improve human lives. The reason, naturally, was they needed Musk's money. Brockman told researchers: "If we don't work hard on this, OpenAI might not exist in a few years."

Although OpenAI eventually won global acclaim for its achievements in chatbots and large language models, in its first few years it toiled in multi-agent simulation and reinforcement learning—areas where DeepMind already dominated. However, the more they chased DeepMind in these areas, the more Altman and his leadership team realized these methods weren't very useful in reality. It was at this point that OpenAI began evolving into a completely different type of organization than DeepMind. DeepMind valued PhDs and championed a rigid academic culture. OpenAI's culture was engineering-oriented; its researchers were mostly programmers, hackers, and Y Combinator founders, often more interested in building and making money than in making discoveries and gaining scientific prestige.

Meanwhile, Musk grew restless. He complained to Altman that he had hired a bunch of great scientists but achieved no results surpassing DeepMind. By the nonprofit's third year, Musk told Altman it had fallen far behind Google and DeepMind. He then proposed a quick fix: he would take over OpenAI and merge it with Tesla. In a December 2018 email to Altman and his team, Musk said OpenAI would never catch DeepMind without major changes. This email was later released by OpenAI and verified by those who saw the original. "How unfortunate that humanity's future is in Demis's hands," Musk added. In other words, if Musk didn't take over OpenAI, the villain Hassabis would win. But Altman and his partners wanted to keep control. They refused Musk's proposal.

In February 2018, OpenAI briefly mentioned Musk's impending departure in a public statement about new donors, giving him a noble reason. Musk was leaving due to conflicts of interest in AI. "Elon Musk will depart the OpenAI Board but will continue to donate and advise," the nonprofit wrote on its blog. "As Tesla becomes more focused on AI, this will eliminate potential future conflicts for Elon."

Many OpenAI employees knew this was nonsense. They believed that while Musk claimed to care about creating safe AI, he also wanted to build the most capable AI. He was already fabulously wealthy and wielded unprecedented influence over US infrastructure: NASA was sending astronauts to space via SpaceX, Tesla dominated EV charging standards, and Musk's satellite internet company Starlink was trying to influence the outcome of the Russia-Ukraine war.

Clearly, Musk had a track record of unreliability. He had promised to donate $1 billion to OpenAI over a few years but actually gave only $50–100 million—a drop in the bucket for the world's richest AI worrier. To a large extent, if he had funded OpenAI with Tesla stock, fulfilling that pledge would have been relatively easy. From 2015–2023, Tesla's stock price rose over 180x, meaning OpenAI could have easily hit the $1 billion fundraising target. Despite Musk's professed concern for humanity's future, he seemed more concerned with staying ahead in the competition.

Musk's departure cut off OpenAI's primary funding source. This was a disaster for Altman. He had staked his entire reputation on this project. To work with him, some of the world's top AI scientists had voluntarily taken pay cuts, and now his grand ambition to help humanity began to look foolish. In the new era of AI development, a simple fact remained: from paying researchers' salaries to training data to the powerful computers running models, everything required massive resource stacking. Without Musk, the chances of meeting these needs were rapidly dwindling.

Altman faced a critical moment. In OpenAI's San Francisco office, he pondered how to keep the nonprofit running and develop AI models that might not be industry-leading with extremely limited resources. Or should he just pull the plug? Raising money for a nonprofit is much harder than for a startup. Altman had been trying to persuade wealthy people to donate to the AGI cause out of goodwill, but saw no prospect of success. He needed tens of millions; Musk was his last big patron.

But he had another option. Beyond the glory of lighting the beacon of an AI utopia for humanity, maybe OpenAI could offer patrons some direct financial returns. That would be a win-win. Patrons wouldn't be "donating" but "investing"—a term Altman preferred. But he believed only a few potential patrons in reality could provide the funds and computing power needed for AGI: tech giants like Google, Amazon, Facebook, Microsoft. No other companies had billions in cash or massive computer clusters the size of several football fields.

Over the past few years, OpenAI and DeepMind had tried to erect barriers to prevent the super-powerful AI systems they built from being misused. DeepMind tried to change its governance structure so profit-driven monopolies like Google couldn't arbitrarily use AGI to make money. Instead, an expert advisory board would control things. Altman and Musk positioned OpenAI as a nonprofit, promising to share research and even patents with other institutions when they neared the threshold of superintelligence. This way, it would put humanity's interests first.

Now, a struggling Altman planned to tear down part of the guardrails. His attitude would shift from initial caution to recklessness, and doing so would change the AI field they had both fought for—from a slow-paced, primarily academic sanctuary into something more like the Wild West. Altman would use his storytelling ability to craft a convincing narrative justifying his imminent departure from OpenAI's founding principles. He's a tech entrepreneur; tech entrepreneurs sometimes must pivot. That's how Silicon Valley works. He just needed to slightly adjust OpenAI's founding principles.

# Act Two: The Leviathan

## 7 Entry

Tourists clustered at London's King's Cross Station, vying to see the magical platform where Harry Potter catches the train to Hogwarts; just steps away, skyscrapers soared, their glass and metal cladding facades glittering, as a different kind of magic unfolded inside. Bustling pedestrians traversed the beautiful walkways between buildings, including DeepMind's engineers and AI scientists. Reaching the glass doors of an office building, they pulled out access badges. Ostensibly, the building belonged to Google, but two floors were dedicated exclusively to the mysterious AI lab.

As part of Google, DeepMind enjoyed many perks—nap pods, massage rooms, indoor gyms—but its founders still strove to escape parent company Alphabet's control. More than two years after the acquisition, the tech giant's executives dangled a new prospect before Demis Hassabis, Mustafa Suleyman, and Shane Legg. DeepMind wouldn't become an "autonomous unit," but rather an "Alphabet company" with its own independent P&L statement.
