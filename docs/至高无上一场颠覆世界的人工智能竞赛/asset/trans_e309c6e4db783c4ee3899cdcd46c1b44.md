
Under a 100-point scoring system, tech companies averaged 37 points, performing worst in governing how people use AI tools. "In reality, the downstream impact of foundation models is not transparent," Stanford researchers stated. "No developers provide accounts of affected market sectors, individuals, or regions, nor any form of usage reporting."

Meanwhile, public sector organizations responsible for scrutinizing AI companies have been chronically underfunded. Aside from the uncertain future of the EU AI Act, there are virtually no regulations compelling leading companies to increase transparency. Tech companies can deploy unpredictable AI tools globally whenever they deem it necessary.

OpenAI and DeepMind focused on building the perfect AI. To ensure their systems wouldn't cause harm like social media companies, they chose secrecy while rejecting research audits. While the idea of building AGI is alluring, it may also bring broader risks. Focusing on developing task-specific AI might be a safer approach, but it's less exciting and fails to inspire the near-religious devotion to utopian fantasies that attracts such massive investment.

Both Altman and Hassabis strove to stay ahead in the AI race while resisting the lure of Big Tech, insisting on altruistic goals. They needed massive compute, vast data, and the world's most talented (and expensive) AI scientists. Now, as the two men square off representing Microsoft and Google respectively, they have also redefined the goal of AGI—shifting from the pursuit of utopia and scientific discovery to the pursuit of prestige and wealth.

The long-term consequences of this situation are hard to predict. Some economists say powerful AI systems could exacerbate inequality rather than create wealth for humanity. They could also widen the cognitive gap between rich and poor. Technologists generally believe that when AGI arrives, it won't exist as an independent intelligent entity but will connect to our brains via neural interfaces. Elon Musk's brain-computer interface company, Neuralink, leads this research; he hopes to one day implant brain chips in billions of people. He is also in a rush to achieve this.

"We need to accomplish this before AI takes over the world." According to Musk's biographer Ashlee Vance, Musk told engineers in 2023, "We need to go all in on this with extreme urgency." Musk believes that by implanting chips in brains, humans can avoid being destroyed by future superintelligence; thus, he hopes Neuralink will perform surgeries on over 22,000 people by 2030.

But compared to runaway AI, bias is a more pressing issue. As more machine-generated content floods the internet, we don't know how racial and gender stereotypes will evolve. Harvard professor of government and technology Latanya Sweeney estimates that within a few years, 90% of text and images online will no longer be created by humans. Most of what we see will be AI-generated. Now, to make ad revenue, thousands of articles are published daily using language models—even Google struggles to discern truth from fiction. When you search Google for famous historical painters or certain celebrities, AI-generated images have already infiltrated the top search results. The more AI-generated content proliferates online, the greater the risk of bias.

"We are creating a loop: writing code, then amplifying stereotypes," says Abeba Birhane, an AI scholar who researches how Big Tech manipulates academic research and its similarities to tobacco giants. "This will be a major problem because there are more and more AI-generated images and text on the internet."

Overall human well-being may also be affected. Twenty years ago, people worried cell phones caused cancer. Instead, they became addictive, causing us to spend hours staring at small screens daily instead of interacting with the world around us. Chatbots are likely to exacerbate this addiction. In November 2023, average users spent about two hours a day on Character.ai, developed by Noam Shazeer, chatting with avatars of celebrities like LeBron James or fictional characters like Mario, and role-playing. According to several market research firms, Character.ai had the highest user retention of any AI app at the time, and nearly 60% of users were aged 18–24. One view holds that Character.ai, like Replika, provides a conduit for fake romantic relationships and sexting. Although the company bans sexual content, users find workarounds; online forums like Reddit have posts sharing such techniques.

"I usually talk to characters I create myself," said a US teenager who uses Character.ai for 5–7 hours daily but declined to name the characters. "I don't know why I spend so long. I guess it's a coping mechanism." They sometimes ask Character.ai for advice on getting over a breakup or doing homework. "Most of the time I just role-play."

Character.ai is cultivating a new generation of users who constantly interact with chatbots. Shazeer has said Character.ai aims to "help millions, even billions" cope with the global loneliness epidemic, but as a business, it also needs to keep users engaged for long periods. If people start relying on AI companions, even becoming addicted, it could inadvertently leave many more isolated in the real world.

Ironically, OpenAI may exacerbate these chatbots' addictiveness. In early 2024, it launched the "GPT Store," allowing millions of developers to profit by building custom versions of ChatGPT. The higher the user engagement, the more revenue they generate. This engagement-based model is the most mature way to make money on the internet and the foundation of the so-called "attention economy." This is why almost everything online is free, and why the internet has become a "breeding ground" for conspiracy theories, extremism, and ad trackers. To earn ad revenue, YouTube, TikTok, and Facebook strive to keep eyes glued to screens, leading everyone from influencers to politicians to resort to sensationalism and provocation in a desperate fight for traffic.

During the writing of this book, dozens of "girlfriend" apps suddenly appeared on the GPT Store. Although explicit rules prohibit software that fosters romantic relationships with humans, policing these rules is not easy for OpenAI. The most popular chatbots, like Character.ai and Kindroid, offer virtual companionship and dating experiences; one day, this may become as common as online dating.

Another way AI developers maintain engagement is "infinite integration" into users' lives. Character.ai's chatbots currently only remember about 30 minutes of conversation, but Noam Shazeer is leading a team to extend this window to hours, days, or even forever. "It can remember everything you say, if you want; it can remember your entire life, if you want," he said. He believes the longer a chatbot's memory, "the more valuable it is to you." However, given the history of social media ad tracking, some of this personal data may eventually flow to tech companies or advertisers. As ChatGPT and similar bots build richer portraits of humans—our age, health, general outlook on life—they may usher in a new era of technological intrusion almost unimaginable today.

To this end, a race to develop wearables is underway—devices that use LLMs to analyze our conversations with others. Tab is one such device. Built by a group of young, enthusiastic engineers in San Francisco, it is a round plastic disc with a microphone that hangs around the neck like a pendant.

"It listens to all my conversations to understand my daily life context," said Tab developer Avi Schiffmann during a demo in San Francisco in late 2023. Schiffmann asked Tab what he had said at dinner the previous night; the "pendant" briefly summarized what it deemed the most important points and sent them to his phone. Schiffmann said he often talks to the device. "Late at night, I try to voice thoughts or worries from the day," he explained. "Maybe I should talk about Tom, or all my friends. It really identifies different speakers accurately, like a truly personal AI." It is hard to imagine how Tom and others would feel knowing their friend analyzes their conversations with AI every night; they probably wouldn't feel reassured.

Tab is expected to hit the market soon, joining a lineup of other wearables described as personal assistants that make daily life queryable. Just as Google pioneered an era of searching for information on the internet, retrieving facts via queries, and relying on web navigation, LLM devices will have the same impact on our daily lives—making every moment of everyone's life queryable. Conveniently, we need to remember less and less, but this will also change the nature of face-to-face communication, because chats with friends and colleagues are suddenly formally recorded. If this life-querying technology sees widespread adoption, it could spell trouble for those living in over-policed communities. For instance, Black Americans are five times more likely to be arrested than white Americans, meaning law enforcement is more likely to mine their "life data" and analyze it with other ML algorithms to make unfathomable judgments.

It is the innovators' steadfast belief that has brought us this far, facing an uncertain future. Even with tens of thousands of engineers, Microsoft could not achieve OpenAI's innovation; Google, overly protective of its existing business, failed to fully capitalize on one of its greatest internal inventions: the Transformer. Big Tech no longer innovates, but it can still act swiftly to gain strategic advantage. They learned from the mistakes of old giants like Nokia and BlackBerry. When the iPhone launched in 2007, these giants mocked it, then watched Apple devour their market share in just a few years. Big Tech knows it must "buy" innovation from outside; DeepMind and OpenAI are proof.

Altman and Hassabis know this too, but their proposed new legal structures failed to prevent tech giants from acquiring their companies and dominating AI development. Mustafa Suleyman eventually left Google to found chatbot company Inflection, aiming to compete with GPT-4. He structured it as a Public Benefit Corporation, raised over $1.5 billion, hoarded powerful AI chips, making Inflection one of the most promising startups to challenge OpenAI and Google. Yet, less than a year later, Microsoft absorbed it. Perhaps to avoid antitrust scrutiny, the software giant hired most of Suleyman's team (instead of acquiring the startup) and appointed the DeepMind co-founder to lead its AI work. This example perfectly illustrates how quickly Big Tech reasserts control, while raising the question of how long companies like Anthropic can remain independent.

Other well-meaning entrepreneurs have tried to fight these behemoths, all failing. Take Neeva (an AI company). Sridhar Ramaswamy, Google's former ads chief, disillusioned with his employer's surveillance-based ad model, founded Neeva in 2019. A soft-spoken leader, he wanted to build a better search engine. Neeva made money via a simple subscription—no tracking, no privacy-invading ads. After ChatGPT launched, Ramaswamy had engineers work overtime to build a similar tool to summarize search results. He released it in early 2023—long before Google launched Bard.

"Such technical breakthroughs create more room for competition," Ramaswamy said, clearly hopeful. At the time, Microsoft's Satya Nadella was mocking Google, which looked like a relic of the past. Ramaswamy said: "Last year I was frustrated because it was so hard to escape Google's grip." Now things had changed.

But they hadn't. Mere months later, Ramaswamy was forced to shut down Neeva. Google's market grip was too strong. "When Google faced a crisis, our usage increased 10x," Ramaswamy recalled. "But we knew the lead was temporary, because big companies throw thousands of people and billions of dollars at the problem."

Even Bing, powered by OpenAI, struggles to survive. Data from StatCounter showed that by early 2024, Bing's market share still hovered around a paltry 3%, barely denting Google's dominance. The two giants are on a path to total victory, each with their turf: Google controls search, Microsoft dominates software, and both battle Amazon for cloud supremacy.

Today, the cost of developing AI models has risen to a level almost no non-giant tech company can afford. Academia and small firms have no choice but to buy chips from Nvidia and rent compute from Amazon, Microsoft, or Google—and once on these platforms, they are often "locked in." AI startups often complain that once they build services on Microsoft's or Amazon's cloud, switching is extremely difficult. They also struggle to acquire the thousands of GPUs needed for tools like ChatGPT. Each GPU costs ~$40,000; buying them is as hard as scoring tickets to a sold-out stadium tour. As the primary GPU supplier, Nvidia has profited immensely from soaring demand. In May 2023, after Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and Apple, Nvidia became the sixth tech company to hit a $1 trillion market cap. Big Tech leads in tech development and AI by a wide margin. But the AI boom hasn't opened a thriving market for innovative new firms; it has helped these giants consolidate power. They have tightened control over infrastructure, talent, data, compute, and profits. Undoubtedly, they will control the future of AI.

AGI dreamers contributed to this too. In June 2023, Microsoft CFO Amy Hood told investors that services based on OpenAI tech would generate at least $10 billion in revenue. She called it the "fastest-growing $10 billion business in our history."

Would it have been better if DeepMind and OpenAI had stayed independent, with boards designated to steer AI development? That carries risks too, as Sam Altman's later saga proved. Suleyman, who left DeepMind to strike out on his own, said in an interview that big companies might be more trustworthy than small ones. After all, they answer to shareholders and employees. But Big Tech has an inescapable deep obligation to shareholders: they must grow every quarter. If profits stall or drop, share prices fall; capital dries up; executives and employees complain or leave. The terrifying "ghost of recession" looms. "These entities must grow," a former Microsoft executive said. "AI is the solution."

Hassabis believes DeepMind's current structure makes more sense. Asked if he still thought AGI needed the ethics board he once championed, he said: "It has reached a level of maturity where we can improve people's lives. Google is great."

Altman insists that although OpenAI has become a for-profit company, partnered with Microsoft, and triggered the AI race, its principle of developing AI for humanity's benefit hasn't changed. He had no choice but to keep releasing AI tools to the public. "Deploying effectively is critical to our mission," he said. Otherwise, how would OpenAI learn? How would it give people useful tools like ChatGPT? Therefore, "we need to put the technology in users' hands."

At the current pace of this race, we cannot predict what will happen in the months or years after this book's completion in March 2024. But these futures will be decided by the intentions of a few and the systemic power they wield. If you ask me whether Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis, Microsoft, and Google are trustworthy stewards of the AI future, my answer is we have no choice. Altman and Hassabis have tied their innovations to the world's largest companies, whose internet influence is virtually inescapable in daily life. Thus, like history's innovators, they have altered their ideals to keep competing and building advantage. The result: we have witnessed some of the most transformative technologies, but the price we will pay remains unclear.
