
In European intellectual property law, *sui generis* intellectual property rights are not a common phenomenon. A comparable example is the *sui generis* database right introduced by the 1996 Database Directive.[⁴](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0821) This article examines the rationale for the creation of the *sui generis* database right and the European Commission's first evaluation of its practical effects,[⁵](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0822) summarizes its similarities with the currently proposed data producer right, and points out flaws in its justification.

## II. The Purposes of *Sui Generis* Intellectual Property Rights

The starting point of this article is to examine the purposes for which the European Commission introduced the *sui generis* database right, as mentioned in the evaluation report published in 2005. They are harmonization, promotion of investment, enhancement of data access, and strengthening of competitiveness, with particular emphasis on comparison with the United States when setting these objectives. All of these purposes can be found to some extent in the arguments supporting a new data producer right; in some cases, the wording used to justify the data producer right is strikingly similar to the objectives for creating the *sui generis* database right. These four objectives will be elaborated in detail below.

### (i) Harmonization

The harmonization objective is closely linked to the objective of promoting investment. The evaluation report states that the Database Directive is, first and foremost, an exercise in harmonization. Since investment in database production requires a "level playing field,"[⁶](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0823) action at the EU level was deemed necessary; therefore, one of the Directive's intended aims was to "eliminate differences in protection in Member States which hinder the functioning of the internal market."[⁷](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0824) The justification for introducing this new *sui generis* right was the need for harmonization; according to the Commission, "differences in the criterion of 'originality' required for copyright protection of databases hinder the free movement of 'database products' within the Community."[⁸](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0825) Common law countries protected databases under a lower "sweat of the brow" copyright standard, while other non-common law countries did not adopt this standard. Instead of harmonizing the copyright originality standard across the EU, thereby increasing the consistency of EU copyright law, the Commission decided to focus solely on the harmonization of database protection and, alongside copyright protection for databases, provided for a *sui generis* database right. The Commission considered that harmonization had to take place at the highest existing level of protection within the EU, requiring the establishment in all Member States of a protection standard for databases equivalent to the "sweat of the brow" principle applied mainly in the UK and Ireland. Under the Database Directive, databases protected by copyright require an element of intellectual creation, whereas the *sui generis* right has no originality requirement. By creating a *sui generis* intellectual property right distinct from copyright, the Commission circumvented the problem of getting Member States to agree on an originality standard under copyright law, while simultaneously expanding the overall scope of intellectual property protection beyond the IP framework accepted by the vast majority of EU Member States to date.

In the "Building a European Data Economy" communication, the Commission put forward reasons for establishing a *sui generis* data producer right, stating that raw data is currently not protected by EU law (except for the protection of the *sui generis* database right and trade secrets), but it vaguely mentions that Member States might decide to introduce such protection at the national level in the future. The Commission considered that "an uncoordinated approach risks fragmentation and would be detrimental to the development of the EU data economy and the functioning of cross-border data services and technologies in the internal market."[⁹](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0826) In other words, it is not actual differences in Member State laws that drive the consideration of harmonization to maintain a level playing field; even the mere theoretical possibility that "Member States... may decide to regulate the matter autonomously"[^(10)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0827) is considered sufficient reason to extend the scope of intellectual property rights beyond the standards currently established by any Member State. Similarly, the alternative approach of prohibiting Member States from introducing more far-reaching IP protection, thereby hindering the functioning of the internal market, was not discussed; instead, a novel *sui generis* protection for raw data was considered, because "modern, consistent rules are needed across the EU to allow data to flow freely from one Member State to another."[^(11)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0828)

A common objective of the *sui generis* database right and the data producer right is that their introduction is considered necessary to eliminate (potential or actual) differences in the scope of IP protection across the EU, thereby improving the functioning of the EU internal market. Undoubtedly, the application of the same rules by all Member States benefits the internal market. However, the degree of IP protection itself may also have an impact, because a higher level of protection does not necessarily lead to more trade and investment.

### (ii) Promotion of Investment

Encouraging and protecting investment in the creation of databases, and in the data itself, is a prominent objective of both the *sui generis* database right and the data producer right. The evaluation report states that "effective and uniform protection of non-original databases in all Member States" in the form of a *sui generis* right was considered an appropriate tool to achieve the objective of "attracting more investment in the creation of databases."[^(12)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0829) Furthermore, besides encouraging future investment, the Commission considered it "necessary to protect investment in the creation of databases against 'free-riding' by those who seek to appropriate the financial and professional investment."[^(13)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0830)

The same logic can be found in the "Building a European Data Economy" communication, which considers the data producer right as one of the policy options to "ensure a fair return on investment for (market players), thereby promoting innovation."[^(14)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0831) "Protecting investment and assets" is itself considered a policy objective, on par with other public policy objectives such as improving data access and promoting data sharing.[^(15)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0832) Following the introduction of a new intellectual property right, there is no explanation of what market mechanisms would lead to the expected increase in investment; it assumes that broader IP protection brings more investment, without empirical verification of this claim.

### (iii) Enhancement of Data Access

An important factor related to the intended objectives of the *sui generis* database right and the data producer right is the expectation that this new right would increase, rather than decrease, public access to protected data. This expectation is counter-intuitive, as, by definition of the respective rights, exclusive rights restrict the circle of beneficiaries who may use protected data without explicit permission.

Despite this apparent contradiction, the Commission determined that the introduction of the *sui generis* database right would contribute to the development of the European "information market,"[^(16)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0833) which would promote the re-use of databases more effectively than simply not protecting databases with proprietary rights. Therefore, the intended objective of the *sui generis* database right regarding data access is to "safeguard the legitimate interests of lawful users of databases,"[^(17)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0834) without considering whether not dividing users into lawful and unlawful would better serve user interests.

Similarly, "Building a European Data Economy" proposes a new data producer right as a policy that will "improve access to anonymous machine-generated data,"[^(18)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0835) rather than restricting data access as exclusive rights would predict, and "promote and incentivize the sharing of such data."[^(19)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0836) Like the objectives of the *sui generis* database right, user interests are seen as a reason *for* rather than *against* legislative intervention: "account should be taken of the inequality of bargaining power between companies and individuals," "lock-in effects should be avoided."[^(20)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0837) In situations of unequal bargaining power, "market-based solutions alone may be insufficient to ensure fair and innovation-friendly outcomes, unable to facilitate convenient access to data for new market entrants and avoid lock-in effects."[^(21)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0838) In other words, legislative intervention is considered necessary to prevent lock-in effects. The creation of a new exclusive right is explicitly regarded as the appropriate legal instrument to achieve this goal.

### (iv) Enhancing Competitiveness

Finally, a very explicit justification basis for the *sui generis* database right in the evaluation report is also reflected more generally in the "Building a European Data Economy" communication: the purpose of establishing the *sui generis* database right was to "enhance the global competitiveness of the European database industry by closing the gap with the US."[^(22)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0839) "Building a European Data Economy" does not explicitly link this objective to the data producer right, but it does state that "compared to the US, the European digital economy has been slow in embracing the data revolution."[^(23)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0840) The Database Directive's intended objective was to "increase European database production compared to the US,"[^(24)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0841) the advantage of this formulation being that it is relatively easy to conduct an empirical ex post evaluation.

## III. Lessons from the First Evaluation of the *Sui Generis* Database Right

The evaluation report completed by the European Commission in 2005, approximately ten years after the Database Directive entered into force, provides a good basis for studying whether the *sui generis* database right was successful in achieving its stated objectives. This is not because it is the most recent or comprehensive analysis of the *sui generis* right in practice, but because it is an evaluation conducted by the institution that proposed the right, which would generally view existing evidence from a more positive perspective. Yet, the evaluation report paints a picture of a completely failed legislative intervention.

Starting from the most objective criterion—increase in database production compared to the US—the evaluation report found that, in fact, the absolute value of database production declined after 2001,[^(25)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0842) by which time the vast majority of EU Member States had implemented the 1996 Database Directive into national law.[^(26)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0843) The gap between the US and EU shares in global database production actually widened significantly between 2001 and 2004, whereas between 1996 and 2001, this gap had initially narrowed (when many Member States had not yet transposed the Database Directive into national law).[^(27)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0844) The report noted that "US database production grew substantially, while in the EU, the introduction of *sui generis* protection appears to have had the opposite effect," concluding that "the assumption that more IP protection for 'non-original' databases means more innovation and growth does not seem to hold."[^(28)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0845) Notably, this conclusion seems to have been completely forgotten by the Commission, so much so that the 2017 "Building a European Data Economy" communication makes no mention of it, but instead once again puts forward the exact same dubious assumption about the relationship between additional IP rights and innovation.

Clearly, the evaluation report's conclusions regarding the *sui generis* database right's promotion of innovation and competitiveness are quite negative; the situation is equally bleak regarding the objective of improving data access. Compared to copyright protection for databases, database users expressed greater concern about the protection of the *sui generis* database right, considering that it "led to overprotection,"[^(29)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0846) with exceptions being too narrow. It was even reported that "certain libraries claim that the *sui generis* database right has already led to the concentration of major database producers (e.g., electronic journals) and the monopolization of information,"[^(30)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0847) a finding that should alert proponents of new *sui generis* IP rights, because, according to the Commission, the data producer right aims to avoid the emergence of "lock-in effects."[^(31)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0848)

Regarding the harmonization objective, the policy recommendations in the evaluation report reveal a rather ironic aspect. After concluding that the *sui generis* database right is "difficult to understand,"[^(32)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0849) leads to extensive litigation, "comes close to protecting data as property,"[^(33)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0850) and that "the economic impact of the *sui generis* right has not been proven," one would expect a recommendation to repeal the *sui generis* right to follow naturally from these negative results concerning the Directive's core objective. However, the evaluation report reached a different conclusion.

When comparing the expected costs and benefits of repealing the entire Directive, repealing or amending the *sui generis* right, or simply maintaining the status quo, the report concluded that the costs generated by any measure to address the obvious defects of the *sui generis* right would likely outweigh the benefits, "even if a piece of legislation has not been proven to have a positive impact on the development of a specific sector, repealing it is not necessarily the best choice."[^(34)](#chapter6.xhtml#wz_01_0851) Although the report does not state this explicitly, it is worth noting that repealing the Directive or the provisions introducing the *sui generis* right would not bring any direct improvement in any case, because removing the obligation for Member States to protect non-original databases via the *sui generis* right would not automatically produce the opposite effect; actually removing the *sui generis* database right from the national laws of EU countries would require a prohibition of such laws at the EU level.

## IV. Conclusion

What lessons can be drawn from the experience of the *sui generis* database right for the current debate on a possible data producer right?

First, the expected benefits in terms of promoting innovation, facilitating data access, and enhancing competitiveness need to be assessed more rigorously, because these assumptions were not realized after the creation of the *sui generis* database right, and one could even say they backfired.

Second, when adopting the argument of enhanced harmonization, it must be noted that while a more uniform legal framework produces positive effects, it also increases the difficulty of correcting past legislative errors. Once a *sui generis* right is established by an EU Directive, even if that right completely fails to achieve its objectives, repealing it is by no means easy. Therefore, the Commission should avoid creating new *sui generis* rights unless there are compelling economic and social reasons to justify their expected benefits, such justification ideally being supported by empirical evidence at the Member State level. The data producer right is a completely new concept not only in the EU but worldwide; the current legislative basis clearly does not meet the aforementioned criteria.

Finally, the Commission should conduct a thorough review of legislative experience[^((2))](#chapter6.xhtml#jz_01_1164) to ensure that when considering new legislative interventions, the experience of similar past legislative initiatives is thoroughly analyzed. If this requirement is correctly applied to the data producer right, it would inevitably require taking the Database Directive's evaluation report into account, thereby prompting reflection on the question: considering the importance of the principles of access to knowledge and freedom of expression, is breaking the convention of keeping raw data free from exclusive rights a good idea.
