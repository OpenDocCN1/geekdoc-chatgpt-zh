
Apart from copyright protection, data interfaces and data formats can also be protected as trade secrets, provided the relevant information possesses secrecy. In fact, this form of protection appears to be the primary mode of protection under US law.[^(37)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0553) Furthermore, the Frankfurt Regional Court held that XML files used for data exchange could potentially be protected under trade secret law.[^(38)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0554) However, the court denied protection based on trade secrets because it found the information to be clearly in the public domain, or at least readily accessible to third parties (given that it was known to countless customers).[^(39)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0555) Secrecy itself thus constitutes an obstacle to obtaining trade secret protection.

Moreover, once the EU Directive is transposed into national laws of Member States, a new issue arises for the Trade Secrets Directive: whether it will restrict the protection of trade secrets. Pursuant to Articles 2(1)(a) and 2(1)(c) of the Trade Secrets Directive, information regarding data interfaces must "be secret in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question" and "the person lawfully in control of the information has taken reasonable steps to keep it secret." Such requirements may become an obstacle for widely used interfaces to obtain trade secret protection.

**(2) Excluded Subject Matter**

More importantly: pursuant to Article 3(1) of the Trade Secrets Directive, the following acts shall be lawful: "independent discovery or creation," "observation, study, disassembly or testing of a product or object that has been made available to the public," and "lawful possession of information without any legally valid obligation to limit the acquisition of the trade secret."

Furthermore, even though Article 4 of the Trade Secrets Directive stipulates that the acquisition, use, or disclosure of a trade secret is unlawful—specifically "any unauthorized acquisition, appropriation or copying of documents, objects, materials, substances or electronic files, lawfully controlled by the trade secret holder, containing the trade secret or from which the trade secret can be deduced"—this provision permits reverse engineering, thereby allowing interface information to be replicated to a certain extent.[^(40)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0556)

##### 3. Patent Law Protection Mode

Patent law may also protect data interfaces.[^(41)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0557) However, this would apply more to the technical level, i.e., the underlying technology, but not to the data fields and sets of data fields discussed in this analysis.[^(42)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0558)

##### 4. Protection Mode under Unfair Competition Law, Civil Law, and Criminal Law (Tort Law)

Certain EU Member State laws based on tort law may protect data interfaces, for example, rules against slavish imitation[^(43)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0559) and regulations against password cracking.[^(44)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0560)

#### (II) Protection by Technical Measures and Factual Protection

Besides legal protection, it should be noted that, in practice, technical or factual protection may prove more effective than copyright protection. This is because, if the information constituting the data interface is not obvious, competitors may find it difficult to produce interoperable software.[^(45)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0561)

#### (III) Consequences of Protecting Data Interfaces and Data Formats: Obstacles to Free Flow of Data and Competition?

There is no doubt that, since the design of data interfaces and data formats requires certain investment, the economic theory of intellectual property indicates that investment should generally be protected.[^(46)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0562) Of course, the protection of investment should be limited to the extent necessary to promote innovation; the current question is whether and to what extent the protection of data interfaces harms the free flow of data and competition.

From a technical perspective, as stated previously,[^(47)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0563) regarding the implementation of data interfaces and their impact on data exchange and portability (distinguishing between different protection subjects), the following points must be noted:

Computer programs, i.e., program code, do not necessarily need to be copied to implement a data interface.[^(48)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0564) However, the situation has changed as programs become increasingly complex and functional data is also transmitted. Therefore, current law considers issues of interoperability, compatibility, and competition, meaning that the provisions of Articles 1(3) and 6 of the Software Directive are no longer fully adequate to some extent.[^(49)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0565)

It may sometimes be necessary to copy the database model in order to implement the interface at the database level. This applies where the interface is implemented by using access to the database.[^(50)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0566)

In any case, the data format must be copied to implement the interface and exchange all data through it. Moreover, data portability cannot be achieved without using the (same) data format. Therefore, to the extent that data formats are protected, this at least apparently impedes the free movement of data.

The conclusion drawn is that any protection of data interfaces, particularly data formats, appears to potentially harm the free flow of data, interoperability of software-based products, and data portability. After all, the European Commission had good reason to seek further exceptions to copyright protection in the legislative procedure underlying the Software Directive,[^(51)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0567) but this ended in a political compromise due to excessive lobbying.[^(52)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0568) Even this exception does not apply to the aforementioned works (if any), as will be elaborated below.

### III. Is Compulsory Licensing Needed (Amending Existing Law)?

In light of the above, the question arises as to whether current law limits protection in a workable manner, or whether compulsory licensing or similar measures are needed. The answer is given in advance: if such limitations exist, the exceptions regarding potential copyright protection are also limited; the same applies to compulsory licensing based on existing law.

#### (I) Articles 1(2) and 6 of the Software Directive

Articles 1(2) and 6 of the Software Directive ensure that at least the underlying ideas and principles of interfaces cannot be protected as computer programs, but these exceptions are limited. This is because these exceptions target computer programs and are therefore unrelated to other works (e.g., databases) and (other) literary works (database formats can be protected as literary works).[^(53)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0569)

Furthermore, if the software implementing the data interface is not protected as a computer program (at least not identically, but perhaps by analogy) due to lack of originality or at least lack of independence under Article 1 of the Software Directive, but only as a trade secret, the applicability of Article 6 of the Software Directive seems doubtful, particularly whether decompilation of the computer program is permitted. The reason is that, pursuant to Article 3(1)(b) of the Trade Secrets Directive, "observation, study, disassembly or testing of a product or object that has been made available to the public" and "lawful possession of information without any legally valid obligation to limit the acquisition of the trade secret" may be considered lawful. In fact, this seems to align with Article 4(3) of the Software Directive, which apparently only permits reverse engineering of a "black box" and does not permit decompilation.[^(54)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0570)

Finally, in cases where computer program components are protected and copying is technically necessary, Articles 1(2) (and possibly 6) of the Software Directive are of no help regarding the protection of data interfaces if the interface is too complex to be programmed independently.[^(55)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0571)

#### (II) Contract Law

It is widely debated whether contract law is sufficient to counterbalance such protection in the presence of intellectual property rights.[^(56)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0572) In short, contract law can work if there are sufficient incentives to license such rights. However, if the "owner" of the interface has no economic interest in sharing it with others, the contract law approach will fail.[^(57)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0573)

It is doubtful whether the contract law path can provide adequate protection for data interfaces and data formats. In fact, the European Commission acknowledged this when drafting the Software Directive[^(58)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0574) based on the experience of the *IBM* case,[^(59)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0575) and even more so today. Practice shows (e.g., the *Microsoft* case[^(60)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0576)) that if a software product is widely distributed on the market, or a customer has invested so heavily in software that they are locked in, the vendor's incentive to share interfaces and interface information decreases significantly. Potentially worse, the network effects of protected interfaces may be strategically used to restrict competition.[^(61)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0577) It is obvious that when software is relatively new on the market, interfaces seem to be distributed free of charge (along with the software), while the software manufacturer will change the terms of use and payment conditions for interface licensing in future versions of the software. In other words, the interface can be used as a **Trojan horse**[^(62)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0578), which may lead to vendor lock-in or network effects due to the necessity of sharing the interface.

Frankly, the contract law path is clearly insufficient to solve the above problems. Likewise, the European Commission acknowledges that in the data economy, data "ownership" may lead to users and businesses being locked into exclusive data-use architectures, and although "voluntary data sharing may emerge, the negotiation of such contracts may impose expensive transaction costs on the weaker party due to unequal bargaining power or the enormous cost of hiring legal experts."[^(63)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0579)

#### (III) Data Protection Law

Data protection law provides certain limited measures against vendor lock-in. It derives from Article 20 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), i.e., the data subject's right to obtain their personal data. However, the right to data portability relates to personal data, points to a one-off static act, and therefore does not allow for the permanent data exchange required for interoperability. Finally, it relates to the data subject, whereas to promote competition, such a right of access should be granted to competitors. In other words, the data protection law approach is essentially not pro-competition but aims to enable individuals to control the use of their personal data, as per the right to informational self-determination (*Recht zur informationellen Selbstbestimmung*) enshrined in the German Constitution.[^(64)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0580)

#### (IV) Competition Law

Therefore, the only viable general approach to limiting protection appears to be competition law. However, the standards of proof for market dominance and abuse of dominance are high, making the competition law approach also seem difficult to succeed and lacking in practicality. More importantly—due to the rapid growth of information technology and digital business—it will be insufficient to help competitors facing barriers and being driven out of the market (e.g., Netscape and Novell in the *Microsoft* case), as actions based on competition law are slow and take too long.

##### 1. Market Dominance

###### (1) De Facto Monopoly

First, the conclusion drawn from the *Magill* decision is that if information created by the right holder and protected by copyright is indispensable information, more precisely unique information, this may serve as an argument for market dominance. The reason is that such information cannot be recompiled or re-collected.[^(65)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0581)

However, theoretically, data interfaces can be programmed independently based on decompilation and reverse engineering. At least from the perspective of computer program protection,[^(66)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0582) it is doubtful whether the aforementioned case law rule helps prove market dominance. Although it is quite possible that, according to this rule, the content occupying a dominant position in the aforementioned data formats[^(67)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0583) is presented, i.e., the corresponding data format is indispensable and unique, created specifically to implement the corresponding data interface.

###### (2) Indispensability Standard

Furthermore, according to the *Microsoft* case[^(68)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0584) and the case law on Standard Essential Patents (SEPs),[^(69)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0585) it may be possible to conclude that a right holder holds a dominant position based on its market share in a primary market or a (de facto) standard. However, this does not apply to situations where pure vendor lock-in excludes any bargaining power for a license, thereby preventing customers or third-party competitors from using or creating data interfaces at a fair market price.

###### (3) Vendor Lock-in

Thus, the final question is whether competition law would support the argument that a right holder holds a dominant position based solely on "pure" vendor lock-in. In fact, this argument may apply where a customer, after deciding to use a specific system,[^(70)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0586) becomes dependent on the manufacturer due to heavy investment. This also seems to apply to complex software,[^(71)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0587) because users have invested in customizing such software, even more so when massive investments have been made in data across various systems. In this case, the lock-in effect is clearly independent of the use of any actual standard.[^(72)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0588)

Unfortunately, EU law does not contain a provision similar to Section 20 of the German Act against Restraints of Competition (*Gesetz gegen Wettbewerbsbeschränkungen*, GWB). In fact, such a provision in German competition law could help in cases of lock-in effects, as it extends the provisions on abuse of dominance to certain companies that do not have a dominant position but at least possess relative or superior market power.[^(73)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0589)

###### (4) Market Share

It is difficult to say that a software vendor or a potential right holder in data interfaces and data formats holds a dominant position solely due to the effects of vendor lock-in. In any case, it will be very difficult for a user to prove in court that a company holds a dominant position due to market share. This is a puzzle in itself, and even if not an impossible task in practice, proving this fact is extremely difficult.[^(74)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0590)

##### 2. Abuse

Arguing that the right holder or software vendor is abusing its dominant position is a challenging issue.

###### (1) Restriction of Competition and Requirement for a "New" Product (According to *Magill* and *IMS Health*)

The European Court of Justice emphasized in *Magill* and *IMS Health* the requirement of a serious impact on the relevant market (secondary market), i.e., the restriction or elimination of competition. The ECJ further emphasized the requirement for a "new" product,[^(75)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0591) which seems to greatly limit the applicability of competition law to data interface issues.[^(76)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0592) In fact, the "new product" referred to in *Magill* and *IMS Health* means a product not existing in the market in that way,[^(77)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0593) whereas in the *Microsoft* case, the Court's requirement for a "new product" seems less strict.[^(78)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0594) According to the General Court's judgment, a product having new functionalities seems to already satisfy the new product requirement.[^(79)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0595) This naturally raises a question: why can a request for (compulsory) licensing be made when substantial new functionalities are introduced to the market, meaning why should there be no competition for existing functionalities? The EU needs to address this discrepancy. At least according to the General Court's decision, the new product concept requirement may not apply to situations solely concerning factually protected information (e.g., information without IP protection).[^(80)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0596)

At this stage, it should be noted that there is a risk of interfaces and data formats being protected by intellectual property (especially copyright), and that even in a sense, data interfaces and data formats will not always be used to create the kind of "new product" seen in the *Microsoft* case. Therefore, neither *Magill* and *IMS Health* nor *Microsoft* will ensure that any obstacles to free data flow, interoperability, and data portability are adequately limited.

###### (2) Discriminatory Treatment under the Indispensability Standard (Based on SEP Case Law)

Furthermore, in cases concerning Standard Essential Patents (SEPs) (especially *Huawei*),[^(81)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0597) it was found that other specific circumstances may also constitute abuse of a dominant position, specifically requiring: 1) the IP right (patent) is essential for a standard set by a standard-setting organization; 2) it must be used when manufacturing products complying with the standard; 3) no license is offered on FRAND terms.[^(82)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0598) In this case, the refusal to grant an (individual) license constitutes abuse of a dominant position based on discrimination. The potential reason is that the "sole quid pro quo for obtaining SEP status is the irrevocable undertaking given by the patent holder to the relevant standard-setting organization, which is prepared to grant licenses on FRAND terms."[^(83)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0599)

In fact, data interfaces and data formats may sometimes be indispensable, and parts of them may even have become part of a standard-setting organization's standard.[^(84)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0600) But this is usually not the case; sometimes proprietary interfaces are preferred over standardized ones. In fact, requests for licensing or access to data interfaces and data formats are usually based on the technical need to exchange data with software or systems used by the company (vendor lock-in in a normal vendor-customer relationship context) or to achieve interoperability with third-party data formats used by many people (software with network effects). This does not necessarily mean the data format has become a de facto standard, let alone part of a basic standard provided by a standard-setting organization. Usually, the right holder/seller abuses the dominant position created by vendor lock-in due to investment in the software (the user cannot easily switch). Moreover, network effects may lead to the emergence of a dominant market player over years, harming competition, which may (after more or less reduced competition) lead to a (limited) obligation to grant access or a license.[^(85)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0601) SEP case law may not solve the data interface problem, but it may at least help in certain specific situations.

###### (3) Even if Abuse is Established, Effect is Limited

Furthermore, from a competition law perspective, it should be noted that to establish a claim for abuse of dominance, the claimant will need years and (correspondingly) enormous financial resources to pursue the claim, which small companies competing with a dominant undertaking may not possess. Even if the competent authority actively intervenes and imposes heavy fines for abuse of dominance, in practice, competition law usually only addresses market damage that has already occurred until a final decision is made, and has limited effect on market impacts already destroyed.[^(86)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0602) This view can be verified in the *Microsoft* case, where even companies like Novell (formerly a leading network OS provider) and Netscape (technological leader in browser technology) could not escape[^(87)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0603) this fate.[^(88)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0604)

In view of this, relying solely on competition law does not seem to be a perfect path.[^(89)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0605)

#### (V) Sector-Specific Laws

In addition to the general principles mentioned above, there are some specific statutory laws on data access in certain sectors (especially in the automotive[^(90)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0606) and telecommunications[^(91)](#chapter4_2.xhtml#wz_01_0607) sectors).
