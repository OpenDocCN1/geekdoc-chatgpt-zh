
EU Commissioner Günther Oettinger recently proposed a "Data Civil Code".[⁴](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0248) Given that the Civil Code sets clear rules for property, possession, and usufruct rights over movable and immovable property, Oettinger argues that we now need a Civil Code for the digital age to comprehensively regulate the issues arising around data rights, which must be implemented at the EU level.

In currently applicable law, there are no specific "property right" rules for data. However, those who collect data are not without protection. For contractual partners, companies can protect their interests through agreements: in the first instance mentioned above, Daimler can stipulate in its contract with TomTom for which purposes the navigation service may use transmitted vehicle movement data and for which it may not.

Regarding third parties, the legal position of data collectors differs based on various legal provisions. In Germany, criminal law prohibits industrial espionage involving data specially protected against unauthorized access.[⁵](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0249) The Unfair Competition Act (UWG) prohibits obtaining third-party business or trade secrets by technical means.[⁶](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0250) These rules enable the prosecution of those who obtain user data by "intruding" into company encryption systems. Both legal provisions reinforce the factual protection companies seek to achieve through technical means—such as encrypting collected user data—by threatening punishment.

Encrypted data is not the only protected object: The German Federal Court of Justice (Bundesgerichtshof, BGH) prohibited a publishing company from using certain data on the success of songs (sales figures and radio play rates) collected and published weekly by another company to create its own charts. The BGH issued the injunction based on provisions protecting investment in databases.[⁷](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0251) The Database Directive[⁸](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0252) underlying these regulations aims to incentivize the creation of mechanisms for storing and processing existing information.[⁹](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0253) However, legal experts generally agree that the initial act of collecting and storing data does not fall within the scope of database protection.[^(10)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0254)

If car manufacturer Daimler imposes no special obligations on its partner TomTom regarding the processing of transmitted vehicle movement data, it cannot object to further use of such data, e.g., in creating meta-analyses of traffic flows. Likewise, if the contract between the two companies lacks such a clause, Daimler cannot take action against TomTom selling the data to others. Thus, data may end up in the hands of companies unrelated to the original collection.

The same applies when a trading company like Amazon stores its customers' purchase history data. If information about previous purchases is used to provide tailored recommendations for future purchases, this information can be very valuable.[^(11)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0255) However, the mere act of storing data is not now, and has never been, subject to specific protective legal adjustments.

If a new data property right were created, the "owner" could prohibit others from using their data. In the TomTom example of sharing data with third parties mentioned above, Daimler could prohibit these companies from using the relevant data. Some scholars argue for the legitimacy of data property rights by analogy to tangible property.[^(12)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0256) However, this analogy is unconvincing. The legal system grants property rights in tangible objects (including land) because rivalrous use of tangible objects is usually impossible: if someone eats the cake, others cannot enjoy it. If someone harvests a field cultivated by another, the investor in sowing loses the yield. To avoid such conflicts, the law establishes exclusive use rights in the form of property.[^(13)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0257)

In contrast, data is merely information in machine-readable form. Generally, the law does not grant exclusive property rights in information, and for good reason: the property conflicts arising with tangible objects do not occur with information. Data can be copied with a mouse click and used by multiple people without conflict.[^(14)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0258) If A processes information, it does not prevent B, C, and D from simultaneously using the same information. A does not need a property right to prevent B, C, and D from using the information to retain the possibility of using it themselves. For goods of this informational nature, which many can consume simultaneously without additional cost, economists speak of non-rivalrous consumption.[^(15)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0259)

Some legal scholars base their call for data property rights on an analogy to copyright. However, on closer inspection, this comparison is not very convincing either: authors are protected because of their creative act.[^(16)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0260) The collection of user data by companies like Daimler or Amazon cannot be equated with a creative act.[^(17)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0261)

If someone has an exclusive right to use a specific piece of information, they can prohibit anyone from using it. Such a right would unnecessarily exclude others from use, potentially reducing social welfare.[^(18)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0262) Unconditional recognition of exclusive use rights to some or all data could lead to a "monopoly" over customer relationships. This is the core of the debate: a new property right would create an exclusive right with absolute effect—binding everyone—which would restrict competition between companies.[^(19)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0263)

The evolution of modern data protection law primarily manifests in the protection of personal data, providing a reference for solving the problems discussed here. However, so far, data protection law has been regarded merely as a defensive law: individuals can remain unaffected by the collection, processing, or use of their personal data without permission, and can demand to be informed which personal data concerning them have been stored. Requests going beyond this (e.g., demanding access to collected data) cannot be realized.[^(20)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0264) The driver of a Mercedes cannot demand the manufacturer send him a file containing the collected data on his car's driving, acceleration, and braking.

The general lack of a "portability" right for personal data collected by other companies allows companies to monopolize customer relationships: if only one company can offer tailored services to the customer, it has the customer. This means no competition emerges, consumers pay higher prices than necessary, and social welfare diminishes. Conversely, if a personal data portability right is created (as a result of further development of data protection law based on the individual), consumers will be able to trigger competition.

In May 2018, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)[^(21)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0265) introduced the right to data portability. Individuals will have the right to receive their personal data in a structured, commonly used, and machine-readable format.[^(22)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0266) Where technically feasible, individuals can also have their personal data transmitted directly to another company. The right to data portability promotes competition beneficial to consumer interests. The extent of its positive impact on competition will depend on the specific design of the portability concept and its practical application—the devil is often in the details.[^(23)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0267)

## III. Conclusion

The conclusion of this article is mixed: on the one hand, regarding the authorized commercial use of data, more clarity and legal certainty are needed. Those who have collected user data can actually protect it to a large extent from unauthorized use. Encryption and cooperation agreements provide the basis for this.[^(24)](#chapter3_1.xhtml#wz_01_0268) But instability remains in details: for example, how far does the effect of so-called database protection extend? Legal uncertainty is sand in the machinery of economic life. Internationally inconsistent provisions make domestic economic activity prohibitively expensive. This is why we should support the European Commission's initiative to create uniform and clear EU rules on data use. This concretization of the law on commercial data use would be a reasonable supplement to the provisions of the GDPR. The GDPR, applicable from 2018, aims to protect personality rights.

However, we must warn against the dangers of hasty legislation. The assumption of a data property right could create new obstacles to competition. In contrast, the current legal situation, although vague in some respects and in need of further improvement, is overall quite balanced.
