
The most controversial recommendation in the report is the introduction of an exclusive "data producer's right." Given the current state of knowledge, this proposal cannot be justified from an economic perspective. Since data is both a non-rival good and an excludable good (there is no serious copying problem), no exclusive property right is needed to ensure incentives for data production. So far, there is also no evidence that transaction problems in data markets are caused by a lack of property rights. Furthermore, a data producer's right can neither solve the problem of "inequality of bargaining power" in contractual negotiations concerning data, nor solve the problem of data access in multi-stakeholder situations. Moreover, if one considers that the introduction of an exclusive data producer's right in the digital economy would entail many costs and dangers for legal certainty, the promotion of competition, and innovation, such a right should not be introduced.[^(64)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0332) This does not preclude further research; for specific types of data, for example, where we have conclusive evidence of an insoluble copying problem, and thus an incentive problem, such an exclusive property right could be part of an appropriate policy solution. Regarding the proposal for a right of access to privately held data, the non-rivalry of data and the extreme importance of data access for the digital economy seem, from an economic perspective, to favor the establishment of a data access right. Although in certain cases involving public interest and scientific research, a data access right solution is relatively unproblematic, a general provision for mandatory access to privately held data by other private entities should be treated with great caution, even if it may appear to have positive effects on competition and innovation at first glance. It is necessary to analyze very carefully which data and under which circumstances such an obligation might be appropriate, particularly from the perspective of incentivizing the production of such data.

This may be different in specific sectors or in cases where several or many stakeholders have complex relationships regarding personal and non-personal data. Many of the examples currently discussed (smart cars, smart energy, smart homes, smart agriculture, and smart manufacturing) fall into this category. Here, it can be assumed that we need complex legal rules and rights specifically tailored to data. Such a "data governance regime" can be seen as a complete set of legal rules and rights concerning the collection/production, processing, storage, use/analysis, protection, access, and trading/sharing of data in a specific sector. Since the interests and costs of stakeholders as well as economic and technical conditions (and certain normative issues related to privacy) differ significantly across sectors, the optimal data governance solutions will also differ greatly from an economic perspective. However, economists also expect that in many sectors, markets can spontaneously find suitable data governance solutions and resolve them through contracts with various stakeholders. However, in complex cases (such as smart cars) or where other serious market failures exist, it may still be necessary to establish appropriate data governance regimes through regulatory or legislative measures. From this perspective, economic arguments can support a view already widely circulated in legal discussions. The next step should focus on analyzing specific data problems in specific sectors and developing appropriate data governance solutions for the respective problems.[^(65)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0333) If we gain sufficient knowledge and experience in the sector-specific analysis of data governance problems, it may be possible to develop more general solutions. Such a strategy would necessarily be in line with the basic philosophy and objectives of *Building a European Data Economy*.
