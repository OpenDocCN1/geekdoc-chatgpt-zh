
Without further legal intervention, these objectives may not be achievable. However, according to a widely accepted economic concept, government intervention should only play a role in cases of market failure. Recent studies suggest that market failure does not typically occur in the data economy.[^(22)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0355) This assessment must be weighed in the discussion on whether to introduce new legal instruments.

Compulsory licensing can build a bridge between the exclusive rights granted by normative provisions and the principles of antitrust law. As mentioned above, compulsory licensing attempts to correct monopoly situations that conflict with the principles of a competitive environment. However, given this function, compulsorily imposed licenses should not extend the scope of antitrust law interests. Therefore, the licensor's positive support obligations (e.g., delivery of know-how) are justified only in special circumstances.

## (II) Starting from Data Access

Data sharing and data transfer presuppose data access. In practice, data generators and controllers can decide on access rights to various datasets stored in structured storage. Only when data is accessed can transactions such as information sharing and information transfer take place. Since accessed data can be reused, the European Commission considers access to private and controlled data as key to a thriving data economy. Therefore, based on this sufficient rationale, the right to data access is viewed as a substitute for the data producer's right.[^(23)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0356)

If data access is refused, the issue of a compulsory licensing regime becomes the subject of discussion. As things stand, the concept of compulsory licensing is somewhat imprecise, because in reality there is no concept of compulsory licensing, but rather rules are proposed for granting certain data usage rights. This conclusion ignores the fact that the value of data itself is very small, while data access adds value to the data.

The European Commission believes that granting data access rights can produce welfare-enhancing effects without harming the economic interests of market participants investing in data collection.[^(24)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0357) Data sharing is a concept describing non-discriminatory access to certain data. This approach is neither intended to implement "open data" nor to grant data access rights without remuneration.[^(25)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0358)

A compulsory licensing regime must be limited to non-personal or anonymized business data, because access to personal data is already restricted by data protection law. Furthermore, it should be considered that data generation incurs certain costs, and data may have value; consequently, regulated access must ultimately be limited to specific categories of data. Additionally, even if data is non-rivalrous and reusable, potential loss of data quality must be guarded against; the concept of "data of public interest" has been introduced into French legislation on open data.[^(26)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0359)

The European Commission's proposal remains relatively vague, but its accompanying *Staff Working Document* mentions that some areas have already implemented compulsory licensing regimes, for example, the EU Directive on *unrestricted and standardized access to vehicle repair and maintenance information*, the *Payment Services Directive* (PSD II), the *Regulation on Registration*, and the *Evaluation and Authorization of Chemicals* (REACH).[^(27)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0360)

The European Commission is also aware of the need to anticipate licensing conditions, referencing other EU legal norms and some rules in Member State legislation.[^(28)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0361)

## (III) Compulsory Licensing in the Context of Data Sharing

So far, the discussion on data sharing has not been as intense as that on data transfer. The reason may be that the relevant facts and legal issues appear more complex. Transfer is an intuitive change of a good or service from one subject to another; its legal basis can be sale, lease, loan, etc. In contrast, sharing requires a form of cooperation, ultimately leading to thorny legal issues related to network structures.

A compulsory licensing regime enabling access to raw data may facilitate subsequent data sharing, because data availability—i.e., knowledge and information—is a prerequisite for sharing. However, the introduction of an intervention regime via compulsory licensing must take into account existing arrangements that allow data sharing, as well as other incentives encouraging companies to share data (e.g., on specific data platforms, pro-competitive data sharing among market participants). Such alternative arrangements can exist in data payment, cooperation concepts, and consensual solutions regarding specific reproduction rights.

### 1. Data Payment by Mutual Consent

If data represents economic value, it can also be viewed as a means of payment. In this case, data can serve as a supplementary consideration in place of money.

In the EU, the *Draft Directive on Digital Content*[^(29)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0362) attempted to reflect this approach for the first time. The acquirer of digital content can provide personal data available for use instead of money (personal data or other data other than money, Art. 3(1)).[^(30)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0363)

In the event of contract termination, the seller must return the data to the customer in a commonly used format within a reasonable period, without charging the customer a corresponding service fee. The problem lies in the fact that the proposed wording differs from the corresponding provisions in the *General Data Protection Regulation*.[^(31)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0364)

Currently, the method of data payment by mutual consent exists only in consumer contracts within the *Draft Directive on Digital Content*. However, extending this concept to B2B business seems feasible. Data as the subject matter of B2B contracts is a reality, but the legal framework has not yet been established (particularly regarding data recovery). Specific provisions could increase legal certainty.

### 2. Cooperation Concepts

In the context of big data analytics, the idea of cooperation concepts has been particularly developed.[^(32)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0365) A practical example is the healthcare sector. If individuals transmit personal data to a central health database, the benefit may lie in the possibility of retrieving information from that database for their own use.[^(33)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0366)

Regarding big data analytics, discussions on wealth maximization also involve the insurance industry. It is claimed that both insurers and policyholders can benefit from the large databases generated by big data analytics.[^(34)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0367)

Cooperation concepts are often referred to as wealth-sharing strategies: the premise of such concepts is that the data controller grants the individual the right to access their data in a usable format; likewise, individuals should be allowed to use applications enabling them to analyze their personal data and draw useful conclusions.[^(35)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0368) If an agreement is not reached, the legislator may have the option to stipulate the parties' obligations by law.

### 3. Reproduction Right

In Switzerland, a member of parliament proposed inviting the Federal Council to evaluate the desirability of introducing a reproduction right. For example, the right to reproduce personal data for reuse. Individuals should be granted a specific right enabling them to commercially exploit their personal data. The economic rationale lies in the potential value an individual can realize when reusing certain data.[^(36)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0369)

The Federal Council's initial reaction was negative.[^(37)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0370) In this situation, it remains doubtful whether parliament will be more open; therefore, a right such as the reproduction right will likely not be enshrined in Swiss law.

## (IV) Compulsory Licensing in the Data Transfer Environment

Similar to data sharing, data transfer also relies on data accessibility or knowledge of relevant information. Whether data can be transferred depends on the relevant person's/entity's assessment of the data content to be transferred. In this context, the following legal concepts can be considered.

### 1. Intellectual Property Rights

Intellectual property law establishes de facto ownership, holding an exclusive position in rights protection. Commercialization of rights can be achieved through contracts (consensual licensing) and, in certain special cases, through compulsory licensing. In recent years, intellectual property law has become the primary field for implementing compulsory licensing.[^(38)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0371)

### 2. Producer Rights

The right to use and authorize others to use non-personal data can be realized by establishing a special data producer right (i.e., the owner or long-term holder of the data). This right would reflect a new category of ownership, because the data producer has the right to use the data and helps unlock machine-generated data.

The European Commission proposed a data producer right in its report: its scope is broader than traditional rights protecting know-how and does not correspond to the concept of investment protection as described in the Database Directive. However, it has more limitations compared to full ownership.[^(39)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0372)

### 3. Contractual Rights

The "owner" or controller of data may conclude contracts transferring the right to use data to a third party; usually, the rights granted are based on a consensual license agreement requiring the licensee to comply with certain conditions and requirements.

### 4. Right to Data Portability

The term data portability refers to the right of individuals and businesses to transfer their data from one system to another. In practice, data portability can be realized if switching costs are low and there are no anti-competitive obstacles hindering data transfer. The right to data portability stems from the idea that individuals should maintain control over their data.[^(40)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0373)

The data portability approach differs from the compulsory licensing system because the right to data portability does not demand access to managed data, but rather the data "owner" has the right to transfer their data from one service provider to another. Nevertheless, they share some similarities. Therefore, it seems necessary to discuss the right to data portability in more detail.

## Four, Can Data Portability Serve as an Analogous Model?

For years, portability has been a topic discussed in antitrust law.[^(41)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0374) Recently, data portability has also become a legal norm in data protection law.[^(42)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0375)

### (I) Antitrust Law

Traditionally, portability has been widely discussed as an antitrust issue because of its connection to lock-in effects of installed software. Another example involves the transfer of relevant information. In principle, there are various norms in antitrust law that can strengthen data portability, but practical obstacles cannot be ignored.

#### (1) Competition Law Applicable Only if Data Controller Holds Dominant Position (Art. 102 TFEU)

In the data sector, establishing the requirement of a dominant position raises significant problems.[^(43)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0376) If the market is not defined in a very narrow way, a dominant position is difficult to establish. Furthermore, the European Court of Justice's previous practice was to handle individual and specific infringement standards on a case-by-case basis (mainly in *Magill*, *IMS Health*, and *Microsoft*), i.e., information or data clearly identifiable and definable in court rulings.[^(44)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0377) In the case of machine-generated (big) data, portability involves data that is much larger in volume and unknown and unspecified in content.

#### (2) Antitrust Litigation Usually Time-Consuming and Laborious

Generally, as shown by many lawsuits against Microsoft and Google, a new technology has often already been developed before a competent court renders a judgment.

### (II) Data Protection Law

To overcome the pressure of antitrust law, the *General Data Protection Regulation* now contains a specific provision introducing a right to data portability. Obviously, originally generated data is not excluded; the portability obligation also includes data collected by sensors (e.g., smart meters). After a long history, the final wording was determined as: the data controller must transmit personal data "in a structured, commonly used and machine-readable format" to a service provider designated by the data "owner". This transmission should occur without hindrance from the data controller and without any cost to the data "owner".

This provision has been severely criticized, particularly by industry circles.[^(45)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0378) If required to transfer data, service providers, besides bearing additional costs, consider government intervention inappropriate as it increases burdens without a clear assessment of its economic rationale.

### (III) Cross-Border Portability of Online Content Services

In December 2015, the European Commission submitted a proposal for a Regulation on ensuring the cross-border portability of online content services in the internal market.[^(46)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0379) Similarly to personal data under Art. 20 GDPR, individuals should be able to move data to another place or use a specific service in another place. In fact, the main driver of this proposed regulation is the principle of equal treatment between the country of residence and the country of temporary stay. The rationale for the new rules can be seen in the 2011 Premier League copyright case, where the ECJ ruled that decoding devices lawfully distributed in one country can move freely within the EU.[^(47)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0380)

The purpose of the Regulation is to grant consumers temporary access to certain data outside their licensed territory. Besides defining individual rights, the Regulation combines the contractual relationship between the user and the internet service provider, and between the internet service provider and the content provider (right holder). Through the newly adopted legal norms,[^(48)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0381) service providers will be able to offer more attractive services to consumers.[^(49)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0382)

## Five, Practical Implementation of Compulsory Licensing Regimes

### (I) Horizontal vs. Vertical Approach

The European Commission proposed a horizontal regulatory approach, i.e., introducing a general rule allowing the implementation of a compulsory licensing regime.[^(50)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0383) Usually, a horizontally applicable licensing concept favors covering a wide range of factual situations and realizing the principle of equal treatment. Moreover, general rules can be formulated in a clear and understandable manner without having to contain too many detailed provisions. However, the question remains whether the complexity of the data economy is adequately reflected in a general rule.

The alternative is to construct sector-specific regulations. The automotive industry needs different rules, e.g., those involving the health or insurance industries. The disadvantage of sector-specific legislation lies in the multitude of rule types, and the design of specific norms is often less consistent. The European Commission pointed out that several sectoral laws in EU law contain concepts of compulsory licensing.[^(51)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0384) However, only sector-specific regulations can easily reflect differences in markets, corporate structures, and consumer needs. Even if a general rule is introduced, concretization regarding the scope of application is inevitable.

Given the different characteristics of the data economy, a sector-specific normative approach seems more feasible regarding the concrete design of compulsory licensing regimes. Furthermore, this approach helps overcome constitutional concerns raised by compulsory licensing regimes regarding the "fundamental right to freedom of economic activity".[^(52)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0385)

### (II) Conditions for Compulsory Licensing

The European Commission explicitly stated in its report that compulsory licensing must combine the market environment with specific conditions. The *Staff Working Document* references several provisions, further narrowing the scope of implementation of compulsory licensing.[^(53)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0386)

#### (1) Pricing Rules

If the data controller is obliged to open access to certain data, the licensee usually has to pay a certain fee, because data reflects value; however, quantifying the price of data is a difficult task, and there seems to be a general consensus that data itself has no intrinsic value, and the potential price of data depends on the usage scenario and data interaction environment.[^(54)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0387) In a recent report, the OECD argued that data prices do not follow basic market rules, tending more or less towards the intersection of supply and demand, because two factors seem to influence the value of data more directly: data accuracy and timeliness.[^(55)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0388) If this difficulty can be completely overcome, it would obviously prove that a sector-specific approach to rule-making is more reasonable, because the value of data is directly related to the expected reuse scenario.

#### (2) Compulsory Licensing Regime Must Define the Volume of Data Granted Access

According to the concept of a reasonable licensing regime, data can only be compulsorily licensed to third parties who cannot collect that data by other means; the level of detail provided depends on the relevant sector of the data economy.

#### (3) Compulsory Licensing Regime Must Define the Scope of Data Reuse

Since compulsory licensing is intended to correct market failure, particularly abuse of a dominant position, rules specifying the purposes for which the licensed data may be used seem reasonable, similar to the principles contained in Art. 10 of the PSI Directive[^(56)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0389) and its revised version.[^(57)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0390)

#### (4) Licensing Conditions Must Clarify the Extent to Which Data Transfer Can Be Bundled

This is a well-known issue in antitrust law. If a rights holder uses bundled data tied to goods or services, and the corresponding tying behavior leads to competitors being excluded from market competition, then bundling sales and tied transactions become antitrust issues.[^(58)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0391)

In antitrust law, the design of licensing conditions is a well-known issue. Discussions usually revolve around FRAND (Fair, Reasonable, and Non-Discriminatory) terms.[^(59)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0392) Compulsory licenses with FRAND terms in practice mainly apply to situations where patent holders implement specific strategies (e.g., patent ambush and patent hold-up); reality poses challenges to the actual implementation of FRAND terms and their reasonable design in court practice.[^(60)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0393) From an antitrust perspective, significant concern arises if a dominant company is abusing its dominant position, distorting market competition.[^(61)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0394)

Norms for licensing conditions in specific sectors still need to be developed. To harmonize the application of compulsory licensing regimes across different sectors, it is necessary to consider drafting model license agreements.

It must be remembered that, according to the normative concept, compulsory licensing concerns non-personal (machine-generated) data; however, it is not difficult to personalize these non-personal data, so the applicability of data protection law must be preserved.[^(62)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0395)

### (III) Enforcement Issues

Two decisions in 2016 demonstrated the high difficulty and time-consuming nature of actual antitrust enforcement:

#### (1) After a Three-Year Investigation, the European Commission Adopted a Decision in 2016

Accepting legally binding commitments from the International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA) and the information service provider Markit to provide FRAND licenses for inputs for credit default swaps.[^(63)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0396) Consequently, all trading venues can benefit from fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory licenses to data and intellectual property owned by ISDA and Markit. This decision may bring more choices and lower transaction costs for investors, and will increase market stability.

#### (2) After Two Years of Litigation, the EU Court Decided in the 2016 Morningstar Case

Regarding the global consolidated real-time data feed market, the European Commission's acceptance of commitments made by Thomson Reuters would allow reasonable access to data.[^(64)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0397) Morningstar's complaint was dismissed because market participants could appropriately use Reuters Instrument Codes (RICs). Naturally, the Court reiterated that the principle of proportionality must always be observed.

## Six, Comprehensive Evaluation

Undoubtedly, the importance of data sharing and data transfer is increasing day by day. The development of the data economy in the context of the Digital Single Market requires a legal framework that promotes data exchange. Whether the introduction of a property rights approach is a forward-looking mechanism remains doubtful, because property rights may not be the best way to handle data.[^(65)](#chapter4.xhtml#wz_01_0398) A compulsory licensing regime may be a more appropriate regulatory approach, as it can ensure data access and facilitate data sharing and transfer; of course, more empirical studies seem necessary to justify the rationality of this new framework.

The discussion on property rights and compulsory licensing faces the important issue of data pricing: data itself has no intrinsic value; the potential price of data depends on usage and processing patterns (particularly in the case of big data analytics). Concerns related to property rights and pricing apply equally to compulsory licensing: general norms or obligations may not be sufficient to meet the reasonable needs of data sharing and data transfer regimes in practice.

Specific legal norms must be designed to set the compulsory licensing regime within a relatively narrow scope of application: at least out of consideration for the constitutional freedom of economic activity, this approach should not play the role of a primary instrument in the digital economy.
