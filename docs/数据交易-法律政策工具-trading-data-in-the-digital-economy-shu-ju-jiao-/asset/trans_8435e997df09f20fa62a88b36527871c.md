
In January 2017, the European Commission published the report "Building a European Data Economy"[¹](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0269) and the accompanying Staff Working Document (hereinafter referred to as the "Data Economy SWD").[²](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0270) This is part of a policy approach dedicated to addressing how the legal and regulatory framework[³](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0271) of the EU market should respond to the challenges of the new digital economy, in which data is regarded as a key resource for innovation and welfare. Although the report addresses issues such as the free movement of data within the EU, liability, data portability, data interoperability, and data standards, the main issue is "data access and transfer," as the Commission considers that most data generated in the digital economy is not sufficiently shared and used. The report does not focus on personal data protected by European data protection law, but rather on non-personal machine-generated data (particularly raw data, e.g., data generated by sensors), which will play an increasingly important role in smart manufacturing and the Internet of Things. Since property rights generally cannot protect such data, this directly triggers academic discussion on the concept of creating a new *sui generis* intellectual property right over (non-personal) machine-generated (industrial) data.[⁴](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0272) Since most data is only used internally by companies and not shared or recombined with others, the report makes a number of targeted policy recommendations, including the introduction of an exclusive "data producer's right" and a compulsory access right to data held by private entities.[⁵](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0273) It can be said that the European Commission is leading the cutting-edge discussion on "data rights."

This article aims to analyze the core issues, justifications, and policy recommendations in the report from an economic perspective.[⁶](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0274) This article focuses on the definition of new data rights, particularly whether the data producer's right should be an exclusive property right while also paying attention to the data access right. To some extent, the views in this article were already formed in a paper published before the report,[⁷](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0275) in which the author analyzed from an economic perspective whether it is necessary to create a new intellectual property right for non-personal data in the digital economy. In that paper, the author concluded that, based on current theoretical and empirical knowledge, there is no need to introduce a new intellectual property right for non-personal data, and such a new exclusive right could even have serious negative effects on legal certainty, competition, and innovation in the digital economy. However, the more general issue of "data rights" is very complex and has not been sufficiently explained to date. This article intends to analyze from an economic perspective in two ways: (1) analyze and evaluate the reasons for the data economy issues in the report and the Commission's policy recommendations; (2) attempt to clarify and extend the discussion on data ownership, data rights, and data governance, and provide guidance for necessary research directions. The policy issues raised by the European Commission are economic policy issues, meaning that the question of whether data needs new property or data access rights must be approached from the perspective of: the existence of market failures and what legal rules and rights can help correct these market failures and promote market development. From a legal perspective, this is a pragmatic approach, using economics as an analytical tool to better understand the impact of various laws and rights concerning data on competition, innovation, and welfare.

The structure of this article is as follows: Section 2 briefly introduces the main arguments, objectives, and policy recommendations regarding data access in the report (including an explanation of the proposed "data producer's right"). Section 3 focuses on the arguments regarding the fundamental economic characteristics of data (non-rivalry and excludability) and explores the reasons for the lack of incentives for data generation. Section 4 will discuss potential market failures in data trading and the corresponding policy recommendations in the report. Due to "inequality of bargaining power," the European Commission is deeply concerned about contractual arrangements for data; therefore, Section 5 will attempt to clarify this issue and discuss whether the data producer's right can help solve these problems. Section 6 addresses the data access problem by introducing a right of access to privately held data, particularly in situations involving multiple stakeholders, where the data access right may play an important role in broader data governance solutions. Section 7 concludes: the data producer's right should not be generalized, but a qualified data access right may be an important component of data governance solutions in specific sectors, which still require more specific research.

## 2. Data Access Rights and Data Producer's Rights in the Report: An Overview

Since most holders of machine-generated data do not allow access to their data but only use it themselves (internally), this leads to the European Commission's primary concern: the vision of a thriving data economy cannot be fully realized. Empirical evidence shows that instances of data sharing and reuse are not common, and data markets are only emerging slowly and are not widely used.[⁸](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0276) In other economic contexts, allowing further access to this data for reuse would bring greater benefits and promote innovation in the digital economy. Therefore, the main objective of the report is to increase access to and sharing of machine-generated data.[⁹](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0277) However, the European Commission also identified a problem: manufacturers of devices purchased by users typically actually control the data generated by the device and prevent users from authorizing third parties to use the data.[^(10)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0278) Therefore, the future EU data access framework[^(11)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0279) should achieve broader objectives. The report lists five objectives, which from an economic perspective can be reduced to three distinct main objectives:[^(12)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0280)

1.  Facilitate access to and sharing of machine-generated data;
2.  Protect investments, assets, and confidential data, and ensure a fair return on investments that promote innovation;
3.  Ensure fair sharing of benefits among data holders, processors, and application providers in the value chain, and address the problem of unequal bargaining power between companies and private entities (particularly SMEs and start-ups), such as "lock-in effects."

The first objective concerns the economic benefits of non-rivalrous data reuse; the second concerns indirect incentives for data and innovation investment; the third seems to focus on the fair distribution of data benefits, especially in B2B and B2C contexts, on how to solve the problem of unequal market bargaining power.

Based on these issues and objectives, the report discusses many specific policy options. A careful analysis of the six proposed measures yields three main groups of strategies:[^(13)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0281)

(1) The first group can be interpreted as measures to promote data sharing and trading through contracts (guidelines on how to handle data control in contracts, technical solutions to facilitate reliable identification and exchange of data, and default rules that can serve as benchmarks for data-related contracts).[^(14)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0282) However, they not only intend to reduce transaction costs but also hope to address issues of negotiation fairness and inequality of bargaining positions (e.g., through unfairness control in B2B contractual relationships).[^(15)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0283)

(2) The second group of measures aims to solve the data access problem by introducing a compulsory access right to privately held machine-generated data. This data access right includes actions by public authorities for "public interest" and scientific purposes, but the report also recommends a right for private entities (e.g., based on FRAND terms) to compulsorily access privately held data, effectively a "compulsory licensing"[^(16)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0284) solution.[^(17)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0285)

(3) The third group of measures is the introduction of a new "data producer's right." This exclusive right grants the owner or long-term user of the (data-producing) device the exclusive right to use the corresponding data and authorize others to use it. The main purpose of this right is to clarify the legal status, provide data producers with more options for utilizing their data, and help resolve the lock-in effect problem with machine-generated data.[^(18)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0286)

Therefore, the measures adopted in the report are dedicated to increasing accessible data: on the one hand, by reducing the costs of data trading and licensing (laws facilitating data access); on the other hand, by setting mandatory regulations on data access rights and introducing new exclusive rights for machine-generated data.

Because the European Commission is fully aware of the potential long-term implications of the "data producer's right," the *Data Economy SWD* discusses this issue comprehensively.[^(19)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0287) This right covers non-personal or anonymized machine-generated data; however, this information should only be protected at the syntactic level (data as "signs"), not at the semantic level (the meaning of the data), i.e., data should only be protected as a compilation. It must be emphasized that it should not be regarded as a "super intellectual property right."[^(20)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0288) A tricky question is to whom this new data producer's right should be granted. The European Commission proposes several criteria, particularly emphasizing the entity that invests funds in data creation, while also mentioning the device manufacturer and the economic operator. Surprisingly, the report explicitly identifies the owner or long-term user of the device as the data producer to whom the exclusive right should be granted.[^(21)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0289) However, there should also be exceptions, particularly that "manufacturers or public authorities should provide non-exclusive access to data."[^(22)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0290) Interestingly, the *Data Economy SWD* explicitly discusses whether the data producer's right should be an absolute right (similar to a property right) or merely a purely defensive right to protect the data holder's de facto "possession," for example, similar to the protection of trade secrets/know-how against illegal misappropriation of data. The report does not mention this option, but it is similar to the choice advocated by many legal scholars not to introduce an exclusive property right but to rely on and potentially improve the current legal situation; this choice essentially already provides data holders with some (defensive) rights (mainly through trade secret protection).[^(23)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0291)[^(24)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0292)

## 3. Economic Characteristics of Data: Non-Rivalry, Excludability, and the (Puzzle of) Lack of Incentives

What are the fundamental economic arguments about data and the data economy, explicit or implicit, in the report? A key issue concerns the economic characteristics of data. The use of data is non-rivalrous, and therefore it should be used as much as possible; this is one of the core assumptions in the report. This assumption is based on the economic view that allowing the widest possible access to data benefits innovation in products and services (data-driven innovation).[^(25)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0293) From this perspective, considering the reality that much data collected by companies is not used or only used internally and not made available to other companies, raises the issue of potential market failure. Therefore, the European Commission's focus on data access, sharing, and transfer is undoubtedly pertinent. However, before solving these problems, we must first examine the production of this data.

The fact that data can be regarded as a non-rivalrous intangible good leads to the question in academic discussions of whether data should be protected in a manner similar to intellectual property.[^(26)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0294) This is because the non-rivalrous nature of data does not support an analogy to physical attributes, while rivalry in use provides the main justification for granting exclusive rights to tangible property. However, the main justification for providing exclusive intellectual property protection for innovations and original works has always been the need to solve the incentive problem for producing these intangible goods, because imitation through knowledge spillovers (or direct copying) would mean that the effort of innovation or creative works would not be adequately rewarded, thus requiring incentives. From an economic perspective, this is one of the surprising aspects of the discussion on data property rights: no one claims there is an incentive problem, i.e., insufficient incentives to produce data due to too-rapid imitation or copying. Interestingly, empirically, there does not appear to be a serious data copying problem similar to the well-known issues with IP-protected digital goods. Although the *Data Economy SWD* briefly mentions investment in data,[^(27)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0295) it never addresses whether the data economy suffers from insufficient incentives affecting data production, which would then require data property rights to solve this incentive problem. The European Commission seems to implicitly assume—consistent with academic discussions on data ownership—that there is no market failure in the incentive mechanism for data production; the problem lies mainly in the access to and sharing of existing data. However, if there is indeed no incentive problem for data production, then for non-rivalrous intangible goods, the economic rationale supporting exclusive intellectual property rights fails, and the arguments supporting the "data producer" cannot stand.[^(28)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0296)

The author agrees with a general view that the empirical evidence of exponentially growing data production, which can be expected to continue or even accelerate in the future (especially through IoT sensor data),[^(29)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0297) does not support serious concerns that the data economy suffers from a general lack of incentives for data production. However, to correctly understand the data economy and the effects of the proposed policy measures, it is necessary to clarify why this incentive problem seems not to exist: data will be produced if the data producer's benefits exceed costs. One possible explanation is that the cost of producing data is zero or very low. If data is a by-product of other production processes, this explanation may hold true. If data production has significant economies of scale and scope, then at least the marginal cost of producing additional data may approach zero. However, in most cases, data collection involves significant fixed costs, such as producing and installing sensors, or providing "free" services in exchange for the possibility of collecting data from users. In recent discussions, it has also been emphasized that "curating" data can incur substantial costs, i.e., the costs of ensuring data quality, recording data provenance, and converting it into formats that allow the data to be used (interoperability).[^(30)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0298) Regarding personal data, consent must be obtained and properly stored, and effective anonymization of personal data also incurs costs. Therefore, one cannot assume that the cost of producing datasets is usually zero or very small. On the contrary, we can expect data production costs to vary greatly, depending on the type of data and the economic and technological environment in which they reside. These costs of data production can also be applied to the discussion on the protection of data investments.[^(31)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0299)

If data production is not costless, then the lack of a general incentive problem implies that data producers can derive sufficient benefits from this data to cover costs, i.e., by using the data themselves or allowing others to use, trade, and license the data through data sharing. An important prerequisite for ensuring revenue possibilities is the second economic characteristic of data: data holders are usually able to exclude others from using their data (excludability), particularly through data secrecy (e.g., encryption). Empirically, data secrecy does not seem to be a problem if data is stored and used internally.[^(32)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0300) Data holders can commercialize their data, for example, by using it to innovate or improve their own products, or to sell other services to their customers (e.g., predictive maintenance and data analytics). Another important option is that generated data is only used internally to develop and provide data-based services to other companies (e.g., targeted advertising). Therefore, the fact that data is not directly provided to other companies does not allow the conclusion that the information in this data is not provided to other companies in the data economy. Through Google and Facebook's targeted advertising services, many advertisers can use the information in these companies' vast datasets without directly accessing the data. Therefore, we should also be cautious: if these companies hold data themselves and have not established direct access to it, one cannot directly conclude that the corresponding data use is sufficient.

Another way to reap benefits from produced data is to sell or license it directly to other companies for use, linking the incentive problem of data production to the problem of data trading. If there is serious market failure in data markets, then revenues from selling or licensing data that are too low may lead to insufficient incentives for data production, resulting in underproduction of data. One potential market failure in selling or granting data access rights (licensing) is that the buyer (or licensee) should not resell the data to others. This raises the question again: can the excludability of data be guaranteed? That is, if a data holder grants access to data to other companies through data trading, it can control the use of the data. Both technical and contractual restrictions can be used to solve this problem (provided these restrictions are effective), allowing data holders to ensure their actual control over data, even when allowing other companies direct access to it. In the discussion in Section 4, we will find that so far there is no empirical evidence that technical and contractual restrictions cannot sufficiently effectively ensure de facto control in data trading (and thus excludability). All this is supported by the fact that we have not found a serious data copying problem.

What conclusion does this analysis yield? Although data production costs exist, there is no general incentive problem; de facto data "ownership" (through "de facto excludability") seems to enable firms to derive sufficient benefits from their data, which rules out the necessity of protection through exclusive property rights. However, this result also implies that—due to the costs of data production—this de facto control over privately held data is necessary to maintain incentives for data production. This is an important implication for evaluating policy recommendations on compulsory access to this data.

However, a deeper analysis of data economics will show that in different economic and technological environments, the cost and benefit conditions of data production can vary greatly. Moreover, there may be significant positive and negative externalities in the costs and benefits of data production, which on the one hand creates a divergence between private and social costs of data production, and on the other hand a divergence between private benefits and social value of data. Depending on these circumstances, incentives for data production may be excessive or insufficient. Therefore, while we empirically believe there is no general underproduction of data due to insufficient incentives, this does not mean incentives are always optimal, nor does it mean there are not various types of data for which incentives are either insufficient or excessive, affecting the production of certain types of data accordingly. Therefore, we cannot rule out market failure due to incentive problems in data production either.[^(33)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0301) Neither the report nor the *Data Economy SWD* raises the question: for certain types of data, is there an incentive problem for data production? Would this become a problem affecting the prosperity of the data economy? Addressing this question clearly requires more in-depth research.

## 4. Market Failure in Data Trading? An Open Question

In academic discussions, the most important argument for introducing exclusive property rights for machine-generated non-personal data is to enhance their tradability in the market. From the discussion on traditional intellectual property, we know that patents and copyrights can help reduce the transaction costs of selling and licensing innovations and original works. Therefore, this transaction cost argument could also provide a rationale for the "data producer's right."[^(34)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0302) To answer this question, we must analyze from an economic perspective whether the trading and licensing markets for data suffer from market failures that hinder market efficiency. Empirically, although these markets are expanding rapidly, they remain underdeveloped and fragmented, which warrants further attention. From this perspective, the *Data Economy SWD* first conducting a survey on the awareness of data and data market sharing is undoubtedly appropriate. However, the problem is that although some initial empirical studies on data trading and data markets provide some insights into data trading issues, to date we do not have a comprehensive analysis of trading problems in data markets. Therefore, in the *Data Economy SWD*, only the current state of data markets and the extent of data sharing and trading are described, without analyzing the real trading problems and market failure problems in data markets, which is not surprising.[^(35)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0303) However, such analysis is necessary for formulating policy measures that can make data markets more effective.
