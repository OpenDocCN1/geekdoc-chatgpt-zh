
Another reason for seeking contractual protection of user rights is the protection of intellectual property rights, particularly copyright and related rights. Clearly, a clear contractual intent provides legal certainty regarding the scope of data use. However, in the absence of contractual intent, users may still use data content. For instance, a user's public posting of data content online may constitute the right holder's implied consent to the use of such content.[^(40)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0794) Furthermore, the legitimacy of the right to use data content may also derive from exceptions and limitations to copyright control. It should be noted that, pursuant to Article 5(1) of the *Computer Programs Directive*,[^(41)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0795) a lawful acquirer of a program is entitled to use the program in accordance with its intended purpose. Therefore, purchasers of devices with embedded software may use that software without concluding a contract with the software developer. This means that the right of use is limited by "specific contractual terms"[^(42)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0796) and acts controlled by the copyright holder (reproduction or adaptation of the computer program and any form of distribution to the public). Consequently, even where the user has not waived core rights, software producers have some leeway to restrict the use of the software, which is particularly common in legitimate data reproduction and error correction.[^(43)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0797) Software producers do not limit their restriction rights to this alone. For example, car manufacturer Tesla attempted to restrict customers' commercial use of Tesla's Full Self-Driving capability: "Please note that users may use Tesla's Full Self-Driving capability for self-driving or to provide ridesharing for family and friends, but paid services may only be offered via the Tesla Network."[^(44)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0798)

## 2. Claims for Damages for "Defective" Content

Contract law plays a decisive role in determining the rights and obligations of the parties. Generally, if a contract has been concluded, the data content must conform to certain qualities reasonably expected by the recipient of the data content.[^(45)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0799) Given the different target markets and product definitions of various products or services, determining their reasonably expected qualities is not an easy task.[^(46)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0800) Of course, there is reason to expect that data content or services will function as promised (e.g., data stored on the provider's server can be retrieved), and security vulnerabilities should be classified as defects in the data content.[^(47)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0801) However, considering that in gratuitous contracts (donation, loan for use, gratuitous deposit, gratuitous mandate) and their contract law rules, the standard of liability is appropriately reduced because one party has no obligation to pay consideration.[^(48)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0802) The European Commission emphasized this point in its proposal for a Directive on the supply of digital content and noted that in certain circumstances data exchange can be considered consideration.[^(49)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0803)

In practice, the only significant contractual remedy is the right of the other party to claim damages in the event of non-performance or improper performance by one party. This is because requiring a contractual party to perform a promise made without economic consideration is overly burdensome.[^(50)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0804) If data content or services fail to meet user expectations, the user simply chooses to transact with another data provider. In this situation, a remedy through price reduction is obviously ineffective. For example, where a customer's data can no longer be retrieved from a cloud hosting provider; where a service error during an auction leads to an unduly low auction price; or where a software security vulnerability causes damage to the user's data environment—in such cases, granting the recipient of defective data content or services a right to claim damages is clearly more effective. Data content or service providers attempt to eliminate this risk by inserting limitation of liability clauses in their standard terms. The next question to be resolved is whether these limitation clauses are unfair and void.[^(51)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0805)

If there is no contractual consensus between the parties, data product or service providers may be liable under tort law to compensate users for damage suffered due to defects in data content. Given the provider's commercial purpose and the potential risks posed by the product or service to consumer interests, the mere fact that data content is provided without consideration cannot negate or mitigate its duty of care. Nevertheless, defining the scope of such a duty of care is not easy.[^(52)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0806) For example, under German law, data content provided by a provider must meet the user's reasonable expectations of data security; this duty of care stems from producer liability. However, in certain cases, a software producer may fulfill its duty of care merely by issuing a warning not to use the data content (rather than providing an update).[^(53)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0807)

Moreover, there are differing views among the legal orders of various Member States as to whether negligent acts or omissions give rise to liability for pure economic loss or whether non-material damage caused by tort falls within the scope of damages. As long as the damage is direct, certain, and prescribed by law, it should be compensated, but this is not the case under German tort law and English tort law. Under German law, the loss of data stored on a user's personal computer can only be considered an infringement of property rights when the data service provider has an obligation to monitor the user's data content and warn of its potential dangers.[^(54)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0808) However, German tort law lacks provisions for bringing an action for pure economic loss caused by negligent acts or omissions. Under English law, compensation for pure economic loss resulting from negligent tort can only be obtained where there is a special relationship between the claimant and the defendant whereby the defendant owes a duty, or where the claimant reasonably relied on the defendant's professional skill, resulting in damage.[^(55)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0809) This rule applies primarily to negligence; whether it can be extended to the context of data content is debatable (although we believe there is a special close relationship between software producers who regularly update their software and users of devices on which that software is installed). Finally, the EU *Product Liability Directive*[^(56)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0810) only addresses damages for death, personal injury, and property damage—but for data access, the primary issue remains whether data content can be subject to the provisions of the Product Liability Directive.[^(57)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0811)

Therefore, the existence of a contract may play an important role in damages, while the scope of liability of data content or service providers largely depends on the qualification of the data content (functioning, updates, etc.). Furthermore, in situations where the customer provides no economic consideration, the law may correspondingly impose a lighter liability on the provider.

### (IV) Confidentiality

Finally, if data product or service providers are able to access personal data or other economically valuable data generated by users, confidentiality becomes a significant issue. Clearly, if a contract has been explicitly formed, confidentiality clauses can be stipulated therein. Even if the contract does not explicitly provide for confidentiality clauses, the law can supplement them through mandatory rules.[^(58)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0812) If no contractual relationship has been formed between the parties, recourse must be had to tort law.

Although tort law generally falls within the autonomous jurisdiction of Member States, there are two fundamental rules regarding confidentiality. Pursuant to Article 82 of the *General Data Protection Regulation* (GDPR), any person who has suffered damage[^(59)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0813) (material or non-material) as a result of an infringement of the Regulation has the right to receive compensation from the controller (or processor). This provision establishes a rule of presumed fault under fault-based liability. This means that the controller or processor bears the burden of proving that they are in no way responsible for the event giving rise to the damage.[^(60)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0814)

Non-personal data with economic value is governed by the *Trade Secrets Directive*[^(61)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0815) (effective June 9, 2018). Under Article 4 of the Directive, Member States must take appropriate measures to protect trade secrets against unlawful acquisition, use, and disclosure. Information meeting the following conditions is considered a trade secret:

1.  The information is secret, in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question;
2.  It has commercial value because it is secret;
3.  It has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret.[^(62)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0816)

However, issues remain to be resolved in this data access relationship: first, if a party has no contractual relationship with the trade secret holder but can access this data, can the data still be considered a trade secret? For example, if a car manufacturer can obtain global positioning system data from cars owned by a car rental or transport company, can this data (from which customers and operational capacity can be inferred) still be considered a trade secret? The author believes that as long as the trade secret holder does not change the mode of data access and takes reasonable measures to protect its acquisition and use in all other respects, it can be considered a trade secret.

If the information qualifies as a trade secret, it is protected against unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure in any manner, and against disclosure in breach of a confidentiality agreement or any other duty of confidentiality.[^(63)](#chapter5_2.xhtml#wz_01_0817) The author believes that even if the acquisition of information by a software or hardware producer is considered lawful (to comply with the product's purpose) and there is no confidentiality clause in the contract, at least one party has an obligation not to disclose the trade secret, because the fact that the trade secret holder granted data access to the other party creates a close relationship between them.

In addition to the aforementioned EU provisions, secret information is also protected by the tort laws of Member States, such as Section 823 of the *German Civil Code (BGB)* and related legislation governing interference with established and ongoing business operations, misuse of personal data, and breach of trust.

However, these interpretations do not mean that every single piece of private information of a user of a service or device is protected by tort law. Compared to resorting to tort law, confidentiality clauses in a contract are certainly more effective, even if these clauses are implied.

## VI. Conclusion

In this article, the author proposes that under traditional contract law principles, data access is not necessarily associated with the existence of a contract. Because it is often difficult to determine whether there is a consensus between the parties to create specific legal effects. Given that parties often do not consider whether their relationship produces binding effects, it is necessary to resort to objective standards. However, as discussed in Part III, determining objective standards is far from easy. In the author's view, in many of the situations listed in Part III, there is a lack of consensus to create legal effects. Nevertheless, the user of a device or the recipient of a service should accept or at least tolerate the provider or producer accessing data generated during use. This phenomenon has led to an academic trend of expanding the concept of contract by presuming implied consent and lowering the requirements for the intent to create legal relations.

However, such conceptual expansion is only meaningful if contract law actually addresses the problems arising from this relationship defined as a "contract" by consensus. In Part IV, the author argues that contract law does indeed solve some problems arising from data access, particularly regarding update obligations and confidentiality obligations. Nevertheless, the denial of contract law's default rules must rely on imprecise reasonableness standards in many respects, while in other cases (such as who gets the opportunity to access data) they are completely useless.

Having clarified this, the author believes that searching for a dividing line between contract and pure social agreement in this context does not aid the application of law in this situation. Instead of extending the traditional concept of contract or attempting to determine this elusive boundary between the two, it is better to seek a new concept: a factual relationship defined by tolerated data access. Such data access may be granted based on the user's autonomous decision, or the user of the device or service may have waived existing access rights. In either case, this tolerated data access creates a certain close relationship between the parties, giving rise to special duties of care. When we consider creating new rules for the data age, we should not limit ourselves to contract law. On the contrary, we should accept the existence of a special relationship arising from tolerated data access, rather than having to establish the existence of a contract. Rules for the data age need to emphasize this factual relationship.
