
This sufficiently demonstrates that, in a broader context, as a matter of course, consideration should also be given in the future to transforming certain optional exceptions and limitations in the InfoSoc Directive into mandatory ones. Although the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) has played an active role and has emphasized the need for the consistent application of optional copyright exceptions in Member States,[^(53)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0087) the exceptions to copyright and related rights can only be truly effectively harmonized where Member States are compelled to implement them. It warrants further analysis whether Art. 6(4) of the InfoSoc Directive, which also applies to the sui generis database right, provides for the possibility of safeguarding permitted uses where rightholders use technological measures to restrict acts permitted by the Directive. In this regard, further research should determine the additional need to revise copyright law concerning the enforceability of certain fundamental copyright exceptions related to de facto exclusivity (e.g., based on the establishment of effective TPMs[^(54)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0088)). In this respect, it is necessary to consider establishing non-waivable, irrevocable, sector-specific user rights at the EU level in the future.[^(55)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0089)

Another future research challenge is to further develop and clarify the role of fundamental rights, particularly that of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, in the area of copyright exceptions, which may provide further illustration for the revision of exclusive rights and exceptions to general copyright law (as well as exceptions in the Trade Secrets Directive). The CJEU's case law in this area is not entirely satisfactory: on the one hand, the Court increasingly considers the balancing of fundamental rights; on the other hand, in some cases, the fundamental approach still seems somewhat lagging and lacking in rigor, thereby failing to achieve the goal of methodologically "importing" fundamental rights in a normative manner so that they unfold concrete effect in private law disputes.[^(56)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0090)

### (IV) Term of Protection

Research indicates that, compared to the typical exploitation period in the field of dynamic electronic databases, the term of protection of the sui generis database right of fifteen years (extendable upon any substantial update, potentially leading to perpetual protection[^(57)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0091)) is clearly too long.[^(58)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0092) Therefore, when revising the Database Directive, the term of protection should be strictly assessed and aligned with the actual economic needs of this sector, which should lead to a significant reduction in the term of protection. Regarding the scope of protection of continuously updated databases and the pressing issue of their perpetual protection, the author has proposed a differentiated solution based on a correct interpretation of current law, namely defining the updated database by the "substantial part," thereby limiting and reducing the scope of protection for continuously updated databases.[^(59)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0093)

Another option might lie in considering whether the sui generis database right should be transformed into an appropriate registered industrial property right.[^(60)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0094) In this case, registration fees should increase significantly over time, making the cost of extending exclusive protection to the maximum term increasingly high; consequently, only those rightholders seeking to recoup investments in databases of exceptionally high economic value would seek such protection.

### (V) Reciprocity in the International Context

In international big data business models, a major defect of the existing sui generis database right stems from the so-called reciprocity clause (Art. 11(3) of the Database Directive). Under the reciprocity clause, whether special protection is granted to third-country database makers or rightholders depends on bilateral agreements concluded by the EU Council; such agreements can only be concluded if the condition is met that "the third country provides similar protection for databases produced by nationals or residents of the EU" (Recital 56 of the Database Directive).

Initially, this reciprocity condition aimed to exert political pressure on third countries to make the sui generis right model for database protection a global paradigm. However, this plan has largely failed. Particularly in the US, after several failed legislative attempts, the latest legislative agenda increasingly favors a more flexible, unfair competition-oriented protection regime, without establishing a sui generis protection regime similar to the EU's.

In order to effectively perform an incentive function and provide structural support for the formation of data usage contracts based on data access mechanisms in the international environment, the reciprocity clause should be abolished, and third-country nationals should be granted protection equal to that of EU nationals and residents. Naturally, nevertheless, if the sui generis database right can be maintained and substantially modified in the future to sufficiently meet the regulatory needs of the European data economy, a renewed commitment to working towards a possible international protection agreement should be made.

### (VI) The Problem of Overlapping Protection

Finally, some technically more complex, doctrinal legal issues must be discussed, which have already caused problems in the practice of the sui generis database right and may cause even more in the future. This mainly concerns the issue of overlapping protection regimes and the relationship with other areas of law.

First is the issue of "internal" overlapping protection within intellectual property law. Under the Database Directive, copyright protection for the original structure of a database (selection and arrangement of contents) overlaps with the investment-protecting sui generis right. In fact, compared to the sui generis database right, database copyright may play a more prominent role in shaping the structure of the data economy than currently anticipated. This is because copyright may apply to the original structure, or even certain metadata, which effectively organize pre-existing data pools in specific situations. However, the relevance of harmonized EU copyright in this respect remains rather unclear. This is because the CJEU recently tends to require actual creative freedom as a prerequisite for copyright protection, while the mere expression of skill and labor in a field is insufficient.[^(61)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0095) Against this background, it is not entirely clear what role copyright law can play in protecting metadata and other structural elements of databases, but it will in any case protect certain structures in this field if the conditions for sufficient room for personal creation are met. However, the resulting general copyright protection for certain database structures lacks specific provisions on reverse engineering and other safeguards for free competition and data interoperability, such as those embodied in the Computer Programs Directive (Art. 6) for the copyright protection of computer programs. This can indeed lead to interoperability problems and become an obstacle to opening up new markets for value-added database use (i.e., third parties requesting the use of a specific data selection or elements). The problem is even more acute where third parties modify a specific original structure for a new use, because even if explicit exceptions exist for the contained elements, the exclusive right of adaptation of the database structure may be infringed (such a right exists in many Member States, but is not explicit in EU copyright law[^(62)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0096)). Therefore, by analogy with the relevant provisions in the Computer Programs Directive, safeguards for reverse engineering and data interoperability should at least be considered as supplements to both database copyright and the sui generis database right. This is also in line with the approach of the recent Trade Secrets Directive, which explicitly defines reverse engineering and lists it as a permitted act (Art. 3(1)(b) of the Trade Secrets Directive). If these subtle modifications are made, database copyright can indeed play a beneficial role in data markets, because, ideally, if such metadata is protected by copyright, it can at least support the disclosure of certain metadata on the source, quality, and methodology of already published datasets.

Second is the issue of "external" overlapping protection outside intellectual property law. Alternative protection mechanisms create typical overlapping protection effects, such as unfair competition law in many Member States (the influential French "free-riding" theory or the provisions of Sec. 4(3) of the German Act against Unfair Competition), which threaten the limitations and exceptions to the sui generis database right. In fact, this "overlapping" protection[^(63)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0097) should be pre-emptively regulated, because the application of other laws makes no sense in protecting investments against unfair imitation, since intellectual property rights themselves protect a broadly defined form of investment.[^(64)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0098)

Currently, under Art. 13 of the Database Directive, unfair competition clauses may be applied without prejudice to the other provisions of the Directive providing protection. The statement that the application of unfair competition clauses is not excluded is not improper per se, but it may be misinterpreted to mean that national laws are free to provide additional protection for data compilations even where the basic requirements for sui generis database right protection are not met. For example, where investment in a database compilation is insignificant, or where the extraction or re-utilization of data does not constitute an infringement under Art. 7(2) of the Database Directive, sui generis protection is simply not available. However, granting such an additional, flexible, unpredictable "safety net" protection in unfair competition case law clearly conflicts with the objectives of the Directive, considering that its purpose is not only to ensure a minimum level of protection but also to achieve a workable degree of harmonization of Member States' laws in this area and to guarantee that the use of compiled data remains free where the requirements of Art. 7 are not met.[^(65)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0099) Clearly, for the widely discussed issue of data access in existing and future European data economy infrastructures, the problems caused by unfair competition clauses urgently need to be resolved.

Therefore, it is very important to establish the priority of the database right over other laws protecting investments, absent other factors. Consequently, additional protection under unfair competition law for the copying of databases should only be considered where the unfair competition action is based on genuine additional factors (such as genuine counterfeiting or bad faith misappropriation of data through breach of confidence), and these factors have not already been encompassed by the EU legislator in the specific provisions of the Directive and its balance of interests. In short, while the act of re-utilization or extraction of data should not receive additional protection from unfair competition law, the risk of confusion of data sources or other factors may still trigger unfair competition protection.[^(66)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0100) Regarding the latter, the unfair competition law paths against counterfeiting, consumer confusion, and misrepresentation of data retain their value, as this is reflected not only in the protection of individual users but can also institutionally serve as a baseline safeguarding the authenticity and reliability of datasets on the market. In summary, a corresponding amendment to the Directive should incorporate explicit wording reflecting the solution of the priority of the sui generis right, thereby regulating the overlapping protection issue between this Directive and Member States' unfair competition laws.

Regarding personal data, privacy protection, and other legal areas, questions may arise concerning their exact relationship with the sui generis database right and its limitations. The relationship of these legal areas with the Database Directive should adhere to the principle of subsidiarity. This means that as long as the interests involved have been comprehensively balanced by the EU legislator within the framework of the Database Directive, the application of other areas of law must not contradict or conflict with the protection conditions, scope, and limitations stipulated in the Database Directive. Naturally, legislation protecting other rights and interests should not be affected by this Directive. A necessary conclusion, therefore, is that individuals must retain the right to protect their personal data—although in the near future, how to coordinate the intellectual property protection and access regime for machine-generated data with the personal data protection regime will undoubtedly attract more attention.[^(67)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0101)

However, personal rights and interests cannot justify the existence of a genuine "data ownership" outside data and privacy protection laws, because for the "incidental" other data generated by them, no possibility has been found to effectively remedy market failure through additional intellectual property protection.[^(68)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0102) On the contrary, in this respect, the existing sui generis right should be seen as a targeted, conclusive path that should be modified and perfected before considering any additional protection mechanisms.

### III. Conclusion

Since the implementation of the Database Directive in EU Member States, database copyright, and particularly the sui generis database right, have been applied to a variety of big data usage scenarios. It is conceivable that the CJEU and Member State courts will apply the sui generis database right to machine-generated data. In the author's view, this fact has not yet received sufficient attention in academic and policy discussions on the future regulation of the European data economy.

The significant role of the sui generis database right (and other legal instruments, such as the protection offered by database copyright and the new Trade Secrets Directive) brings opportunities, challenges, and dangers to current and future European and global data markets. It is undeniable that the design of the sui generis right offers a targeted and proportionate opportunity for the existing European intellectual property system to adapt to the challenges of the data economy. Since, to date, no identifiable market failure risk in data markets can be effectively remedied by a "data ownership" or similar exclusive rights path,[^(69)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0103) rather than further evaluating such new rights instruments, attention should be directed towards revising EU law on general copyright, database copyright, the sui generis database right, and trade secrets, in order to improve their functioning to build current and future data markets.

Regarding the sui generis database right and other related issues, there is a clear need for necessary revisions and further initiatives, including the provision of model contracts and best practices for data transactions at the European level, non-mandatory contract law rules as a safety net to reduce transaction costs, and mandatory regulation of unfair contract terms. Furthermore, the provisions in the Database Directive on the rights of the "lawful user" of a database (and those in the Computer Programs Directive on computer programs) could potentially be expanded: if such a purpose-limited data access right is deemed justified in certain sectors, it may be possible in the future to form the core of an irrevocable data access right in specific sectors, working in synergy with the provisions on the lawful user's rights. Moreover, from a more general perspective, carefully designed compulsory licenses are worth attempting for uses that promote competition but conflict with the exclusive rights of database makers. In fact, the European Commission's first proposal for the Database Directive even contained the basis for a more comprehensive compulsory licensing regime, which should equally be an object of further research. Furthermore, it is beyond doubt that the exceptions to the sui generis database right must be modified, at least to align them with the exception scenarios for general copyright in EU law. Other necessary adjustments include the fact that the fifteen-year term of the sui generis database right seems too long, and its relationship with other legal instruments should be more clearly defined—this includes not only the overlapping protection issues between the sui generis right and Member States' unfair competition laws and the Trade Secrets Directive, but also the relationship between the sui generis right and privacy and personal data protection. Therefore, issues such as data portability and interoperability, which have already been resolved in some more specific protection regimes, should also be considered when revising the Directive. In summary: all the aforementioned issues should be taken into account when evaluating and revising the Database Directive. This paper also attempts to propose targeted solutions for these issues and other technical issues awaiting clarification.

Such a "technical" revision of the Database Directive, while profound and highly detailed, may have a more fundamental alternative: transforming the sui generis database right into a registered industrial property right with a shorter term, where registration and renewal fees increase significantly over time until the maximum term is reached. However, it is still unclear whether the possibilities involved in this change (i.e., the opportunity to narrow the scope of the sui generis right to those cases essentially requiring the correction of market failure and the reduction of the undeniable information problems in existing database markets) truly outweigh the undeniable risks of such a fundamental change (i.e., additional rent-seeking and public choice effects, which may ultimately even increase the inefficiencies caused by the existing sui generis right and lead to additional transaction and administrative costs). Therefore, this option deserves further research, but at the same time, it is obviously inadvisable to assert its utility before this option has been further considered in policy.

The inefficiency problems of the sui generis database right mentioned above persist at least in its current legal form. Furthermore, potential hold-up dangers, particularly in cases of multiple co-ownership, the dangers of cancellation of redemption rights and other leveraging in secondary markets, increased administrative and transaction costs, and many other issues—although sometimes possibly exaggerated (particularly, the CJEU's case law in the British Horseracing case[^(70)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0104) has at least resolved some of the most pressing issues in single-source data situations)—cannot be denied. Therefore, a thorough analysis of the implementation and application status of the Database Directive should naturally balance these inefficiencies against the relatively limited cases and situations where the sui generis database right indeed helps correct sector-specific market failures, with the protection of geographic data against competitors' systems and systematic extraction being a case in point.[^(71)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0105) Based on the results of this assessment, we can also consider completely abolishing the sui generis database right protection regime. The author has always been reluctant to propose this most radical solution[^(72)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0106) and remains hesitant. The entire paper essentially attempts to propose the necessary changes to make the sui generis database right ultimately succeed in the context of the European data economy. However, if these changes are not considered and made, the current sui generis database right architecture will have no place in the next legislative evaluation for providing an effective legal framework for the European data economy.[^(73)](#chapter2.xhtml#wz_01_0107)
