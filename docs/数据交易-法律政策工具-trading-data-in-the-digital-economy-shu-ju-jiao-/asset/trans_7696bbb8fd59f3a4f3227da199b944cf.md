
The "Working Document" considers that a new data producer's right could be defined as a property right (right *in rem*), granting the owner the exclusive right to use and authorize the use of data.[^(12)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0119) Alternatively, it could be constructed as a defense right following the approach of the Trade Secrets Directive—i.e., a right to seek remedies against certain unauthorized uses of data. If it is a property right, ownership would be determined by the investor responsible for creating the data, and co-ownership could be established. If the defense right path is chosen, the de facto holder lawfully in possession of the data would be entitled to remedies. Exceptions to any data producer's right must be acknowledged; the Commission suggests exceptions primarily covering four scenarios: first, manufacturers of machinery or devices needing to use data to improve product design; second, cases where access to certain data is necessary for reasons of public interest; third, public authorities may have a legitimate interest in accessing data; fourth, creating exceptions to allow scientists conducting publicly funded research to access and use privately held protected data. The Commission also proposes that, where anti-competitive behavior may arise, non-personal, commercially held data should be disclosed to a certain extent, potentially achieved by establishing a licensing obligation.[^(13)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0120)

## III. Overview of the EU Trade Secrets Directive

The Trade Secrets Directive aims to address legal divergences among Member States regarding the protection of trade secrets. Specific changes include the legal mechanisms for protecting trade secrets, the definition of trade secrets, whether trade secrets are categorized as intellectual property rights, available remedies, and procedural mechanisms available to protect confidentiality during litigation. Consequently, the Directive harmonizes the protection of trade secrets in both substantive and procedural law.[^(14)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0121) According to Recitals 1–4 and 8 of the Directive, the positive significance of harmonizing EU legislation includes incentivizing innovation, cross-border innovation and knowledge sharing, and enhancing the EU's commercial competitiveness. In terms of protecting investment and encouraging knowledge or data sharing, these theories align with those articulated by the Commission regarding the "data economy."

The following summarizes the Directive's efforts and effectiveness in harmonizing substantive law. Article 2 defines a trade secret as secret information—meaning the information is secret, has commercial value, and has been subject to reasonable steps to protect its secrecy. Considering it reflects the requirements of Article 39 of the TRIPS Agreement, this is a relatively uncontroversial definition. Misappropriation of trade secrets refers to the unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure of trade secrets and infringing goods. Without the consent of the trade secret holder,[^(15)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0122) the acquisition, misappropriation, or copying of any material containing the trade secret, or acquisition by any other conduct contrary to honest commercial practices, constitutes unlawful acquisition.[^(16)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0123) Unlawful use or disclosure of trade secrets refers, on the one hand, to the acquisition, use, or disclosure of a trade secret without the consent of the trade secret holder; on the other hand, it refers to the acquisition and use of a trade secret in breach of a contractual or other duty not to disclose or to limit the use of the trade secret.[^(17)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0124) If a third party knew or should have known that the trade secret was obtained directly or indirectly from another person who unlawfully used or disclosed it, their acquisition, use, and disclosure are also defined as unlawful acts.[^(18)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0125) Article 4(5) prohibits the trading of infringing goods, which, under Article 2(4), are goods whose "design, characteristics, functioning, manufacturing process, or marketing significantly benefit from the trade secret unlawfully acquired, used, or disclosed."

However, Article 3 stipulates that the acquisition of trade secrets is lawful in certain circumstances, particularly independent discovery or creation and reverse engineering. Furthermore, Article 5 on "exceptions" provides that in certain cases—for example, based on freedom of expression or to reveal misconduct, wrongdoing, or illegal activities in the general public interest—the trade secret holder is not entitled to legal remedies for the acquisition, use, or disclosure of trade secrets. These provisions are seen as particularly important protections for whistleblowers and journalists.[^(19)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0126)

The Directive's procedural provisions are mainly embodied in Article 9, which aims to resolve a paradox: a trade secret can be protected if it is secret and valuable, yet court proceedings are inevitably open and public. Therefore, a trade secret holder attempting to litigate would inevitably destroy the secrecy of the trade secret by disclosing it in court proceedings. Thus, Article 9 obliges Member States to ensure confidentiality is maintained in court proceedings, while paying full regard to transparency and public justice. Article 8 sets a six-year limitation period for bringing actions, though Member States may determine the starting point themselves. Finally, Articles 6–7 and 10–15 prescribe the remedies that must be available where misappropriation is established. These measures involve provisional and final measures, damages, and publication of judicial decisions. An important feature is that courts must assess the proportionality of the remedies granted.

The above obligations to protect trade secrets are minimum standards; therefore, Member States may provide broader protection provided certain substantive and procedural safeguards are met.[^(20)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0127) While these minimum standards resonate with unfair competition law, they are drafted neutrally enough to give Member States some flexibility in choosing the civil law mechanisms to implement the obligations.

Crucially, the Trade Secrets Directive excludes the creation of a property right. This is reflected in Recital 10: "In order to foster innovation and competition, this Directive should not create any exclusive right in respect of know-how or information protected as a trade secret." Moreover, Recital 1 describes trade secrets as a "complement or alternative to intellectual property rights," not an independent IP right itself. Therefore, the EU Enforcement Directive does not apply to trade secrets; Recital 28 states that, where its scope overlaps with the Enforcement Directive, the Trade Secrets Directive is *lex specialis*.[^(21)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0128) The policy choice to avoid a propertarian approach to trade secret protection is a useful path; aside from other concerns regarding the proposed data producer's right, consideration should be given to whether a propertarian approach would be appropriate if such a right were to be pursued.

## IV. Relevance of the Trade Secrets Directive to the Data Economy

This section explores the Directive's relevance to the data economy by examining the breadth of the trade secret concept, the category of trade secret holders, and the scope of protection.

### (1) Definition of Trade Secrets

Article 2(1) defines a "trade secret" as information meeting the following three conditions:

1.  The information is secret, in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question, nor does it constitute public knowledge, skills, or experience acquired by ordinary workers in the course of their work;
2.  It has commercial value because it is secret;
3.  It has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret.

The above provisions broadly define what may constitute a trade secret. Recital 14 emphasizes the importance of creating a "homogeneous definition of trade secrets without restricting the subject matter eligible for protection against misappropriation." The definition is therefore sufficiently broad to cover requests for protection of commercially valuable private information.[^(22)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0129) In the context of the (big) data economy, the question is whether individual data (such as personal data), datasets (whether raw or processed), or data analytics techniques fall within this definition.

Regarding whether personal data constitutes a trade secret, the Commission expresses doubt: personal data generated by interconnected machines and devices lacks commercial value and is therefore insufficient to constitute a "trade secret."[^(23)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0130) According to Recital 14, commercial value should be understood to include actual or potential commercial value. Furthermore, this recital links the commercial value of know-how or information to the prejudice caused to the trade secret holder by unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure (e.g., undermining "scientific and technical potential, commercial or financial interests, strategic position, or competitive ability"). This is actually a broad concept of commercial value, as it encompasses potential value and adopts a harm-based approach to defining value, where prejudice is easily established without a quantifiable threshold.[^(24)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0131) Therefore, while Recital 8 excludes "trivial information," raw data may possess commercial value, albeit low.[^(25)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0132) Although the concept of commercial value is overly broad, protecting personal data as trade secrets is not within the Directive's objectives. Reasons include: first, Article 2(1)(a) refers to the secrecy of information existing "as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components," indicating the object of protection is not individual data points. Second, personal data protection is inconsistent with the Directive's rationale, as it is difficult to envision individual personal data serving as a basis for innovation, knowledge generation, or sharing.

However, data or datasets can relatively easily satisfy the broad definition of commercial value. The Commission acknowledges the existence of non-personal data markets related to data in fields such as financial or commodity markets, credit scoring, weather, and automotive mobility.[^(26)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0133) Geolocation data is also highly valuable and can be utilized in various ways.[^(27)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0134) Where specific markets for such diverse data exist, it should be directly evident that unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure of datasets would undermine the trade secret holder's business or commercial interests or damage their competitiveness. Even where data markets do not yet exist, potential commercial value may be established.

The other two requirements for data or datasets to obtain trade secret protection are secrecy and the taking of reasonable steps to maintain secrecy. Whether data meets the secrecy requirement depends on whether it is viewed as open-source or closed-source, and to some extent on its type. For datasets in the open data movement, they obviously cannot be considered secret.[^(28)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0135) However, other datasets may not be publicly accessible; in such cases, the question is whether the data is not "generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question" or "public knowledge, skills, or experience acquired by ordinary workers in the course of their work." This is difficult to predict, as it largely depends on the type of dataset involved, and in the big data economy, volume, variety, and velocity are typical characteristics of data.[^(29)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0136) Consequently, the requirement to have taken reasonable steps to maintain secrecy has an inherent degree of vagueness—whether it should be subjectively assessed based on the specific trade secret holder's circumstances or objectively judged based on standard protective measures adopted in the specific industry.[^(30)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0137) Even so, we might expect certain practices to satisfy the "reasonable steps" requirement, such as restrictions on data access levels, use of technical protection measures, and prudent internal data handling.

An implicit issue in the definition of trade secrets is the need to identify the information. Indeed, identifying the trade secret is an implicit requirement when assessing unauthorized use and ensuring remedies (e.g., injunctive relief) have a sufficiently certain scope.[^(31)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0138) However, in "big data" and "Internet of Things" societies, the volume of collected and analyzed data exhibits characteristics of large volume, variety, and high velocity; in some cases, delineating the boundaries of a dataset may be challenging.

Next are data analytics techniques, usually implemented via software, so they seemingly meet the definition of "trade secrets" without issue.[^(32)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0139) However, it is worth noting that some data analytics techniques are open source, such as the Apache Hadoop or Apache Spark systems; in these cases, the secrecy requirement and the steps to maintain secrecy cannot be met.

In summary, it is argued that the definition of trade secrets is flexible enough to encompass datasets and data analytics techniques, but essentially does not extend to personal data.[^(33)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0140) However, for datasets and data analytics techniques, whether the secrecy requirement and reasonable secrecy steps are met will depend on the specific circumstances. Despite this uncertainty, it can be said that the protection method prescribed by the Trade Secrets Directive raises fewer problems than the proposed data producer's right. First, the Commission barely mentions how to delineate the concept of "data" as the object of protection, except to state that the right should target the syntactic level (i.e., data or code), not the semantic level (i.e., ideas or compilations of information).[^(34)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0141) This is Professor Zech's view, who suggests the object of the right should be "machine-readable compilations of information, defined solely by their representative characters (bits), irrespective of their content (data delineated at the syntactic level)."[^(35)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0142) He further suggests there might be a threshold for data created "by automated measurement processes, intellectual activity, or mere computing power," and that intellectual creation could be excluded.[^(36)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0143) However, this seemingly simple definition may create great uncertainty. Would it extend to personal data, datasets, and data analytics techniques?[^(37)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0144) Moreover, as Professor Kerber points out, "since data is a compilation of information, it is difficult to draw a clear line between the separate protection of the compilation (syntactic level) and the content of the information itself (semantic level)."[^(38)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0145) For example, in data analytics, the focus is on searching for links, patterns, or "new meanings" in information at the semantic level by searching at the syntactic level.[^(39)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0146) Therefore, distinguishing between syntax and semantics seems artificial. Conversely, this could lead to a *de facto* exclusive right over information at the semantic level, and issues of overlap and conflict with existing IP rights.[^(40)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0147)

### (2) Trade Secret Holders

Under Article 2(2) of the Trade Secrets Directive, a trade secret holder is "any natural or legal person lawfully controlling a trade secret." This definition contains inherent uncertainties; it is currently unclear whether "persons lawfully controlling a trade secret" extends to licensees, joint venture participants, persons commissioned to create information, reverse engineers, or other persons to whom the trade secret has been disclosed with consent.[^(41)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0148) In the data economy context, other uncertainties arise. For example, taking a dataset generated by sensors embedded in a device or machine: who "lawfully" controls the data in this case? The manufacturer of the device or machine containing the sensor? The owner? Or the user of the device or machine? These questions indeed mirror similar uncertainties arising under the Commission's proposed data right.[^(42)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0149) For data analytics techniques, identifying the creator of the software may be more direct, but here the general uncertainties apply—e.g., whether a licensee is a trade secret holder or a person lawfully reverse engineering the data analytics software. The lack of clarity in Article 2(2) is not a reason to avoid trade secret protection, but highlights a broader point: this unified concept needs clarification at the EU level.

### (3) Scope of Protection

Article 4 of the Trade Secrets Directive requires Member States to ensure trade secret holders can seek remedies against unlawful acquisition, use, or disclosure. Equally important, certain acts are deemed lawful (Article 3), and there are "exceptions" (Article 5). Critics argue trade secret protection is unsuited to the data economy because it merely constitutes a "further legal reinforcement of factual exclusivity."[^(43)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0150) In other words, it applies only to acts of misappropriation and does not create an exclusive right over the trade secret itself. Although no property right is created, the protection afforded by the Directive remains quite robust; trade secret holders can utilize various enforcement mechanisms. In some cases, third parties may be involved, and in relation to infringing goods, protection may even be excessive (broader than patent law), as the definition of infringing goods does not require establishing a factual link between the goods and the unauthorized use of the trade secret.[^(44)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0151) The protection provided by the Directive, while limited, remains highly significant; Professor Drexl terms it "better suited to serve the purposes of the data economy, because it focuses on the specific manner in which third parties acquire data, rather than providing protection against the use of data."[^(45)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0152)

Importantly, the Trade Secrets Directive specifies lawful acquisition, use, or disclosure, extending to independent discovery or creation and reverse engineering. However, in Professor Zech's view, this means protection for data is incomplete, especially considering data can be independently re-stored.[^(46)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0153) But legitimizing such acts is a crucial means of maintaining competition and enhancing innovation.[^(47)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0154) Indeed, we see this policy objective supported by various IP exceptions, such as reverse engineering exceptions in integrated circuits[^(48)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0155) and copyright-protected software,[^(49)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0156) and private use[^(50)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0157) and experimental use[^(51)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0158) exceptions in patent law, which facilitate reverse engineering to some extent.[^(52)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0159) The "exceptions" listed in Article 5 are not mandatory exceptions but rather exceptions to the availability of remedies in specific circumstances. The listed situations may have varying relevance to the data economy (freedom of expression, disclosure of wrongdoing in the public interest, disclosure by workers to their representatives); however, one might rely on Article 5(4) to understand the whole provision: "The purpose of this paragraph is to safeguard legitimate interests recognized by Union or Member State law."

## V. Conclusion

Although not perfectly suited to the data economy, we should not overlook the value of trade secret protection for datasets and data analytics techniques. Indeed, if the Commission's proposed data producer's right is based on liability (damages) or a so-called "defensive" basis, it would not add to the protection already provided by the Trade Secrets Directive. Of course, we should not rush to seek legislative intervention establishing an exclusive data right. As critics point out, the economic rationale for this right is unconvincing: there is simply no need to incentivize the production or collection of data, especially machine-generated data, nor is there a need for exclusive incentives to commercialize such data or ensure access permissions.[^(53)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0160) Moreover, copyright (in software) or trade secret protection may already provide incentives for creating data analytics techniques. Legislative introduction of an exclusive data right risks triggering serious competition problems, which could be highly detrimental to the development of the data economy.[^(54)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0161) Meanwhile, before exploring business models and other methods to ensure data access,[^(55)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0162) trade secret protection, alongside other existing legal tools (sui generis database rights, contract, and competition law), provides a solid foundation for data protection in the data economy.[^(56)](#chapter2_1.xhtml#wz_01_0163)
