
# Legal Dilemmas and Analysis of Data Property Rights

## I. Introduction

With the continuous growth of the "data-driven economy,"[¹](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0164) calls for the introduction of new data property rights are growing louder. In response to the needs of the automotive industry,[²](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0165) driven by some German lawyers and scholars,[³](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0166) the European Commission, in its 2017 report on "Building a European Data Economy," preliminarily proposed the idea of creating an EU-wide *erga omnes* "`data producer's right`" to protect industrial data.[⁴](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0167) Commissioner Günther Oettinger, a supporter of "`Dateneigentum`" (data ownership), previously led the Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology (DG CONNECT) before 2016. An op-ed published by Oettinger in the *Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung* revealed the views and powerful backing behind this revolutionary legal concept. Oettinger argued that data is the "**gold of the future**," particularly in the automotive sector, where modern sensor-equipped vehicles automatically generate and collect vast amounts of data, including traffic and road conditions, engine performance, etc.[⁵](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0168) These machine-generated sensor data hold immense value, for example, in the development of autonomous vehicles. However, Oettinger also pointed out that it is currently unclear who owns this data: the car manufacturer, the vehicle owner, or the sensor device producer? Or does it belong to no one? Oettinger believed that legal norms establishing data ownership need to be determined at the EU level.[⁶](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0169)

Clearly, the impetus for calls to protect industrial data lies in the fear that valuable European assets might be misappropriated by large US companies—a concern common in other recent policy initiatives. Previously, the shadow cast by Google's "misappropriation" of European news led the EU (following similar rules in Germany and Spain) to negotiate a proposal for a neighboring right for press publishers.[⁷](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0170) The EU's creation of the *sui generis* right for database makers in 1996 was similarly motivated by European apprehension regarding the dominance of the US database industry.[⁸](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0171)

Although the overall concept and economic rationale of the "data producer's right" envisioned by the European Commission are rather sketchy, this right might elevate the EU's protection of industrial data to a higher level compared to the database right (which remains heavily criticized and controversial). The *sui generis* database right protects data on a dual condition: first, that the data is arranged in a "database," and second, that the database is the product of "substantial investment," whereas the data producer's right would directly protect machine-generated data without any substantial preconditions.

This article argues that the establishment of such a comprehensive data property right would severely damage the existing European intellectual property (IP) law system, conflict with fundamental freedoms enshrined in the *European Convention on Human Rights* (ECHR) and the *EU Charter of Fundamental Rights*, distort competition and freedom of services within the EU, restrict academic freedom, and broadly undermine the prospects of big data in the European economy and society. In short, this approach is highly undesirable.

This article first briefly examines the background and rationale of the proposed data producer's right: Why is there a demand for property rights for industrial data? What are the subject matter and scope of the right? It then turns to the existing IP regime to investigate the extent to which these regimes can apply to data and speculates on how a data property right in the data sphere would affect these regimes. Subsequently, it analyzes the data property right in detail from the broader perspective of fundamental rights and freedoms, before summarizing the article's findings.

The creation of a data property right would undoubtedly impact other areas, particularly informational privacy (personal data protection), but the focus of this article is on IP law. Therefore, we will not examine whether data protection law already encompasses a property right in personal data.[⁹](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0172) Nor should we question whether the concept of private property in civil law can be extended (or has already been extended) to (recorded) industrial data, thereby providing alternative protection for data sets.[^(10)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0173) We should also avoid discussing other legal rules that might support "data property rights,"[^(11)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0174) such as criminal law[^(12)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0175) or trade secret law[^(13)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0176), and we will not discuss the "transaction" of personal data for services relevant to contract and consumer law, as these issues are rendered moot by the proposed `EU Digital Content Directive`.[^(14)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0177)

Finally, we must offer some general caveats. Although the European Commission has now raised the issue of "data ownership," which deserves serious discussion, the policy arguments supporting the introduction of such a right are not well-developed, and its contours are vague. Consequently, critiquing the data property right amounts to aiming at a moving target.

## II. What is the Rationale for Data Property?

The arguments put forward by proponents of a "data property" right can be summarized as follows. First, industrial data possesses enormous economic value. For instance, according to European Commission estimates, "**the total value of the EU data economy reached €257 billion in 2014, representing 1.85% of EU GDP, growing to €272 billion in 2015, representing 1.87% of EU GDP (a year-on-year increase of 5.6%). This estimate predicts that, if policies and legal frameworks for the data economy are formulated and implemented in a timely manner, its value will grow to €643 billion by 2020, representing 3.17% of total EU GDP**."[^(15)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0178) Research findings from the OECD and the European Commission provide similarly staggering figures.[^(16)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0179)

The rapid growth in the value of machine-generated data can be attributed to multiple factors: the rise of "**smart manufacturing**," where vast amounts of data are exchanged in real-time between machines and robots; the economic potential of big data "**mining**" (i.e., extracting information through complex large-scale data analysis);[^(17)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0180) in the Internet of Things era, machines can communicate and exchange data semi-autonomously with other machines, such as "**smart**" electricity meters capable of independently sending consumption data to energy companies, or refrigerators that automatically order milk and coffee from online supermarkets without human intervention. In Oettinger's op-ed, many examples stem from the automotive industry, where data has become not only a necessary input for car manufacturing and navigation systems but also an important output.[^(18)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0181) The competitive panic that Google's autonomous vehicles might surpass the European automotive industry has never dissipated.

Having argued that data possesses enormous and growing value, proponents further point out that current legal regimes—such as traditional property ownership based on civil law and existing IP regimes—cannot protect or adequately protect this data. Admittedly, non-property regimes like contractual protection and trade secret protection sometimes work, as do technical protection measures that create *de facto* ownership positions. However, these regimes do not create universally applicable rights; valuable data remains at risk of misappropriation, and markets for the primary use and trading (licensing) of raw data cannot be developed.[^(19)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0182) Therefore, a new type of property right capable of protecting industrial data is urgently needed. The European Commission preliminarily suggested in its report, as one of six policy options for "Building a European Data Economy," that "**this option aims to clarify the legal situation, give data producers more choices, enable users to use their data, and thereby help unlock the potential of machine-generated data**."[^(20)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0183)

Surprisingly, within these radical views, the economic rationale for data property rights is scarcely mentioned. According to the standard paradigm of economic analysis, the justification for creating such a new IP right manifests mainly in two aspects: (1) incentivizing data production through economic incentives to address public interest issues; (2) promoting the use and trading of data. Regarding the first argument, renowned German economist Professor Kerber argues that "**there is no evidence that incentives for producing and analyzing data are generally insufficient in the digital economy**."[^(21)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0184) In fact, large volumes of machine data are usually generated (almost) automatically as by-products of industrial production or services; it is unclear how a data property right, as a legal incentive, could play an enhancing role.[^(22)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0185)

As for the second argument, Professor Kerber states: "**Although the possibility of market failure in data trading and licensing markets cannot be ruled out, and from a practical perspective, data markets are still developing and require more regulation, so far data producers and holders seem to have sufficient potential for data commercialization.** The main potential market failure problem is that a monopsonist might resell data, but this seems solvable through contractual and technical restrictions, or through sales strategies for related data services."[^(23)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0186) Professor Kerber concludes: "**There are no convincing economic arguments for the introduction of this new IP right.**"[^(24)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0187) A more detailed recent study by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre expresses somewhat less skepticism.[^(25)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0188) This study indicates that legal uncertainty regarding data ownership may negatively affect the efficiency of data markets. However, this study equally concludes that there are currently no strong economic arguments to support the proposal for regulatory intervention.

However, this article will not conduct a further economic analysis of this possible data property right but will focus on its impact on the existing IP regime. To this end, we first need a preliminary understanding of what this right might entail. Based on the Annex "`Staff Working Paper`"[^(26)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0189) of the European Commission report, and drawing primarily on Professor Zech's research,[^(27)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0190) we speculate that the characteristics of the data producer's right are roughly as follows: It is an *erga omnes* right concerning "**non-personal or ownerless machine-generated data**," including the "**exclusive right to use the data and authorize its use. This includes a range of rights enforceable against any third party outside contractual relationships, thereby preventing unauthorized third parties from further using the data, as well as the right to claim damages for the unauthorized acquisition and use of data**."[^(28)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0191)

The European Commission has not yet finalized the issue of original ownership; Professor Zech believes the right should initially vest in the "**user who bears the economic responsibility for the device generating the data (the data producer)**."[^(29)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0192) As the Commission acknowledges, since the owners, users, and investors of the data-generating machines often differ, the allocation of rights in practice could pose a major challenge.[^(30)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0193)

Given its stated purpose, this right should be transferable.[^(31)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0194) Regarding the term of protection, the Commission made no stipulation, but Professor Zech believes "**short-term protection seems appropriate, because the use of data through analysis is relatively quick**."[^(32)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0195) The Commission excludes personal data from the scope of the new right, "**because the protection of the latter is a fundamental right concerning personal data, under which natural persons should have the right to control their own personal data**."[^(33)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0196) Otherwise, the Commission's proposal appears to cover all machine-generated data.

Regarding the specific subject matter of the right, Professor Zech proposes: "**The subject matter can be defined as machine-readable encoded information, defined solely by its representative characters (bits), irrespective of its content (distinguishing data syntactically).**"[^(34)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0197) This mode of definition is reflected in the intended scope of the right. "**The scope of protection specifically refers to the use of data through statistical analysis, but does not include the re-generation of the same data through independent measurement.**"[^(35)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0198) The Commission seems to affirm this definition, perhaps considering that this limitation can prevent undue information monopolies.[^(36)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0199) This article will examine the distinction between syntactic and semantic data in the next section.

In summary, whether in terms of its intended subject matter (data, intangibles) or its scope of protection (third-party copying and use of data), the proposed data producer's right would likely constitute an intellectual property right.

## III. Data in the Intellectual Property System

Before further analyzing the proposed data right and its potential impact on the IP system, we must first examine how the EU's current legal regime regulates machine-generated data. This section focuses on relevant IP legislation, without discussing other potentially relevant legal mechanisms such as tangible property rights, contracts, and trade secrets. While the focus of this section will be on the two IP regimes most closely related to the protection of data frameworks—copyright law and the *sui generis* database right—we will also briefly introduce the neighboring rights regime.

### (I) Copyright in Data

Does data enjoy copyright protection? The textbook answer is negative. US Supreme Court Justice Warren Brandeis (sic, likely referring to Louis Brandeis) stated in his dissent in the *INS* case: "**The law should reflect that the noblest achievements of humanity—knowledge, ascertained truths, conceptions, and ideas—after voluntary communication to others, become free as the air to common use.**"[^(37)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0200) This axiom reflects the so-called **`idea/expression dichotomy`**, which European copyright scholars prefer to formulate as the dichotomy between protected form and unprotected content. This rule has been widely enshrined in the `US Copyright Act` (Section 102(b)), the `TRIPS Agreement` (Article 9(2)), and the `WIPO Copyright Treaty` (Article 2). In the EU legal order, we find a similar rule in the `Software Directive`[^(38)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0201), although it applies only to computer software. Although these provisions do not explicitly mention "data," it is generally accepted that data *per se* is not a subject of copyright protection, whether based on the "**idea/expression dichotomy**" or directly applying the requirements for copyright protection—i.e., the existence of an author and originality—this is undisputed.[^(39)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0202)

Although data itself is therefore not protected by copyright, the copyright issue regarding data compilations is more complex. The `Berne Convention` protects "**compilations of literary or artistic works such as encyclopaedias and anthologies**" (Article 2(5)), but does not mention data collections, and explicitly denies copyright in "**daily news or miscellaneous facts having the character of mere items of press information**" (Article 2(8)). The `TRIPS Agreement` (Article 10(2)) more broadly protects "**compilations of data or other material, whether in machine readable or other form, which by reason of the selection or arrangement of their contents constitute intellectual creations**." The `WIPO Copyright Treaty` (Article 5) contains a similar provision. Based on these modern treaties, the `Database Directive`[^(40)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0203) instructs Member States to provide copyright protection for "**databases which, by reason of the selection or arrangement of their contents, constitute the author's own intellectual creation**" (Article 3(1)).

In the *Football Dataco and others* case (football league fixture lists), the Court clarified the standard of "**author's own intellectual creation**" (i.e., originality), meaning that the selection or arrangement of data must be the result of creative activity. Relying on the second sentence of Article 3(1) ("**no other criteria shall be applied to determine the eligibility for protection**"), the Court held that the mere investment of significant skill and labor cannot establish originality. In other words, the Directive's originality standard excludes any (so-called) copyright protection for databases based solely on investment or other factors.[^(41)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0204) The Court's judgment not only ended the UK's long-standing practice of copyright protection for data compilations based on the "**skill and labor**" (investment) standard but also ended similar rules in other Member States. For instance, the Dutch "**protection of non-original works**" (`geschriftenbescherming`), which existed as a standard for protecting non-original works and compilations in the Netherlands for over a century, was formally abolished in 2014 following the *Football Dataco* case.[^(42)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0205)

The *Football Dataco* case also excluded copyright protection for data compilations generated by machines without any human intervention. This aligns with the general rule that the author of a copyright work must be a human. However, it is noteworthy that the UK `Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988` extended copyright protection to computer-generated works: "**In the case of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work which is computer-generated, the author shall be taken to be the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken**" (Section 9(3)).[^(43)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0206) Whether this rule can coexist with the *Football Dataco* rule remains to be seen. The CJEU held that "**where the creation of a database is dictated by technical considerations, rules or constraints which leave no room for creative freedom**, it lacks originality."[^(44)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0207)

Both the `TRIPS Agreement` and the `WIPO Copyright Treaty` emphasize that copyright in compilations of data "**shall not extend to the data or material itself**." Similarly, the `Database Directive` (Article 3(2)) reminds that copyright in the database "**shall not extend to its contents**," thereby excluding copyright protection for the data compiled within the database. The scope of database copyright protection is limited to the original structure of the database (selection or arrangement) (Database Directive, Recital 5). The extraction of (parts of) the contents of a database does not infringe the database copyright, provided there is no appropriation of the database's selection or arrangement.[^(45)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0208)

### (II) The *Sui Generis* Database Right

Article 7(1) of the `Database Directive`, as part of the copyright regime, requires Member States to protect databases where there has been qualitatively and/or quantitatively a substantial investment. This *sui generis* database right (`sui generis right`) has made the Directive a subject of mixed reception internationally. This "substantial investment" (`substantial investment`) must be in "**the obtaining, verification or presentation of the contents**" of the database (Article 7(1)). "Obtaining" refers to the act of collecting data, works, or other materials for the database. "Verification" refers to the acts of checking, correcting, and updating existing data in the database. "Presentation" refers to acts such as digitizing (scanning) analog materials or creating compilations. A judgment of the German Federal Court of Justice (`Bundesgerichtshof`, BGH) indicates that the "substantial investment" standard is not difficult to meet. Any investment in a database that "**is not entirely insignificant under objective standards [...] and cannot be simply accomplished by just anyone**" satisfies the condition.[^(46)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0209) The CJEU has not yet opined on this minimum standard.

In four landmark cases concerning betting companies' unauthorized use of sports fixture schedules ("**regular sporting events**"), the CJEU held that the database right does not protect investment in the *creation* of the data or other contents of the database. The Court ruled that "**investment in the obtaining of (database) contents**" refers to resources used to seek out existing materials and collect them into a database, but **not** resources used for the creation of the database materials.[^(47)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0210) The main reason for this distinction is explicitly stated in the judgment: the economic rationale of the `Database Directive` is to promote and reward investment in the *making* of databases, not investment in the *creation* of new data. In the Court's words, "**the purpose of the *sui generis* right provided for by the Database Directive is to promote the establishment of storage and processing systems for existing information, not the creation of materials capable of being collected in a database in the future**."[^(48)](#chapter3.xhtml#wz_01_0211)
