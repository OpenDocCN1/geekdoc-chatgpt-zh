
# Data Ownership, Data Producer Rights, and Data Access Rights: An Analysis from the Perspective of EU Competition Law

This statement triggered academic discussions and public debate on data ownership in the EU, particularly in Germany. As early as 2011, the German government, industry, and research structures had established the "Industrie 4.0" platform as a joint initiative to meet the challenges of the fourth industrial revolution, including the integration of information and telecommunications technologies into manufacturing processes.[⁵](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0639) The reasons why the debate focused so intensely on data ownership rather than data access rights are mainly threefold: First, as part of the rapidly growing public interest, besides describing data as the most valuable resource and the "oil of the 21st century,"[⁶](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0640) the media began to ask "who should own the data?" Because an increasing number of people purchase and use smart products, this question has attracted broad public attention, with all citizens as "data producers" qualifying as potential data owners. Second, German scholars have conducted significant research on the possible design of data ownership.[⁷](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0641) Third, EU Commissioner Oettinger, who was responsible for the free flow of data initiative until the end of 2016, made public statements in German, leaving the impression that the European Commission would seriously consider legislating on data ownership.[⁸](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0642) However, at the same time, a growing number of scholars warned against introducing new property rights in data.[⁹](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0643) These scholars expressed skepticism about the economic rationale of such a right and its potential negative impact on the data economy. Most strikingly, German industry quickly questioned the need for introducing such a right.[^(10)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0644)

Faced with the discussion triggered after the publication of the relevant report in 2015, the European Commission still raised the issue of data ownership in its report on January 10, 2017.[^(11)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0645) However, the Commission adopted a rather surprising approach. While the nature of property as an exclusive right is usually considered to increase barriers to data access, the Commission now discussed acknowledging a potential "data producer right" as a means to *increase* data access,[^(12)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0646) thereby transforming the 2015 "ownership and access" debate into an issue of "ownership for data access."

The Commission's view rests on two premises: first, that the free flow of data is always good; second, that it is necessary to unlock data for the benefit of smart product users. This article will analyze these premises, as well as the Commission's considerations on the data producer right, using a more principled, competition-oriented approach.[^(13)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0647) The premise of this approach is that in a market economy, competition and transactions generally lead to the most efficient utilization of resources. Therefore, from an economic perspective, interventions, including the adoption of new intellectual property rights, are only authorized and justified when they help correct an identifiable market failure. In this regard, the European Commission's assumption that there is a need to unlock data in certain situations is correct. However, this article argues against proposing the data producer right as a new intellectual property right with a property law nature, and suggests considering more targeted, and even more effective, data access rights instead.[^(14)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0648) It should also be noted that the free flow of data is not always efficient. Therefore, this article first examines the current competitive situation and the resulting competition problems. It then discusses possible solutions to these problems, including the data producer right and the data access right as an alternative. Finally, it analyzes situations where excessive data flow causes competition problems.

## II. Current Competitive Situation and Possible Competition Problems

Generally, intellectual property rights are expected to provide incentives for innovation. However, there seems to be no need for additional incentives to generate data. This also applies to data collected by sensors in smart products. Currently, companies invest heavily in developing smart products with embedded sensors because they view the transformation of manufacturing and the Internet of Things as a disruptive innovation that forces companies unable to adapt out of the market.

However, in this competitive environment, manufacturers still need to convince customers to buy their smart products. In this regard, a distinction must be made between industrial customers and end-users.

For industrial customers, the fourth industrial revolution is a significant step in process and product innovation. On the one hand, sensors embedded in smart machines enable predictive maintenance, thereby helping producers using these machines in their factories avoid production stoppages. Thus, smart machines reduce production costs (process innovation). At the same time, machine sensors are designed to identify and even avoid defects in manufactured products, thereby improving product quality (product innovation). Consequently, the use of smart machines in factories helps manufacturers compete better in downstream product markets.

Of course, there is a concern that suppliers of sensor-embedded machines might spy on industrial customers' trade secrets. However, if the machine supplier is not a competitor in the downstream product market, that supplier has no particular self-interest in misappropriating the customer's trade secrets. Conversely, customer trust in the supplier and the supplier's respect for the customer's trade secrets should become an important competitive parameter for the sale of smart machines. Simultaneously, a stable and effective trade secret protection framework is key to the functioning of the new data economy.[^(15)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0649)

Regarding end-user acceptance of smart products, the situation is more difficult. They need the product to have higher utility, for example, safety (autonomous driving), comfort, and cost savings (smart home).

Furthermore, end-users may worry about the collection of sensitive personal data, including health data collected by wearables monitoring bodily functions during medication, and data collected by cars or home appliances. Therefore, to persuade consumers to buy products, suppliers of smart consumer products should guarantee a high level of data protection, unlike search engine or social platform operators who offer "free services" to obtain large amounts of consumer data. Thus, a stable and effective legal framework for data protection is needed to build end-consumer trust in the data economy. Likewise, the legislature should consider the potential negative impact of new legal obligations to collect data on consumers' willingness to purchase smart products.[^(16)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0650)

In summary, data ownership does not seem necessary to incentivize bringing smart products to market. However, there may be other reasons, such as the need to enable downstream markets for the utilization of sensor-generated data. A data licensing market only emerges when third parties can be excluded from accessing and using that data. However, such exclusivity does not necessarily depend on conceiving data ownership as a property right. Manufacturers of smart products already exercise factual control over the data generated by such products and can protect their datasets from unauthorized access through technical protection measures.[^(17)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0651) This situation places the manufacturer, as the factual data holder, in a position very similar to that of a data exclusive right holder for the purpose of data transactions. According to the European Commission, if existing data silos cannot be sufficiently utilized,[^(18)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0652) the reason for this phenomenon is likely not a lack of legal protection, but rather that parties find it difficult to assess the utility and value of datasets, making it impossible to determine the appropriate price to pay for access[^(19)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0653) and the emergence of technical barriers (e.g., lack of data format interoperability).[^(20)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0654) This does not exclude the need for additional protection, particularly liability rules for infringing circumvention acts.[^(21)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0655)

If factual control over machine-generated sensor data is sufficient to conclude contracts on data utilization, then before discussing whether additional data property rights are needed, the law should first assess the functioning of contract law. From a competition policy perspective, the question here is whether the relevant product markets will function, or conversely, whether they are likely to be affected by anti-competitive behavior.

The analysis of the functioning of relevant product markets should again start with the agreements reached between smart product manufacturers and purchasers of such products. In fact, contracts for the sale of smart products differ significantly from those for traditional products. The digitization of products fundamentally changes the landscape of industrial business models. While a transaction for the sale of a car aims to provide the purchaser with ownership and property in the car, perhaps with some additional warranties protecting the purchaser from defects, in the case of autonomous and driverless driving, a car transaction will no longer, or not only, rely on a pure sales contract, but will include manufacturer services for predictive maintenance and safety-related services,[^(22)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0656) or one could say, a comprehensive service obligation for the operation of the car. Similarly, a manufacturer can sell a smart home device as part of a more global software-based home management system that connects different household devices via a computer. Whenever the washing machine or refrigerator needs refilling, the computer can even order other products, such as detergent and food, from suppliers cooperating with the device manufacturer. Electricity, energy, or water providers will begin to offer their products as components of data-based facility management systems, and pharmaceutical companies will monitor patients' bodily functions via smart wearables, thereby transforming into providers of more comprehensive healthcare services during medication. This indicates that, as an expression of data-driven business models related to smart products, simple sales contracts for goods will be replaced by "system contracts" bundling numerous goods and services, potentially locking customers into the providers of such systems.

Therefore, new business models related to smart products have the potential to exclude competitors from the market. From the perspective of classic competition law assessment, the first question concerns the relevant product. Should competition law regard the "system" with all its elements as the relevant product itself, or should it separate the different elements in the sense of distinct products? In the latter case, such an offer of a digital system dependent on smart products can be viewed as potential anti-competitive tying.

Of course, as always, competition law must apply to the specific facts of the individual case. A home delivery service related to a refrigerator will be regarded as a separate product because such services are also offered independently of smart home devices. Similarly, if a sales contract for a smart product with predictive maintenance services is offered, exclusive effects may arise if independent repairers cannot access the data collected by sensors embedded in the product.[^(23)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0657) In these cases, anti-competitive concerns involve both the exclusionary effect on competitors and customer lock-in, because customers are unlikely to buy from competitors after making substantial investments.

However, potential anti-competitive effects are not only related to competition in the smart product market and the repair of such products. The business models of smart product manufacturers may also exclude independent data analysis service providers from the market. If electricity, energy, and water suppliers link facility management services to such supply, independent providers of digital facility management services will find it difficult to enter the market. In particular, many customers usually prefer to purchase various smart products from different manufacturers. They have different needs but still wish to digitally connect these devices and manage them holistically internally, or entrust them to independent data service providers. For example, a factory operator purchases smart machines from different manufacturers; a farmer uses agricultural machinery of different brands; a homeowner wants all the benefits of a smart home but still values a smart home in the broader sense. Finally, patients need medication for multiple diseases and are monitored by their trusted doctor or hospital. Clearly, for the patient's benefit, that doctor needs access to data collected by any pharmaceutical company controlling the patient's physical health.

Indeed, when discussing the potential data producer right to "unlock data," the interests of purchasers or users of smart products must be considered,[^(24)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0658) to which the European Commission has already paid attention.

## III. How to Unlock Data: Why Data Access Rights Should Take Precedence Over Data Producer Rights

To address these competition problems, different solutions can be discussed, some of which are also within the European Commission's consideration.

### (1) Limitations of the Contract Law Path

The first approach is to assist purchasers of smart products through rules controlling standard contract terms. The European Commission has also considered this possibility,[^(25)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0659) advocating for the amendment of the Unfair Contract Terms Directive.[^(26)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0660) Purchasers of smart products (especially machines) are usually industrial users; however, the scope of application of this Directive must be extended from B2C transactions to B2B transactions.

However, this is still insufficient to solve the problem. The functioning of the Directive also requires default contract rules that can serve as benchmarks for fairness standards, but such rules are not yet well developed. In particular,[^(27)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0661) the European Commission, in its proposal for a Digital Content Directive, maintained the classic approach of EU law in contract law by establishing mandatory contract rules for consumer contracts. Therefore, the scope of this Directive needs to be expanded, perhaps by adding another rule according to which the rules of this Directive can also apply as non-mandatory rules.

Furthermore, the scope of application of the Digital Content Directive proposal remains unclear for contracts concerning the delivery and sale of goods[^(28)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0662) and services related to those goods. Finally, one must doubt whether the rules proposed by the European Commission can adequately protect purchasers of smart products. The Digital Content Directive proposal only grants consumers a right to data portability upon termination of the contract,[^(29)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0663) whereas, given the competition problems outlined above, purchasers particularly need access to data products *during* the use of the smart product.

Even if the Digital Content Directive were to be further developed in this regard, the general approach of relying on standard contract term control has other disadvantages. Although the law on unfair contract terms attempts to correct unequal bargaining power and is very flexible in this respect,[^(30)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0664) it only protects the *direct* purchasers of smart products. Therefore, the contract law path faces the following obstacles: users often do not purchase smart products directly from the manufacturer, but from retailers, or even from persons reselling the smart product as a used product. Moreover, in some cases, smart products collect data from persons who are not even linked to the original manufacturer by a contractual chain, but who still have a legitimate interest in accessing the data collected by the smart product. An example of this situation is a farmer using land data generated by a machine operated by an independent agricultural service provider.

This raises the question: how can the person whose data is collected and the person who has a legitimate interest in unlocking the data be entitled to access that data for themselves or for third-party data analysis service providers, vis-à-vis the manufacturer. This seems to be the case the Commission is considering with the data producer right.

### (2) Why the Data Producer Right Cannot Promote Data Access

Apart from the fact that the term "data producer" seems to be misunderstood (after all, data is "produced" according to a business model and a smart product, both implemented and developed by the manufacturer), the data producer right as a property right, on the one hand, is insufficient to remedy the fundamental problem of unequal bargaining power distribution, and on the other hand, by providing the data producer with the right to prevent the producer from trading, enables third parties to access their datasets.

Regarding the first problem, the Commission overlooks the fact that the data producer as a property right holder will always be able to designate data rights in this way, or grant an exclusive license to the smart product manufacturer for free. Therefore, in situations where the manufacturer has high bargaining power, the data producer right will fail to achieve the European Commission's intended goal in creating this right.[^(31)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0665)

Regarding the second problem, the Commission overlooks the fact that manufacturers do not possess strong market power in all cases. For example, in the case of a powerful industrial purchaser of smart products, this purchaser would likely rely on its data producer right to restrict the supplier's ability to commercialize its dataset and restrict third-party access to these datasets. In this case, it should be noted that, due to the wide divergence in uses of this dataset, the data generated by all smart products sold to consumers and aggregated by the manufacturer must be considered a distinct product from a competition law perspective, which cannot be replaced by datasets controlled by individual purchasers of smart products. Therefore, the EU legislature should particularly avoid creating barriers to these datasets, which usually involve specific public interests.

In any case, the introduction of a data producer right would create complex rights clearance problems. Whenever data is accessed, one would find that the holders of the data contained in the dataset are not the data producers. For example, if the legislature acknowledges the data producer right of the car holder regarding data generated by car sensors, a third party seeking to access the larger, more valuable dataset enjoyed by the car manufacturer would risk infringing such rights. This indicates that the introduction of such a right could easily become a serious obstacle to establishing an autonomous driving functional system, which largely relies on data sharing between many manufacturers and many other data providers.

### (3) In Favor of an Irrevocable Data Access Right

An alternative to the data producer right that can actually promote data access is the recognition of an irrevocable (non-waivable) data access right.[^(32)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0666)

The advantages of such a right include: First, data access rights are more targeted because they can respond to potential market failure. Compared to the data producer right constructed in the form of a property right, it does not create any barriers to the commercialization of data in downstream data markets. For example, a data access right can entitle a farmer to connect data collected by different agricultural machines of different brands to operate their farm, without in any way restricting the ability of these machine manufacturers to aggregate and commercialize data from all machines. Data access rights would particularly increase access to data held by public authorities (e.g., data access for environmental protection). In fact, the legislature faces merely a data access problem; therefore, it has no reason to go beyond access rights by adopting a new property right, which would create additional exclusionary effects on the market without any additional benefit.[^(33)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0667)

Second, the irrevocability of this use right would guarantee that this right cannot be set aside by contractual agreement between the farmer and the machine manufacturer.

Third, data access rights can and should be granted to persons with a legitimate interest in data access. As a statutory non-contractual right, it does not necessarily have to belong to the direct purchaser of the product, nor does it necessarily belong to the user of the product. The question is who is directly affected by the data collection and needs access to that data; if the data is collected by a machine owned by an independent agricultural service provider, that subject may also be the farmer.[^(34)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0668) This special interest limitation restricts the scope of the data access right; it should be limited to purposes of operating a farm or household use, rather than for the purpose of commercializing data to third parties. However, the data access right should not depend on whether the person entitled to access the data conducts data analysis internally or outsources it to an independent data analysis agency. Therefore, the data access right should include the right to demand that the manufacturer grant direct access rights to third parties to provide such services.[^(35)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0669)

Fourth, data access rights can be regulated in a positive manner, i.e., by setting specific requirements for granting such rights. From the perspective of legislative technique, this is a clear advantage. In discussing the data producer right, the European Commission realized that this right might go too far, and therefore pointed out that the granting of this right needs specific exceptions and limitations.[^(36)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0670) However, this is not an easy task, because the legislature must foresee all different situations, and a propertized right is obviously not conducive to such foresight. In contrast, data access rights can avoid the problem of over-protection.[^(37)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0671)

Fifth, the recognition of access rights is also consistent with competition law principles. It responds to the strong incentive of manufacturers to refuse to grant data access rights for the purpose of excluding competitors from the market. Of course, in any case, competition law enforcement can serve as an alternative. However, given the need to prove market dominance in each case, private enforcement of competition law usually faces a heavy burden of proof, a problem that becomes more prominent with a large number of cases.[^(38)](#chapter5.xhtml#wz_01_0672) Conversely, if the manufacturer is not interested in locking up market competition, the data access right causes no harm, because under the competitive pressure of the smart product market, the manufacturer would be willing to grant data access rights anyway, possibly even free of charge.

However, the question remains whether the European legislature should enact a generally applicable data access right or adopt a sector-specific approach. Although there is an urgent need to design a generally applicable legal framework for the data economy, it is better to recommend that the European Commission adopt a sector-specific approach.
