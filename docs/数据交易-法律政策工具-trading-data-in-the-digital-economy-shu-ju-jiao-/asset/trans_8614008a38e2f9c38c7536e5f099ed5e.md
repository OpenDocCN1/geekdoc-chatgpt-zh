
# Data Markets, Trading Problems, and Governance Solutions: An In-Depth Analysis

How much do we know about the problems of data markets? First, data trading can take very different forms, for example, through data brokers, data markets, and with many different business models and contractual arrangements. In the theoretical study on data markets by Koutroumpis et al., data trading is divided into four categories: "one-to-one" (bilateral trading), "one-to-many" (dispersed markets), "many-to-one" (data collection, e.g., data from "free" services provided to a single buyer), and "many-to-many" (multi-sided markets).[^(36)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0304) The gains from data trading are growing rapidly, but neither in the US nor in the EU are there fully developed (multi-sided) data markets for buyers and sellers to trade directly.[^(37)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0305) This is consistent with the view of Koutroumpis et al. that "many-to-many" markets in particular seem to have significant trading problems.[^(38)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0306) So far, the few empirical studies on the EU do not support the argument that legal issues of data ownership or data exclusivity are the main problems of data markets. The problems mentioned in these studies are: insufficient demand for data (stemming from insufficient awareness of data value), interoperability issues and lack of standards, data pricing problems, lack of open platforms for data sharing, and insufficient numbers of skilled data workers.[^(39)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0307) Issues of data quality and data provenance have also emerged in recent discussions. The value of data to buyers still depends to a large extent on the quality and provenance of the data. This includes explicit information on data type, its correctness and completeness, whether it was legally collected (compliant with privacy laws or appropriately anonymized), and the context and manner of its generation (i.e., what method was used).[^(40)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0308) If information on these aspects is not disclosed (or cannot be verified), data markets may suffer from typical market failure problems due to information asymmetry (such as adverse selection). Whether the reputation of sellers or data markets (if they offer data verification services) can solve these problems remains an open question.

Although a mosaic picture of empirical and theoretical insights on data trading is slowly emerging, a comprehensive analysis of market failure problems is still lacking.[^(41)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0309) The decisive question for whether we need exclusive data producer rights is whether these sellers or data markets can ensure (e.g., through monitoring or data traceability) that buyers do not violate their data licensing contracts by leaking goods or services to third parties. If this turns out to be a serious problem that cannot be solved through technical restrictions and data traceability, then exclusive property rights enabling lawsuits against such third parties might again become an interesting policy option. However, it is currently unclear how the "data producer right" suggested in the report would help solve other trading problems (e.g., data quality issues).[^(42)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0310)

However, all these problems require more theoretical and empirical research. This also makes it difficult to assess the other policy recommendations in the report concerning the promotion of data trading. Facilitating measures are not problematic per se and may even help, for example, guidance on how to handle control of non-personal data in contracts or the creation of new default contract rules, as both offer opportunities to reduce transaction costs. However, without a clear analysis of trading problems, how such guidance and default rules should be designed, and whether the Commission or legislator can really provide more optimal solutions than those gradually achieved in the market by companies and lawyers (as transaction cost engineers) developing standard contracts for data, remains an open question. The brief explanation in the report suggests that guidance and default rules seem to be aimed more at solving fairness issues than genuinely addressing transaction costs.[^(43)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0311) An even more interesting contribution to the whole discussion is the recommendation to promote the development of technical solutions for reliable identification and data exchange. A huge (but insufficiently discussed) problem of the digital economy is the verifiability of data contract compliance. How can a company selling data by granting access rights to it detect whether the acquiring buyer uses the data only in the contractually agreed manner, especially not granting access to other companies? If compliance with contracts cannot be detected and verified, data trading lacks the necessary trust. Therefore, technical solutions for "traceability and clear identification of data provenance"[^(44)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0312) (e.g., through "watermarking") may be a very important prerequisite for functioning markets for data trading and sharing.[^(45)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0313) This also relates to interoperability and standardization issues (which can necessarily promote data trading) and the discussion on `APIs`.[^(46)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0314) Overall, these policy options may help solve trading problems and reduce transaction costs, but clear policy recommendations can only be given after a careful analysis of the real trading problems, which may also differ greatly for different types of data, and in this process, entirely new policy options may emerge and become relevant.

## Five: On the Distribution and Fairness of Data?

An important issue repeatedly mentioned in the report is that market solutions may be subject to "unequal bargaining power" on the one hand between companies, and on the other hand between private individuals or other companies (especially SMEs).[^(47)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0315) The European Commission emphasizes that solutions to promote the sharing of machine-generated data should take into account possible differences in bargaining power between market participants and ensure a "fair sharing of benefits between data holders, processors, and application providers in the value chain".[^(48)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0316) Regarding policy solutions, the introduction of legal control of unfair terms in `B2B` contractual relationships is proposed, combined with default contract rules, which can also serve as a benchmark for fair solutions in data-related contracts. Although it is possible to link this issue to the goal of increasing data sharing and trading, in the report, it seems to be treated as a separate issue. This is obviously related to similar views in academic literature that private parties and SMEs may not be able to obtain a fair share (or return) of the benefits from data through contractual arrangements on the market.[^(49)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0317) Furthermore, it touches on a more general question of who should benefit from the data collected/generated in the respective situations.

The problem is that neither the report nor the *Data Economy SWD* manages to provide a clear analysis of this "unequal bargaining power" situation.[^(50)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0318) For an article, this is a complex topic. However, distinguishing three distinct groups of problems may help systematize the discussion. The first group includes situations where one party is weaker than the other due to clearly identifiable market failure problems. In some cases, the respective company possesses market power as defined by competition law. Then, due to a lack of competition, a situation of "unequal bargaining power" arises. In these cases, the company with market power may have the ability to conclude contracts on data with private individuals or other companies (especially SMEs) that lead to "unfair" outcomes regarding actual control or redistribution of data.[^(51)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0319) Other reasons for the relative weakness of one party in bargaining are information asymmetries and behavioral biases (especially vis-à-vis private individuals). This may lead to more general market failures due to information and behavioral problems, which are usually addressed by consumer protection law (and for personal data, also by data protection law). For such cases, there are established legal rules to address these problems (competition law, consumer protection law, data protection law, etc.).

These legal rules may not be seen as solving all problems. For example, the threshold for establishing market dominance under Article 102 TFEU is high. Therefore, policymakers may also look for legal rules that can address weaker forms of market power. Under German competition law, Section 20 GWB (Act against Restraints of Competition) also allows control of market power in bilateral relationships between companies. The European Commission and many legal scholars consider applying the unfairness control of the *Unfair Contract Terms Directive*[^(52)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0320) to `B2B` contracts to provide remedies for "unfair" outcomes in contractual arrangements involving data.[^(53)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0321) However, we should be skeptical about the extent to which this solution can work. Its main problem is the lack of clear criteria for application, which may lead to legal uncertainty and the danger of excessive market intervention, and may create a false optimism that these policy options (especially extending unfairness control to `B2B` contracts) can solve existing problems. Beyond the recommendations, there seems to be a broader consensus that introducing exclusive property rights to data (as a "data producer right") cannot solve the "unequal bargaining power" problem by overcoming these market failures, because these rights can easily be contracted out in situations where one party has market power or information asymmetries (or behavioral biases) exist.[^(54)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0322) Therefore, this group of "unequal bargaining power" problems should first be addressed through the regular policy solutions targeting these market failures, i.e., competition law, consumer protection law, and data protection law.

The second category of problems concerns situations where specific data is simultaneously produced or used by several usually clearly definable stakeholders, i.e., multi-stakeholder problems. This typically occurs in areas such as smart manufacturing, smart agriculture, smart energy, smart health, and smart cars.[^(55)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0323) Here, the parties usually jointly contribute to the production of data or need the same data to improve their products (and services) and innovate. The decisive point is that in such multi-stakeholder situations, "ownership" solutions (where only one party can uniquely control the data) may not work as intended. Importantly, it makes no essential difference whether such exclusive ownership by one party is based on the private party's "factual exclusivity" of holding data or on explicit exclusive property rights (such as a data producer right). In a complex multi-stakeholder situation, the problem with granting one private party a monopoly status over specific data, whether de facto or de jure, is that this monopoly control gives it strong bargaining power vis-à-vis other stakeholders. Such a position can be used to demand high license fees or other benefits from other stakeholders, which are "unfair" (such as data hold-up). We will discuss this issue in more depth in the next section in connection with data access rights and the formation of more complex data governance solutions. But an important conclusion can already be drawn here: granting one party an exclusive data producer right instead of insisting on the data holder's current factual exclusivity does not solve this problem.

However, a third group of problems may arise, which can only be touched upon here. This is a more fundamental problem independent of the first two, namely who should benefit from the use of data in the digital economy from a distributional perspective; the structure of data rights may influence the distribution of huge potential benefits. In the discussion on data ownership, this may also be linked to "unfair" expected market outcomes regarding who gets the data (benefits). Whether and under what conditions granting property-like status to data affects the distribution of data benefits must be analyzed in depth from an economic perspective. This is also important for personal data, because EU data protection law grants individuals rights to personal data that are to some extent similar to exclusive rights.

## Six: Data Access Problems and Data Access Rights

The report rightly points out in its analysis that a problem may exist where private holding of data prevents sufficient access to and further use of data. Promoting data trading and licensing is one possible solution. Another group of solutions includes direct measures, such as introducing mandatory data access solutions (or promoting open data schemes) to enable other parties to access data. For a more in-depth analysis, distinguishing two different groups of situations may help answer why the exclusive "factual control" of data is problematic:

1.  The former is based on the general argument that if the use of non-rivalrous data is insufficient, it is inefficient, especially in completely different economic environments (big data and cross-sector business models). In this case, promoting trading (and licensing) and general mandatory data access schemes may also be viable policy options, for example, compulsory licensing of data use in the public interest (public authorities, public statistics, scientific research).
2.  The latter group of situations involves multi-stakeholder scenarios, where either multiple parties jointly contribute to data production, or several parties have a strong economic interest in accessing the same data. It has already been argued above that unique ownership of data rights (whether through factual control or explicit property rights) may not be the optimal "data rights" solution, because this "unique owner" may use this monopoly position to marginalize other stakeholders.[^(56)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0324) This not only leads to the aforementioned fairness problems due to unequal bargaining power but also leads to high transaction costs (conflicts among various stakeholders), thereby inhibiting investments in cooperation based on value chains and value networks between companies. This simultaneously implies negative effects on economic efficiency and innovation in the digital economy.

Regarding multi-stakeholder situations, let us briefly examine two examples. In smart agriculture, sensor data from agricultural machinery is very important for both farmers and machinery manufacturers (and possibly other specialized service providers). For farmers, access to sensor data from all machines of different manufacturers is valuable for improving their agricultural production levels; for manufacturers, access to data generated by all machines they produce is also highly beneficial for product improvement.[^(57)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0325) Multi-stakeholder situations in other cases can be much more complex. For smart cars, stakeholders have a strong interest in the vast amounts of different types of data they generate. Stakeholders include the car owner, the car driver (and other passengers), the car manufacturer, car parts suppliers, insurance companies, independent repair services and parts manufacturers, public authorities (traffic enforcement and road maintenance), and various companies willing to provide services to car passengers (e.g., media content, etc.). In addition, (as in many future IoT applications) much of this data also contains personal data, to which individuals have corresponding rights under data protection law.[^(58)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0326) Whether from a legal perspective or from the actual situation, establishing a unique exclusive property right to all this data is probably not the optimal solution. It is therefore not surprising that discussions on more complex solutions regarding who should have what rights to this data arise, especially in complex situations like smart cars. Of course, similar problems arise in the areas of smart home devices (e.g., data on energy efficiency) or health data.

One important solution to such multi-stakeholder problems is to grant specific stakeholders (non-exclusive) data access rights. The report also suggests combining data producer interests with certain non-exclusive data access rights (as limitations) to solve the problems arising when one party is granted an exclusive data producer right.[^(59)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0327) However, the decisive point is that for such solutions, we do not need an exclusive data producer right. In a multi-stakeholder context, specific stakeholders' data access rights can also be defined directly, which can be made mandatory against the factual owners of certain data. This is the case, for example, with the access of independent repair services to relevant repair and maintenance information.[^(60)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0328) This is also the solution proposed by the Max Planck Institute for Innovation and Competition as an alternative to introducing a data producer right.[^(61)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0329) The report argues that the solution to such problems lies not in a data producer right, but in the appropriate definition of specific stakeholders' data access rights.[^(62)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0330) However, from an economic perspective, the technical and economic conditions of different multi-stakeholder constellations in health data, agricultural data, smart home data, energy data, etc., differ significantly, partly due to the involvement of personal data. Therefore, whether specific data access rights for certain stakeholders can work requires in-depth legal and economic analysis of these different situations. Consequently, the optimal governance solutions for such multi-stakeholder constellations may be very complex and require sector-specific differentiation.

However, we should also exercise caution and restraint before proposing the legal introduction of specific mandatory data access rights in these multi-stakeholder situations. From an economic perspective, companies in competitive markets have an incentive to offer data governance solutions that solve these problems to customers and business partners, for example, by proactively granting data access rights to the most important stakeholders. It must therefore also be shown why we cannot expect market competition to lead to appropriate solutions. Before intervening in the market by granting specific data access rights, we must also look for market failure problems in these multi-stakeholder situations. In this respect, the farmer/machine manufacturer example may differ significantly from the smart car example, because the complexity of the latter may be much greater than the former. It can be assumed that as the number and types of stakeholders and the complexity of motives for using data increase, the likelihood of market failure in multi-stakeholder situations (due to high transaction costs) may increase. However, this is precisely the subject of future research.

Ultimately, the issue of data access rights also returns to our discussion on incentives for data production in Part Three. If we accept the conclusion of Part Three, i.e., that the lack of a general incentive problem depends on the possibilities of using and commercializing data (and not on the inevitable result of costless data production), all solutions focused on granting private data access rights must balance interests; if data access rights are granted, they may create new incentive problems, which relates to the usual solutions allowing access to certain data (e.g., through public statistics or regular mandatory access schemes) and specific multi-stakeholder data governance solutions. Thus, when discussing data access rights as a solution, the (widely ignored) issue of incentives for data production reappears. This does not mean that if data holders must allow others to access data, setting fair compensation for the data they control is always very important. For traditional IP rights, both economists and lawyers agree that innovators should obtain sufficient returns from their innovations to provide incentives for innovation, but not excessive incentives. The same logic applies to data production: if data production costs are very low or even zero, then access to data even without compensation or with low compensation may not be a problem. However, if we consider that certain types of data production are very costly, then granting mandatory access rights to such data (or requiring high compensation) should be treated with more caution.[^(63)](#chapter3_2.xhtml#wz_01_0331)

## Seven: Typified Data Governance Solutions, Not Generalized Data Property Rights and Data Access Rights

Through the report *Building a European Data Economy*, the European Commission has initiated a very important discussion on the necessity of legislation and the formation of a corresponding regulatory framework for the vast amounts of machine-generated non-personal data (and anonymized personal data) produced in smart manufacturing (`Industry 4.0`), smart cars, and other "`Internet of Things`" devices. In particular, the "data ownership" issue is an extremely complex problem that has led to many confusing controversies. Precisely because of several characteristics of data (data as compiled information, non-rivalry in use, exclusivity of the lack of a general incentive problem, and data as input for big data and innovation), data becomes a very complex economic good that does not fit traditional economic and legal categories. Data trading and sharing on the market also seem to be a complex problem whose essence we have not yet fully understood. From this perspective, the report attempts to explore the potential problems of the data economy and what kind of policy solutions might work, even achieving unexpected effects. By focusing primarily on data access and data sharing, the European Commission has certainly chosen a perspective very suitable for the basic functional logic of the digital economy. However, the main problem is that our theoretical and empirical research on the important characteristics and problems of the data economy is still in its infancy. We should therefore be very cautious in drawing conclusions on: whether market failures exist and what types of market failures exist, and which policy measures might be suitable to solve these problems. Overall, we do not even know to what extent problems exist, or whether the digital economy market is functioning well under the current legal and regulatory framework for non-personal data. It is noteworthy that there is no similar policy discussion in the US yet.
