

# Current AI Capabilities: Boundaries and Limitations

It is worth noting that the current ability to transform into chatbots or application assistants is only a small part of the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). Many professional applications still require calling model capabilities directly via **Application Programming Interfaces (`API`)** to perform complex multi-round operations, and may even require human involvement to complete tasks.

Take an example: if we want to translate a book from Chinese to English, we first need to split the book into multiple segments processable by the model. Secondly, we need to provide the model with the glossary to be used for translation. Finally, we may need to call other models multiple times for grammar checks and language polishing.

When we try to use various AI chatbots or assistant functions, especially applying them to our own work, we also discover their shortcomings. For instance, some people use `ChatGPT` as a search engine to query the latest information, but soon find it doesn't know the latest information; its knowledge cuts off at a certain point in time. Worse still, it sometimes gives completely wrong information, expressed in a very "confident" manner. It even fabricates information out of thin air, like someone talking nonsense after getting drunk, yet its answers are extremely coherent, making it hard to discern. If we blindly trust and use this information, it will cause a lot of trouble. Later chapters will discuss this topic specifically. But generally speaking, if we can smartly avoid their weaknesses and leverage their strengths, these AI tools can provide help in many aspects.

# Section 2: What Capabilities Does GPT Possess? Why Does It Have Them?

After trying various new AI tools based on the `GPT` model, we discover they possess various surprising capabilities, such as writing, programming, logical reasoning, etc. We easily generate questions: What capabilities does `GPT` ultimately possess? Why does it possess them? The more we want to use it well, the more necessary it is to clarify these questions.

`GPT` possesses certain capabilities and professional knowledge but is not good at processing information and is prone to "hallucination".

## GPT's Capabilities

Generally speaking, we can consider a trained `GPT` model to possess the following capabilities:

- **Language Understanding Capability**: It can understand requirements posed and information provided in human natural language;
- **Text Generation Capability**: It can generate text according to our needs, such as continuing articles, writing summaries, answering questions;
- **Logical Reasoning Capability**: It can perform reasoning operations such as deduction and induction;
- **Mathematical Capability**: It can understand mathematical principles and formulas, and solve mathematical problems;
- **Multilingual Capability**: It not only masters multiple languages but can also translate between them;
- **Programming Capability**: It can understand code written in programming languages and write new code according to our requirements.

In short, it not only possesses language, mathematical, and reasoning capabilities but also masters knowledge in some professional fields. We invoke these capabilities and knowledge by asking questions.

At the same time, we have all realized that `GPT`'s capabilities have unique limitations. It seems not to possess some capabilities we originally thought a computer system would inherently have. Specifically, when we search, a computer system retrieves and gives information already stored in databases or on the internet. However, getting `GPT` to give a precise citation without modification is not easy. It seems to know the sentence but cannot give the exact citation. That's not even the worst; the worst is it may give a fabricated citation—this is the "hallucination" problem of generative AI models. To keep the conversation going, it "hallucinates" and then gives false, fabricated answers.

Going back to the beginning, what the `GPT` model actually does is "text completion"—completing the following text based on the preceding text. Colloquially, we say a beginning, and it continues the story. Tracing further back is the `Transformer` architecture, initially used for machine translation, converting one language into another—completing the next word of a sentence based on the semantics of the source language and the vocabulary probability of the target language. Later, this architecture was gradually used for more general generation tasks, such as continuing articles.

So, how does the `GPT` model know what to complete next? It is essentially a "next-token predictor" software; it predicts the next token based on preceding tokens and probability. It completes token by token, thereby "writing" the answer given to us.

Then the next question: how does the `GPT` model predict the next token? Simply put, it has "read" an enormous amount of text data; colloquially, researchers trained this model using massive amounts of text. It calculates the probability of the next token appearing based on preceding tokens, then takes the token with the highest probability as the next token. Stephen Wolfram gave an example: based on the preceding paragraph, the model predicts the next 5 words in order (sorted by probability): `learn`, `predict`, `make`, `understand`, `do`. Then the `GPT` model takes `learn` as the next word. Afterwards, it predicts the next word again.

In fact, we can adjust the probability of selecting the next word by adjusting the "temperature" indicator. The so-called "temperature" refers to an indicator reflecting randomness; the lower the "temperature", the lower the randomness. The model always selects the word with the highest probability; at this point, you might feel it is a bit "rigid"; increase the "temperature", randomness increases, the model randomly selects the next word from a group of words, resulting in large differences in results each time; at this point, you feel it is more creative.

The next question: how does the `GPT` model judge the probability of the next word? Parameters or weights (`parameters/weight`) in the multi-layer artificial neural network determine the probability of the next word appearing. These parameters are adjusted into place by training the artificial neural network with a super-large amount of text. This is also the meaning of "P" in `GPT`—**Pre-trained**, letting it master the parameters needed to predict the next word. `GPT-3.5` has 175 billion parameters.

As the amount of training text and parameters increase, the `GPT` model's core capability of predicting the next word becomes stronger. It seems to have acquired language capabilities, mastered knowledge, and learned reasoning—this is quite similar to human growth: as a child gradually learns to speak, they learn knowledge, and later become capable of reasoning. The evolution of the `GPT` model is: after increasing the training text volume, its knowledge and capabilities improve significantly. So how do we make its capabilities work for us? At this point, the protagonist of this book—**Prompt**—suddenly highlights its importance.

Generally, we give `GPT` a prompt (such as a paragraph to be translated, a paragraph to be continued, a long text to be summarized), then let it continue based on our prompt. However, since it already possesses language, reasoning, and generation capabilities, why not turn the prompt into a question (Please answer...) or a command (Please help me do...)?

We call the request input to the `GPT` model a **Prompt**; it is the interface for using the knowledge and capabilities possessed by the `GPT` model. As shown in Figure 1-2, we can obtain and use massive information (as done by Google and Baidu) and services (as done by Amazon, Taobao, Meituan) by searching keywords on search engines. Now, we can use the knowledge and capabilities mastered by AI models through prompts.

Many people only noticed the word `prompt` when applying generative AI products like `ChatGPT`. One of its Chinese meanings is a cue prepared to prevent actors from forgetting lines. In the 2007 book *Make Questions Work*, the author used `prompt` to refer to further questions used to obtain desired answers or more information. Now, `prompt` has a new meaning: the prompt given to guide it to give an ideal answer when using various generative AI models.

Currently, we usually input prompts into two types of generative AI models. One is Large Language Models like `GPT`, letting them answer questions or respond to requests based on prompts; the other is text-to-image drawing models, such as `OpenAI`'s `DALL-E`, `Stability` company's `Stable Diffusion` open-source model, `Midjourney` image generation software, etc. For the latter, since subtle changes in prompts bring huge changes in images, some jokingly call prompts "spells" and generating images from prompts "chanting spells to conjure magical paintings".

Below, let's focus on prompts used when using Large Language Models like `GPT`. Prompts are almost the only way to invoke the knowledge and capabilities of `GPT` and other LLMs. Below I will try to explain the implementation principle in plain language.

As mentioned earlier, LLMs are essentially trained probabilistic models; what they do can be seen as predicting the next token. They predict token by token, thereby forming complete sentences, paragraphs, or articles. If it is a translation task, the probability prediction of the next token is relatively simple—just translate by comparing with the original text. But if it is a generative task, such as letting it continue a paragraph or answer our question, the difficulty of next-token probability prediction increases greatly.

The role of the prompt is to guide the model to better calculate the next token. Guo-Yang Shen once excellently elucidated the essence of prompts in an article: "Adjust and optimize the values of the first m elements in the article; these elements usually include task descriptions, context information, etc., so that the LLM can generate results with higher probability and more in line with expectations in the context of a given task."

A prompt is essentially a text pattern we write, then let `GPT` continue writing according to the current pattern—this is why what we write subtly influences it. If we write casually, it responds casually; if we write formally, it answers formally. In short, if we give appropriate prompts, including clear action instructions (e.g., write an article), appropriate reference content (e.g., about spring), etc., the model can better decide the next token, ultimately generating an answer that meets our expectations.

## Unleashing AI Potential with Prompts

Prompts are the interface for invoking `GPT`'s knowledge and capabilities. We input prompts to `GPT`, inspire and invoke the knowledge and capabilities acquired by `GPT` through pre-training and fine-tuning, to let AI answer our questions and fulfill our requests.

In fact, `OpenAI`'s several iterations of `GPT` versions are, to some extent, all related to prompts.

- The title of the `GPT-1` paper is *Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training*, proposing a method to achieve language understanding and text generation through pre-training transformers.
- The title of the `GPT-2` paper is *Language Models Are Unsupervised Multitask Learners*, explicitly introducing the method of invoking model capabilities using prompts.
- The title of the `GPT-3` paper is *Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners* (Note: Original text says "Bidirectional Language Models Are Also Few-shot Learners" which seems to be a translation variance in the source; the actual GPT-3 paper title is "Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners". I will translate the source text faithfully but note the actual title). The so-called few-shot means we can add a few sample examples in the prompt to guide the model to answer similar questions more accurately (we will specifically illustrate the few-shot method later).
- The title of the `InstructGPT` (equivalent to `GPT-3.5`, also the main model used by `ChatGPT`) paper is *Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback*, where the instructions used for training can be seen as a combination of prompts and human-preferred ideal results.

From this, we can see that every technical iteration aims to enable the model to better respond to prompts and give answers.

In short, good prompts correspond to good results; poor prompts correspond to poor results; wrong prompts correspond to wrong results. Learning to ask `GPT` questions is learning to input effective prompts.

Below, let's analyze the relationship between prompts and models from another angle. The LLMs we use now are all models optimized on the basis of pre-training via **Instruction Fine-tuning**, which enables the model to better understand and answer instruction-related questions. `Facebook`'s parent company `Meta` provided an open-source LLM `LLaMA`. In early 2023, a Stanford University team improved upon it to create the model `Alpaca`, claimed to perform close to `ChatGPT`.

`Alpaca` was optimized for the various instruction tasks shown in Figure 1-3. Let's see what these instruction tasks specifically are?

Therefore, on the surface we interact with a chatbot or software assistant, but behind the scenes, it is trained models playing the role. After training, they possess general capabilities, domain-specific professional capabilities, and capabilities to complete specific tasks. ## Structured Prompts: ICDO — I See, Do!

To use AI model knowledge and capabilities, we need to input effective prompts. So far, our experience is: we should input **structured prompts** to AI. Here, we name the structured prompt "**ICDO**". We can understand it this way: after we input a prompt, AI responds: "**I see, Do! (I understand, executing immediately!)**"

The way to ask `GPT` is: we input a prompt, it outputs an answer. Of course, a prompt is not just "help me translate this sentence" or "help me organize meeting minutes". We need to deeply decompose its constituent elements to master the skill of asking excellent questions to obtain high-quality answers. Usually, a prompt can be decomposed into the following four parts: Instruction, Context, Input Data, Output Indicator. "**ICDO**" stands for the first letters of the four components of a structured prompt: **Instruction**, **Context**, **Data** (Input Data), **Output** (Output Indicator) (as shown in Figure 1-4). It was summarized by Elvis Saravia et al., and we have made two optimizations on this basis: naming it "**ICDO**" and further structuring it. The "**ICDO**" originally summarized by Elvis et al. was prepared for programmers accessing `GPT` via programming interfaces, but now it also applies to everyone who wants to use AI efficiently.

![Figure 1-4 Structured Prompt "ICDO" Framework](Text/https:/res.weread.qq.com/wrepub/CB_3300083703_4vdAI83PM30e54b6G601H55acc.jpg)

The prompts we input to `GPT` usually consist of the following four parts. Usually, we should input them in the following order. Here is a simple example with interpretation.

### Instruction (`instruction`)
The specific task you want `GPT` to perform.

> Your task is to translate Chinese into English.

### Context (`context`)
Additional context or background information to guide `GPT` to respond better.

> Please translate these proper nouns according to this glossary.

### Input Data (`input data`)
The actual content input by the user.

> The sentence to be translated is: The sky is blue. (Note: Source says "天空是蓝色的", translated back to English for the example context)

### Output Indicator (`output indicator`)
Specify the output type, format, etc.

> Translation: The sky is blue.

We should organize our prompts in the order of the above four parts. If we regard `GPT` as a person, then when we assign it a task, the interaction is the same as asking a person to help complete a task: first state the task, give background information, then provide specific task information, and finally put forward explicit requirements for the result.

`GPT` is also like a person; it remembers later information more clearly. If you give the specific task first and then explain the background, it may confuse the specific task with the background. Our purpose of asking is to get results, so requirements for results should be placed last. Meanwhile, a repeatedly verified effective technique for asking `GPT` is to describe the instruction at the beginning of the prompt (Your task is to translate Chinese into English), and repeat the instruction in some way at the end (Translation:); this makes it easier for `GPT` to clearly understand and execute our requirements.

After using `GPT` for a while, you may find an obstacle is forgetting the order. But after abbreviating it as "**ICDO**", we never have to worry about it again. Everyone can ask questions following this structured prompt to get more ideal answers.

Of course, in practice we may have noticed that not every question needs to contain these four elements; sometimes they can be omitted as appropriate. This is also similar to human communication; some things need not be repeated.

To make `GPT` efficiently exert its capabilities, we often need to write more complex prompts. At this time, the structured prompt "**ICDO**" is our framework for writing prompts. Here is an example first for a more intuitive understanding. For instance, we ask `GPT` to rewrite a paragraph in Haruki Murakami's style. The prompt is as follows. We can see we gave the task (ask it to rewrite), context (reference sample), input data (paragraph to rewrite), output requirements (ask it to write three versions).

```
You are a writing style master. Your task is to rewrite articles in the style of writer Haruki Murakami. Do not add any new information to the rewritten article.

Here is a prose by Haruki Murakami as a style reference sample: I can accurately state the moment I decided to write a novel; it was around 1:30 PM on April 1, 1978. That day, in the outfield stands of Jingu Stadium, I sat alone drinking beer and watching a baseball game. ... The bat accurately hit the fastball, a crisp sound echoing through the stadium. Hilton quickly ran past first base, easily reaching second. And I decided: "Right, let's try writing a novel." It was at that instant. At that moment, something seemed to quietly flutter down from the sky, and I unmistakably accepted it.

The paragraph you need to rewrite is: Since the advent of AI, we can use it to assist writing. We no longer need to sit at a desk with pen and paper writing furiously. Now, we open a chatbot, converse with it; sometimes I ask, it answers; sometimes it asks, I answer; sometimes it brainstorms... In the conversation with the bot, our ideas gradually "flow" out.

Refer only to the sample style, not the sample content. Please keep the rewritten paragraph length comparable to the original. Please give three rewritten versions in the format: 1. 2. 3. Please rewrite the above paragraph in Haruki Murakami's style.
```
