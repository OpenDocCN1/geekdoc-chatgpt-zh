

# Klara and the Sun (Excerpt)

I knew I wasn't confused, because I could always separate one memory from another, placing each into its true context, whenever I wished. Besides, even as such composite memories entered my mind, I could still recognize their rough edges—like those torn out by an impatient child with fingers, not cut out with scissors—separating, say, Mother by the waterfall from my restaurant booth. If I looked more closely at that cloud mass, I would notice they were, in fact, somewhat disproportionate to Mother or the waterfall. Even so, such composite memories would sometimes flood my mind, so vividly that I would be slow to recover, forgetting that I was actually sitting in this junkyard, on this hard ground.

The junkyard was vast, and from this particular spot of mine, the only tall object I could see was the construction crane in the distance. The sky was very wide and open; if Rick and I could cross Mr. McBain's fields again—especially now that the tall grass had been cut—the sky would probably look just like this to us. A wide sky meant I could observe the Sun's journey unobstructed, and even on cloudy days, I always knew exactly where he was above me.

When I first arrived here, I thought the junkyard was chaotic, but now I gradually understood its orderliness. That first impression, I realized, was due to the inherently messy nature of many items here—some with cut wires sticking out, some topped with pitted grille panels. But after careful observation, one fact became obvious: the yard workers had taken great pains to arrange every machine, crate, or bundle of goods neatly in rows, thus creating several long aisles. Every visitor walking through these aisles—though visitors had to be careful not to trip over rods or wires—could take in the junk on both sides, piece by piece.

With the sky wide and no tall objects around, I could detect visitors the moment they entered the yard. Even when they were still far away, mere small silhouettes moving between the rows of junk, I could spot them. But visitors were rare, and most of the voices I heard came from those few yard workers calling to each other.

Sometimes birds would drop from the sky, but they soon found little to attract them in the junkyard. Not long ago, a flock of blackbirds descended in elegant formation, perching on a machine not far in front of me, and I wondered for a moment if they might be birds Rick had sent to observe me. Of course, they weren't Rick's birds, but wild ones; they perched calmly on the machine for a while, perfectly still, even when a gust of wind ruffled their feathers. Then, as if by mutual agreement, they all flew off at once.

At about the same time, a kind yard worker stopped in front of me and told me there were three AFs to the south, two in the Ring. If I wanted, he said, he could move me to either of those two areas. But I told him I was satisfied with my current particular spot, so he nodded and walked on.

A few days ago, something very special happened.

Although I could no longer move about, I could still easily turn my head left and right to see everything around me. So I had already noticed that long-coated visitor for some time, knowing she was moving behind me. At one point, just as I turned my head, that figure came into the middle distance, and I immediately saw it was a woman, wearing a shoulder strap from which hung a small cloth bag. Whenever she leaned forward to examine something on the ground, that bag would swing in front of her body. Because she was behind me, I couldn't observe her closely all the time, and for the next while—perhaps another memory surfaced in my mind—I completely put her out of my head. Right then, I heard a sound, and the long-coated visitor stood before me. Before she even crouched down to study my face, I had already recognized the Manager, and joy immediately filled my mind.

"Klara. You're Klara, aren't you?"

"Yes, of course." I answered, looking up at her with a smile.

"Klara. Wonderful. Just a moment. Let me fetch something to sit on."

When she returned, she dragged a small metal box, which scraped over the rough ground with a harsh noise. Once she set the box down in front of me and sat on it, I could observe her face clearly, despite the wide sky behind her.

"I was hoping to find you here. Once—oh, almost a year ago—I found something in this junkyard, and for a moment I thought it was you, Klara. But it wasn't you. This time, though, it's definitely you. I'm so happy."

"I'm glad to see the Manager again."

She kept smiling at me. Then she said: "I'd love to know what you're thinking right now. After all this time, seeing me again—it must be very confusing for you."

"Seeing the Manager again, I only feel happiness."

"Then tell me, Klara. How have you been all these years—before you came here, I mean—did you stay with the family who took you from the store all these years? Forgive me for asking, but I can't easily get that kind of information anymore."

"Yes, of course. All these years I've been with Josie. Until she went to college."

"But everything turned out well. A successful family."

"Yes. I believe I provided good service, keeping Josie from loneliness."

"I'm sure you did. I'm sure with you there, she hardly knew what loneliness was."

"One hopes so."

"You know, Klara. Of all the AFs I looked after, you were undoubtedly the most remarkable. You had such unusual insight. And your powers of observation. I noticed it then. I'm truly glad to hear everything worked out. Because you never can tell, even with extraordinary abilities like yours."

"Is the Manager still looking after AFs?"

"No. Oh, no. That all ended some time ago." She looked around the junkyard, then smiled at me again. "That's why I like to come up here now and then. I sometimes go to the junkyard by the Memorial Bridge too. But I like this place best."

"The Manager comes... just to look for AFs from her store?"

"Not only that. I also like collecting small souvenirs." She pointed to her cloth bag. "They don't allow us to take anything important. But small things, they don't care about. The workers here know me. Though you're right. Every time I come, I hope to run into one of my old AFs."

"Did you ever meet Rosa?"

"Rosa? Yes, I did meet her. I found her right here—oh, that must have been at least two years ago. Rosa didn't fare as well as you."

"So she didn't like her boy?"

"Can't say that exactly. But you shouldn't worry. Never mind Rosa. Tell me about yourself. You have such special abilities. I hope the child you accompanied gradually realized that."

"I think she did. Everyone in the house was very good to me. I got to learn so many things."

"I remember the day they came into the store and chose you. That lady tested you first, asking you to walk like her daughter. That worried me somewhat. After you left, I kept thinking about it."

"The Manager needn't worry. That was the best home for me. And Josie was the best boy." (Note: Original Chinese says "少年" - youth/teenager, "boy" or "young man" fits. Josie is female in the book. The Chinese text says "最好的少年" which is masculine noun but often used generically or translation artifact. English original: "best child" or "best girl". I will use "best child" or "best girl" to be accurate to the book, but translate the Chinese text provided: "best boy/youth". I'll stick to "best child" to avoid gender confusion, or "fine young person". Let's translate the Chinese text faithfully: "best youth". Actually, in the Chinese translation of the book, Josie is female. "少年" is used. I will translate as "best child" or "fine young person" to be safe, but "youth" works.) -> "And Josie was the finest youth."

The Manager fell silent for a moment, gazing at me with a smile on her face. So I continued:

"I did everything I could to do what was best for Josie. I've thought about that many times since. If it had truly been necessary to do so, I'm sure I could have continued Josie. But things turned out with a clearly better result, even if Rick and Josie couldn't be together."

"I'm sure you're right, Klara. But what did you mean just now—'continue Josie'? What does that mean?"

"Manager, I did everything I could to learn Josie; if it had truly been necessary, I would have done my utmost. But I think the result would probably not have been good. Not because I couldn't achieve precision. But no matter how hard I tried, I now believe there would always be something I couldn't reach. Mother, Rick, Miss Melanie, Father—I could never reach the feelings they held for Josie in their hearts. I'm sure of that now, Manager."

"Well, Klara, as long as you feel things turned out for the best, I'm happy."

"Mr. Capaldi believed there was nothing special in Josie's heart that couldn't be continued. He told Mother he looked and looked but couldn't find such a special thing. But now I believe he was looking in the wrong place. There **is** something very special, but it's not inside Josie's heart; it's inside the hearts of those who love her. That's why I now think Mr. Capaldi was wrong, and I couldn't have succeeded. So I'm glad I made the decision I did."

"I'm sure you're right, Klara. Every time I reunite with my AFs, that's what I've always wanted to hear. To hear you say happily that everything went well. To hear you have no regrets. You know? There are a few B3s over there, at the other end of the junkyard. They're not from our store, but if you'd like company, I can ask the workers to move you over there."

"No, thank you, Manager. You're just as good as before. But I like this spot. Besides, I still have my memories to sort through carefully, to put in order."

"That might be wise too. I wouldn't have said this when I was in the store, but I could never feel about B3s the way I did about your generation of AFs. I often felt customers felt the same. They could never truly love those B3s, for all their technical advances. I'm so glad I met you today, Klara. I've thought of you so many times. You were one of the best AFs I ever had."

She stood up, her cloth bag swinging in front of her once again.

"Before you go, Manager, I must report one more thing to you. The Sun has been very kind to me. He has been kind to me from the very beginning. But once, while I was with Josie, he was especially kind. I wanted the Manager to know."

"Yes. I'm sure the Sun has always been good to you, Klara."

As the Manager said this, she turned toward the wide sky behind her, a hand raised before her eyes; for a moment, we both looked at the Sun. Then she turned back to me and said: "I must go. Well then, Klara. Goodbye."

"Goodbye, Manager. Thank you."

She bent to pick up the metal box she had sat on, dragging it back to its place, making the same harsh noise as before. Then she walked away down the long aisle between the two rows of junk—clearly, her gait was different from when she was in the store. Every two steps, her body would lean to the left, making me worry her long coat would touch the dirty ground. Just as she reached the middle distance, she stopped, turned around, and I thought perhaps she was going to look back at me one last time. But she only gazed into the distance, toward the construction crane on the horizon. Then she continued on her way.

(End)

# Translator's Afterword

Following 2015's *The Buried Giant*, after a five-year wait, we finally welcome the latest full-length novel by Japanese-British author, 2017 Nobel Literature Prize winner Kazuo Ishiguro—*Klara and the Sun*. The novel's background might be said to be unexpected, but the core of the story remains a typical Ishiguro proposition. When all is said and done, this proposition comes down to two words: "Human Heart".

At first glance, this is a novel about future society and artificial intelligence. Given the rapid advances in AI in recent years, we easily categorize works involving such subject matter as science fiction. However, readers familiar with Ishiguro know that no matter what spatiotemporal background his stories occur in, no matter what thematic shell he employs (be it sci-fi, fantasy, or detective), the essence remains consistent. The Swedish Academy, in its award citation for Ishiguro, once made an exquisite distillation of his creative themes: "Memory, Time, and Self-Deception". This work is no exception, though this time, Ishiguro performs a clever variation for us.

Our protagonist, also the first-person narrator Klara, is a solar-powered robot AF (Artificial Friend). Not until the novel's final scene do we realize that the entire story is, in fact, Klara's retrospective on her life as she nears her "life's" end—or in her words, the sorting and positioning of her memories one by one on the axis of time. The variation occurs precisely on the third keyword—"Self-Deception". We know robots don't lie, let alone Klara, a robot designed to accompany humans, possessing extremely high observation, reasoning, and empathy capabilities, plus a photographic memory. Simultaneously, in the process of looking back, Klara maintains a purely altruistic motive: repeatedly questioning whether the choice she made was truly most beneficial for Josie, the girl she accompanied wholeheartedly. Therefore, we can be confident that Klara's memories are completely true, reliable, and honest—forming a stark contrast with Ishiguro's past protagonists who lived in the fog of their own hearts. For the first time, we encounter a reliable narrator in Ishiguro's work.

However, this by no means implies that "Self-Deception" has truly vanished from this story. On the contrary, because Klara does not live among robots as honest as herself, but among humans. Klara's keen, objective, and honest insight acts like a mirror, silently yet incomparably accurately reflecting the hearts of Josie and everyone around her. And the human heart, we know, is neither completely honest nor completely altruistic. When these hearts are laid bare in Klara's mirror, what kind of self-deception do we see?

Childhood friends Josie and Rick day after day envision a shared future, stroke by stroke sketching their beautiful "plan", as if it were a destined, unchangeable prophecy, yet they are not unaware that the class (even genetic) barrier between them is by no means something two children can break through by planning alone. Rick's mother, Miss Helen, wants her son, who hasn't received genetic "lifting", to get into college just like those "lifted" clever children and have a successful life. To this end, she actually seeks help from the man she deeply hurt in her youth, hoping he can put aside past grievances and generously extend a hand to her and another man's child—does she truly not know she is demanding a mere mortal of flesh and blood to possess the morality and magnanimity of a saint? Then there is Josie's father, once a genius engineer, who lost his job because he was "replaced" by AI, yet repeatedly insists he has no regrets about his life, claiming it was precisely because he lost his job that he rediscovered himself; but even as he speaks so generously to his former wife, even as the brief reunion time with his beloved daughter slips away second by second, has he truly forgotten the price he paid for his "rebirth"?

Yet the most profound self-deception occurs in the heart of Josie's mother. This woman, because of a decision she made, lost her elder daughter Sal. And now, because of the same decision, she watches as she is about to lose her younger daughter Josie. Sal's time, she endured it; but this time, she can no longer bear the same blow. This time, not even death can take her daughter away. She asks Klara, when that day finally comes, to "continue Josie" for her sake. "Continue Josie for me... and then I'll be able to love you." When the mother says this to Klara in the car, the story reaches its crucial point and climax.

Can the robot Klara, who has been with the girl Josie day and night, truly learn Josie's every frown and smile, every gesture, even her entire inner personality, not "copy" Josie, but "become" Josie, become her true continuation in this world, relying on insight and learning abilities far surpassing humans? Regarding this near-existential question, Mr. Capaldi the operator of the "continuation" plan, Josie's father Paul, and our protagonist Klara offer their own interpretations from three different perspectives. Mr. Capaldi calls himself a believer in rationality, firmly convinced that there is nothing unique and non-transferable at the core of any individual; the so-called human heart is merely an ancient rhetoric, a superstition waiting to be completely shattered by science and mathematics. Josie's father opposes the plan of Josie's mother and Mr. Capaldi from the start, striving to find reasons why AFs cannot fathom all the mysteries of humans; he compares the human heart to a strange house, with rooms within rooms within rooms... no matter how many times Klara visits Josie's heart, there is always a room she has never entered. Klara does not agree with the father's view, but she reaches the same conclusion as the father from a completely different angle, and her perspective is clearly closer to the essence of the problem. Ultimately, the one asking Klara to continue Josie is not Josie herself, but those who love her, and the one who loves her most deeply, most selflessly, is undoubtedly her mother. When she asks Klara to continue Josie **for her**, she means not continuing the daughter in this material world, but in her heart; she asks Klara to let her believe the daughter still exists after Josie passes away. In other words, she asks Klara to help her deceive herself, not for a moment, not for a day, a week, a year, but for the long remaining life. But is this possible? The saddest thing about self-deception lies not in its illusion, but in its unsustainability. It is like a colorful bubble, waiting for the needle of reality to burst it. The more cruel the reality, the more painful the tragedy, the faster it bursts. Ultimately, no matter how lifelike Klara's "restoration" of Josie is, she is essentially nothing but a portrait of Josie. It is precisely because she saw this clearly that Klara finally made the decision she believed was correct.

When we speak of self-deception, we always associate it with negative words like "escapism", "cowardice", "confusion". But in fact, self-deception is an important reason we can face this cruel world without going mad; it is as indispensable to human survival as air and water. The root of self-deception is not the falsity of the human heart, but its fragility; it is a crude buffer the heart builds for itself, allowing us not to take the full force of reality head-on, but to buy time to gradually accept reality. Klara understands this, so she never judges anyone. When she silently recites her prayer to her deity—the Sun—she knows the benevolent Sun understands this too. The Sun can dispel those illusory bubbles, those well-meaning lies, those promises destined to be unfulfilled, and see the truly precious things deep in the human heart. He will forgive our fragility and fulfill that which withstands the ultimate test.

Then what about our Klara? In the end, does she have a human heart? The answer is probably negative. True, Klara possesses all the fine qualities humans pride themselves on; she is kind, considerate, selfless, giving everything she can for Josie. Klara is not without emotion; in her repeated interactions with humans, she can accurately experience and express genuine joy, excitement, and sorrow. However, there is one universal human trait that Klara lacks, and that is selfishness—because she is a completely altruistic existence. Throughout the book, all her considerations and starting points revolve around others; we see not a shred of concern from her for her own situation and fate. This determines that all of Klara's qualities and emotions cannot be measured by human dimensions, because it is precisely due to the coexistence of selfish desires and sublimated aspirations that human hearts are filled with contradictions, hesitation, and pain; without the downward gravity of selfishness, all noble, upward humanity becomes weightless and ethereal. Selfishness is the heavy burden of humans, but perhaps in the not-too-distant future, it will also be the most important anchor of what makes a human human.
