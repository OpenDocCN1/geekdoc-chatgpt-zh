

# The Story of Clara and Josie

## Chapter 1: Tidying Up After the Visit

Another silence fell. I could see Josie sitting on the bed, pretending to be utterly absorbed in drawing her next picture. Rick’s shadow I could no longer make out clearly, but I knew he was still there, motionless, leaning back against the modern wardrobe, his gaze passing over me, fixed on the world beyond the rear window.

---

After Rick’s visits ended, Josie would usually toss her sharp pencils, sketchbook, and loose sheets of paper onto the floor in exhaustion, then curl up on the bed to rest. At such times, I would climb down from the button sofa and gather the many objects now scattered across the floor, giving me the chance to glimpse what the two of them had been discussing during the visit.

Though Josie buried her face in the pillow, she wasn’t really asleep; instead, she would lie there with eyes closed, talking incessantly. She was therefore fully aware that I observed her drawings while putting the papers away, and she clearly didn’t mind. In fact, she very likely hoped I would see every single one.

On one occasion, while performing this tidying task, I happened to pick up a sheet of paper; though I only glanced at the image for a split second, I immediately recognized the two most prominent faces in the drawing as likely representing Missy and the long-armed girl from the interaction meeting. There were many inaccuracies in the picture, but Josie’s intent was unmistakable. The two sisters occupied the foreground, wearing unfriendly expressions, while a cluster of other, less defined faces gathered around them. There were no furniture details in the background, but I knew it was the Great Room. There was also a small, featureless creature squeezed into the narrow gap between the two sisters—you would hardly notice it were it not for the large bubble floating above its head. Unlike the drawn Missy and the drawn long-armed girl, this creature lacked typical human features—face, shoulders, arms—and resembled more a droplet of liquid coalescing on the central island countertop by the sink. In fact, without the bubble on its head, a passerby would likely never guess the shape was meant to represent a person. The two sisters completely ignored the droplet-person’s existence, even though it was right beside them. Inside the bubble, Rick had written:

> “Those clever kids think I have no form. But I do. I just keep it hidden. Because who would want them to see it?”

Though I had only glanced at the drawing for a moment, Josie knew I had grasped the meaning, and she asked lazily from the head of the bed:

> “Don’t you think it’s strange for him to write something like that?”

Hearing me chuckle softly and continue tidying, she pressed on:

> “Do you think he believes the person I drew is *him*? That little figure stuck between the two nasties? Do you think that’s *why* he put those words in the bubble?”

> “It’s possible.”

> “But you don’t think so. Right, Clara?” She added, “Clara, are you listening? Answer me. Let me hear your wise opinion?”

> “He’s more likely to think that little figure is Josie.”

She said nothing more, letting me stack the sheets into neat piles and shove them, along with the previous ones, into the space beneath the dressing table. I thought she had fallen asleep, but just then she spoke again:

> “Why do you say that?”

> “It’s just an inference. I believe Rick feels that little figure is Josie. I also believe Rick was trying to express kindness.”

> “Kindness? Where’s the kindness?”

> “I believe Rick is worried about Josie. Worried that she sometimes seems to change in different environments. But in this drawing, Rick is expressing kindness. Because he’s implying that Josie cleverly protected herself and didn’t really change.”

> “So what if I act differently sometimes? Who wants to stay the same forever? Rick’s problem is, whenever I show the slightest side he doesn’t like, he always blames me. That’s because he wants me to stay exactly as I was when we were both little kids.”

> “I don’t think that’s truly what Rick wants.”

> “Then what about what he wrote? No shape, hiding it away? I don’t see any kindness in that. That’s Rick’s problem. He doesn’t want to grow up. At least, his mother doesn’t want him to grow up, and he’s gone along with it. The idea is, he’ll live with his mother forever and ever. Does that help our plans? Every time I show any sign of wanting to grow up, he starts sulking.”

I did not respond. Josie lay there still, eyes closed. After a while, she truly fell asleep, but just before she did, she whispered:

> “Maybe. Maybe he really *was* trying to be kind.”

I kept wondering if Josie would bring up this drawing—or the words in the bubble—during Rick’s next visit. She didn’t, and from this I recognized a rule existing between them: once a drawing, once a line of text placed in a bubble, was committed to paper, it could not be discussed directly again. Perhaps it was precisely this understanding that allowed them to draw and write so freely. Even so, as I said, from the start I felt their bubble game was fraught with peril, and ultimately it was this game that brought Rick’s half-hour visits to an abrupt end.

## Chapter 2: Conflict on a Rainy Afternoon

It was a rainy afternoon, though the pattern of the sun was still faintly visible in the bedroom. The previous few visits had been relatively relaxed, and the atmosphere that day was quite pleasant. The visit had been going on for twenty minutes—the two were playing the bubble game again—when Josie said from the bed:

> “What about the one at the bottom? Not finished yet?”

> “I’m still thinking.”

> “Ricky, the whole point is for you *not* to think. Just write down the very first thought that flashes into your head.”

> “Okay. But this one really needs a bit more thought.”

> “Why? What’s different about this one? Hurry up. I’m almost done with the next one.”

The window glass reflected Rick’s shadow. I saw he was still sitting in his usual spot on the floor, legs curled up to rest the drawing board on his knees, hands hanging by his sides. He stared at the picture before him, a puzzled expression on his face. After a moment, Josie spoke again, her pen still moving busily:

> “You know, there’s something I’ve always wanted to ask. Why doesn’t your mom drive anymore? You still have the car, right?”

> “No one’s started it in years. But, yeah, the car’s still in the garage. Maybe when I get my license, I’ll go over it from top to bottom.”

> “Is she afraid of crashing or something?”

> “Josie, we’ve talked about this.”

> “Yes, but I don’t remember. Is it because she’s too scared?”

> “More or less.”

> “**My** mom’s the opposite. She drives too fast.”

Rick didn’t answer, so she continued:

> “Ricky, still not done filling it in?”

> “I’ll finish. Just wait a bit more.”

> “Not driving is one thing. But does your mom not even care that she has no friends?”

> “She has friends. Mrs. Rivers comes over often. She’s your mom’s friend too, isn’t she?”

> “That’s not what I mean. Everyone has one or two **personal** friends. But your mom—she has no **social**. My mom doesn’t have many friends either. But she *does* have social.”

> “Social? Sounds weird. What’s that mean?”

> “It means when you walk into a shop or get into a taxi, people take you seriously. Treat you right. As long as you have social. It matters, right?”

> “Look, Josie, you know my mom’s health isn’t great sometimes. Don’t talk like it’s all her own choice.”

> “But she *has* made choices, hasn’t she? Like, she made a choice about you. A long time ago.”

> “I don’t know why we’re talking about this.”

> “You want to know what I think, Ricky? If I say the wrong thing, you tell me to shut up. I think your mom has never let you take that step because she wants you all to herself. And now? It’s too late.”

> “I don’t know why we’re talking about this. So what? Besides, who wants social anyway? It won’t interfere with our business.”

> “It *will* interfere with our business, Ricky. First of all, it’ll interfere with our plans.”

> “Listen, I’m trying my best…”

> “But you’re *not* trying, Ricky. You keep talking about our plans, but what are you actually *doing*? Days go by, we get older, things come up. I’m doing everything I can, but you’re not, Rick.”

> “What exactly am I supposed to be doing that I’m not? Going to another one of your interaction meetings?”

> “You should at least try harder. You could do the things we talked about before. Study harder. Try to get into Atlas Brookings.”

> “What’s the point of mentioning Atlas Brookings? I don’t have the slightest chance.”

> “You *do* have a chance, Ricky. You’re smart. Even my mom says you have a chance.”

> “A theoretical chance. Atlas Brookings might blow their own trumpet all they like, but that chance is less than two percent. That’s it. They take less than two percent of non-lifted kids.”

> “But you’re smarter than all those other non-lifted applicants. So why not try? I’ll tell you why. Because your mom wants you to live with her forever. She doesn’t want you to go out and become a real adult. Hey, what are you doing down there? Still not finished? The next drawing’s ready.”

Rick said nothing, just stared at the drawing in his hand. Though Josie claimed it was ready, she kept adding things to the next picture.

> “Anyway,” she went on, “how can it work? Our plans, I mean. If I have social and you don’t, how can it work? My mom drives too fast. But at least she has courage. She messed up with Sal, but after that she found the courage to take that step again with me. That takes courage, right?”

Rick suddenly leaned forward and began writing on the picture. He usually liked to slip a magazine underneath, but this time I saw the paper pressed directly against his thigh, already wrinkling. Yet he kept writing rapidly, then stood up and threw the sharp pencil onto the floor. He didn’t hand the picture to Josie; he tossed it onto the bed, letting it land on the duvet in front of her. Then he backed away, all the way to the door, staring at her the whole time with wide eyes, full of both anger and fear.

Josie turned toward him in surprise. Then she put down her own sharp pencil, reached out, and picked up the sheet. She stared at the paper with blank eyes for a long time, while Rick stood in the doorway watching her.

> “I can’t believe you wrote that,” she finally said. “Why would you do that?”

I turned away on the button sofa, judging that the tension had reached a level where the two could no longer be left completely undisturbed. Perhaps Rick had forgotten my presence, because my movement seemed to startle him. He looked at me, gazed for a moment, his eyes still full of fear and anger, then strode out of the room without a word. We listened to his footsteps descending the stairs.

The moment the front door closed, Josie yawned, threw everything off the bed, and lay flat on her stomach, as if the outcome of this visit was no different from usual.

> “He can be such a pain sometimes.” She spoke into the pillow.

I climbed down from the button sofa and began tidying the room. Josie said nothing more, eyes remaining closed, but I could tell she wasn’t asleep. As I continued my work, I naturally glanced at the sheet that had caused the tension.

As I suspected, the drawing depicted variants of Josie and Rick. There were many inaccuracies, but also enough similarities to identify who Josie intended to portray. The drawn Josie and drawn Rick seemed to float high in the sky, the trees, roads, and houses below tiny as models. Behind them, in a corner of the sky, seven birds flew in formation. The drawn Josie held a larger bird cupped in her two hands, offering it as a special gift to Rick. The drawn Josie wore a broad smile; the drawn Rick looked surprised and delighted.

There was no bubble above the drawn Rick’s head. The only bubble belonged to the drawn Josie’s thoughts, and inside it Rick had written:

> “I wish I could go out, go for a walk, go running, go skateboarding, go swimming in the lake. But I can’t, because my mom has **courage**. So I just lie in bed being ill. And I’m happy about it. I really am happy.”

I slipped this drawing into the stack I held, ensuring it wouldn’t end up on top. Josie remained motionless, silent, eyes closed, but I knew she was awake. In the days before the Morgan’s Falls trip, I might have spoken to her then, and she would have answered honestly. But the atmosphere between us had changed, so I decided to say nothing. I went to the dressing table, bent down, and placed this latest stack into the space beneath, alongside the previous stacks.

## Chapter 3: Waiting and Deciding

The next day and the day after, Rick didn’t come. The housekeeper, Melania, asked, “Where’s that boy? Is he ill?” But Josie just shrugged and said nothing.

Days passed with no sign of Rick; Josie grew quieter, giving off nothing but “go away” signals. She still drew incessantly on the bed, but without Rick and the bubble game, her enthusiasm quickly dwindled; she would often throw half-finished drawings onto the floor and lie stiffly on the bed, staring at the ceiling.

One afternoon, as she lay staring at the ceiling like this, I said to her:

> “If you like, Josie, the two of us could play the bubble game. If Josie wishes to draw, I will do my best to come up with suitable words.”

She kept staring into the middle distance. Then she turned her head and said to me:

> “I’m telling you, it won’t work. I don’t mind you listening. But nothing you say can replace Rick’s. No way.”

> “I understand. I’m sorry. I shouldn’t have suggested…”

> “Yes. You shouldn’t.”

More days passed, Rick still didn’t come; Josie grew increasingly drowsy, and I began to worry she was losing her strength again. An idea leapt into my mind: now was the perfect moment for the Sun to send his special help; I watched him with particular eagerness whenever the pattern he cast into the bedroom suddenly shifted, or when he broke through the clouds and reappeared in the sky. Yet, though he continued to provide his ordinary nourishment tirelessly, that special help of his remained nowhere to be seen.

## Chapter 4: The Special Drawing

One morning, I took Josie’s tray downstairs and returned to the bedroom to find her propped up on pillows, busy with paper and pen—her former enthusiasm seemed to have returned. Not only that, but she wore a deadly serious expression, the likes of which I had never seen on her while drawing. I tried speaking to her, but she didn’t respond. At one point, as I tidied the room and approached the bedside, she shifted her sitting position, preventing me from catching even a glimpse of the paper.

After a while, she tore off the sheet, crumpled it tightly into a ball, and shoved it into a fold of the duvet, wedged between her own body and the wall. Then she started a new drawing from scratch, eyes wide, expression tense. I sat on the button sofa, this time facing her, so she would know I was available to talk whenever she wished.

Nearly an hour later, she put down her sharp pencil and stared at her drawing for a few more moments.

> “Clara? See that bottom drawer on the left down there? Could you get me an envelope? A large padded envelope.”

Just as I reached the drawer and crouched down, I saw Josie raise her sharp pencil again, and from the movement of the tip, I could tell she wasn’t drawing—she was writing. Then she folded the paper down the middle, slipping a blank sheet inside to protect the drawing, took the padded envelope from me, and carefully slid the paper in. She peeled the narrow adhesive strip, sealed the envelope, and pressed the flap down firmly, just in case.

> “All done. Happy now.” She said, turning the envelope over in her two hands as if the action brought her comfort. But just as I turned to leave the bedside, she suddenly held the envelope out to me: “Could you put this back in the drawer you got the envelope from? The bottom left one.”

“Of course.” I took the envelope from her but didn’t head straight for the drawer. Instead, I stood in the center of the room, holding the envelope, looking at her.

> “I was wondering if this drawing might be a special gift from Josie for Rick.”

> “Why do you say that?”

> “Just a guess.”

> “Okay, your guess is right. I thought I’d save this for Rick. For the next time he comes over.”

A silence fell—she watched me, and I wasn’t sure if she was just impatient for me to put the envelope back as requested, or if she was waiting to hear me talk about Rick and his visits. Finally, I said:

> “Perhaps he’ll come again soon.”

> “Perhaps. But there’s no sign of it so far.”

> “I think Rick would be very pleased to see this drawing. He’d see how much extra care Josie took with it.”

> “I didn’t take extra care,” anger flashed in her eyes, “I was just bored, so I drew another picture. That’s all. But you’re right. The picture is for Rick. The problem is, he has to come here first to get it. And he’s never coming back.”

She kept staring at me. I remained standing in the center of the room.

> “Josie,” I said after a pause, “if you wish, I can take this drawing to him.”

Surprise and excitement appeared in her eyes.

> “You mean, you’d take the picture to him? To his house?”

> “Yes. After all, his house is right next door.”

> “I suppose having you deliver it isn’t so strange. Other people’s AFs are always running errands, right?”

> “I’d be very happy to go. I believe I can find the way to his house.”

> “Would you go today? Before lunch?”

> “Whenever Josie wishes. If you like, I can take it to him right now. Immediately.”

> “Do you think that’s a good idea?”

I lifted the padded envelope slightly:

> “I would very much like to deliver Josie’s drawing to Rick. Exploring the outdoors would be good for me. Besides, if Rick accepts this special drawing, perhaps he will forgive Josie and become her best friend again.”

> “What do you mean ‘forgive’? *I* should be the one forgiving *him*. That’s a stupid thing to say, Clara. I don’t want you to take it to him anymore.”

> “I’m sorry. That was my mistake. I don’t fully understand the rules of forgiveness yet. Even so, I still believe taking the drawing to him is the best course. I think he’ll like it.”

The anger vanished from her face.

> “Alright. Go on. Take it.”

Then, just as I turned to leave, she added softly:

> “Perhaps you’re right. I think I *do* need him to forgive me.”

> “I’ll take the drawing to him, and then we’ll see what he does.”

> “Okay.” She smiled faintly. “If he’s rude, you tear the picture up, okay?”

That smile was almost identical to the one she wore before the Morgan’s Falls trip. I smiled back and replied:

> “Let’s hope it doesn’t come to that.”

She flopped back onto the pillow in a playful pose.

> “Right then, off you go. I need a rest now.”

But just as I clutched the padded envelope tight against me, about to leave the bedroom, she spoke again:

> “Hey, Clara?”

> “Yes?”

> “It must be pretty boring, right? Living here with a sickly kid.”

She was still smiling, but I saw the fear beneath the smile.

> “It’s never boring with Josie.”

> “You waited for me at the store for so long. I bet you’re thinking now that you should have gone with a different kid.”

> “I’ve never had that thought. My wish was to be Josie’s AF. That wish has come true.”

> “Yes, but…” she let out a small laugh, heavy with sadness, “that was before you came here. I promised you life here would be wonderful.”

> “I am very happy here. I only want to be Josie’s AF. That is my only wish.”

> “If I get better, we can go out all day together. We can go to the city, see my dad. Maybe he could take us to other cities too.”

> “Those are hopes for the future. But Josie must understand: I will never find a better home than this. Nor a better child than Josie. I am so glad I waited for you. That the Manager let me wait so long.”

Josie considered this. Then she smiled again, a smile full of kindness, with no fear behind it.

> “So we’re friends, right? Best friends.”

> “Yes, of course.”

> “Good. That’s settled. Then remember: don’t let Rick get away with anything.”

I smiled back, then lifted the foam envelope, signaling I would take good care of it.

## Chapter 5: Going to Rick’s House

Melania the housekeeper raised no objection to my going alone to Rick’s house to deliver the picture. Even so, as I crossed the gravel and walked toward the picture-frame door, she stood in the doorway watching me until I stepped onto the first patch of field, then turned and went back inside.

I followed the trampled path, the surface quickly becoming unpredictable, uneven underfoot. The wild grass reached my shoulders, and fear entered my mind: I would get lost here. However, this section of the field was divided into neat, orderly squares, so as I moved from one square to the next, I could clearly see the remaining squares lined up ahead of me. The tall grass was less cooperative, constantly springing up from the sides to block my path, but I quickly learned to overcome even this difficulty—by extending one arm. If I could free both arms, I would walk faster, but of course I had to hold Josie’s envelope in one hand, unwilling to risk damaging it. Just then, the tall grass around me disappeared, and I stood before Rick’s house.

Observing from a distance earlier, I had already seen that Rick’s house was not as grand as Josie’s. Now I saw that many of the white-painted wooden boards on the exterior had grayed—some even turning a muddy brown—and three windows were just black rectangular holes, with neither curtains nor blinds. I climbed a flight of wooden steps, each board warping under my feet; then I stepped onto a platform made of more such boards, the gaps between them wide enough to see the mud below. A refrigerator stood beside the front door, pushed to one side, its back fully exposed to passersby; I could see how spiders had made homes in the intricate metal framework. I paused to observe their delicate webs, and just then the front door opened—though I hadn’t pressed any button—and Rick walked out onto the platform.

> “I’m sorry,” I hurried to say. “I don’t mean to disturb you. I’ve come on an important errand.”

He didn’t seem angry, but he didn’t speak either, just kept looking at me.

> “AFs often carry important errands,” I continued. “Josie sent me to deliver this.” I held up the envelope.

Excitement flashed across Rick’s face, then vanished just as quickly.

> “Then you’ve come to the right place,” he said.

Perhaps he expected me to simply hand over the envelope and leave. But I had anticipated this and showed no sign of letting it go. We stood there on the platform, facing each other, listening to the wind whistle through the gaps in the boards.

> “In that case,” he said finally, “I suppose you’d better come in. But be warned. It’s not very nice in here.”
