
The night deepened, the clamor of the theater crowd fading into a blur, but I knew my observational faculties had not been too severely impaired, for I could still clearly see the lit-up little restaurant we were approaching. I could make out its shape like a slice of pie, the pointed end aimed at us; and how the street forked on either side of it, the restaurant windows running the length of both branching sidewalks, so that whichever path pedestrians took, they could peer through the windows into the brightly lit interior—seeing the gleaming leather booths, the polished tabletops, and a bright, transparent counter; behind the counter, the manager stood wearing a white apron and a white cap, awaiting customers.

At this moment, no vehicles drove down the road, the surrounding buildings were pitch black, and this little restaurant was the sole light source in the area, casting slanted shadows onto the paving stones. I wondered which fork Mr. Vance would choose, but as we drew nearer, I noticed a door right at that pointed corner. The only reason I hadn't noticed it before, I supposed, was that the door looked too much like the restaurant windows—it was mostly glass, with lettering painted on it. Mr. Vance pulled the door open and stood aside to let Miss Helen enter first.

Moments later, when I followed Rick into the restaurant, I found the light inside so glaring and yellow that for a moment I couldn't adjust. Gradually, I began to distinguish the slices of fruit pie displayed inside that transparent counter, each slice shaped just like the restaurant itself, and that manager—a tall, dark-skinned man—standing motionless behind the counter, his face turned not toward me but constantly twisted away. I realized then that for the duration of Mr. Vance and Miss Helen selecting a booth and sitting opposite each other, he had been watching the two of them the whole time.

I watched Rick's figure cross the shiny floor and sit down beside his mother. At that moment, Josie's parting words returned to my mind, and I couldn't help wondering what momentous matters Mother would discuss with her in the friend's apartment, and why I could not be present.

It took me a while to reach the three of them, during which Miss Helen and Mr. Vance had been silently gazing at each other the entire time. I felt I didn't know Mr. Vance well enough to sit beside him. Besides, he had sat right in the middle of the two-seater; it was obvious that if I sat there, it would make him uncomfortable. So I didn't choose that spot, but sat alone in a nearby booth across the aisle.

Mr. Vance finally stopped looking at Miss Helen; he twisted in his seat and called out his order loudly to the manager. Only then did I realize that despite there being no other customers besides us, all the tables and chairs were still meticulously set, ready for any other patrons who might walk in. I thought then that perhaps this manager was lonely too—at least for this stretch of time, lonely guarding his restaurant, both street-facing fronts brightly lit, beckoning to every passerby in the night.

"Sir?" Rick was speaking. "I very much appreciate you taking time out of your busy schedule to see me. And thank you for even being willing to consider helping me."

"You know, Rick," Mr. Vance said, as if dreaming, "it's been a good long while since I've seen your mother."

"I understand, sir. I also understand you've never met me before, except for a brief glimpse when I was about two. Which makes your generosity all the more remarkable, agreeing to meet me under these circumstances. Then again, Mom always said what a generous person you are."

"It's gratifying to hear your mother's been speaking well of me. Perhaps she told you a negative thing or two?"

"Oh, no. My mother only ever had good things to say about you."

Is that so? All these years I thought... ah, never mind. Helen, I've already revised my opinion of this son of yours."

Miss Helen had been observing Mr. Vance closely. "You probably know without my saying, Vance, that I'm equally grateful. I'd love to go on and on expressing my thanks, but this is Rick's opportunity, so I don't intend to speak for him."

"Well said, Helen. So then, Rick, why don't you tell us what this meeting is about?"

"Well, I'm not sure where to start, so I'll just say whatever comes to mind. I have a strong interest in drone technology. You could call it an obsession. I've been developing my own system, and now I have my own fleet of mechanical birds..."

"Wait a minute. When you say 'your own system,' Rick, are you saying you've surpassed all previous achievements?"

Panic flickered across Rick's face, then he glanced over at me. I smiled at him, trying my best to convey one thing with my expression: this smile was not just mine, but also Josie's. Whether he understood that or not, he seemed encouraged.

"No, sir, you can't put it like that," he said with a light laugh. "I'm not claiming to be a genius. But I will say my drone system **is** entirely my own design, without any mentor's help. I used various information sources I found online. My mother has been very supportive too, buying several expensive reference books. In fact, I brought a few sketches with me, in case you wanted a general idea. Here. Though I don't feel I've made any breakthrough achievements; I also know that without proper guidance, that kind of achievement is impossible for me."

"I hear you. So now, you intend to get into a good university. To fully realize your talent."

"More or less. My mother and I both thought perhaps Atlas Brookings, as an inclusive and free university..."

"Inclusive and free to the extent of being open to all genuinely talented students, even those who haven't benefited from genetic editing technology."

"Exactly, sir."

"And no doubt, Rick, you also understand—because your mother probably told you—that I'm currently the Chairman of the Founding Committee of that university. Meaning, I control the body that awards the scholarships."

"Yes, sir. That's what she told me."

"Now, Rick. I hope your mother wasn't implying there's any backdoor situation in the Atlas Brookings selection process."

"Neither my mother nor I intend to seek your help through the backdoor. I only ask you to extend a hand in one situation: if you believe I deserve a place at Atlas Brookings."

"Well said. Right, then let's take a look at what you've got."

Rick had already placed his notebook on the table, and Mr. Vance reached out and opened it. He stared at the diagram on the open page for a while, then turned to the next page, saw another diagram, and seemed to become gradually absorbed. He continued turning pages slowly, one by one, occasionally flipping back to a previous one. At one point, he murmured, his head still buried in the notebook:

"All these represent your future creative plans?"

"By and large, yes. Though some designs I've already realized. Like the one on the next page."

Miss Helen watched quietly from the side, a gentle smile on her face, her gaze shifting from Mr. Vance to Rick's notebook. In that moment, I felt it again—fleeting, yet vividly real—my head cradled at the specified angle in Father's hand, hearing the *drip, drip, drip* of liquid flowing into the plastic bottle held in his other hand, pressed against my face.

"Now, Rick," Mr. Vance said, "I'm quite ignorant about these things. Even so, I can sense your drones have very strong surveillance capabilities."

"These mechanical birds can collect data, yes. But that doesn't necessarily mean they have to be used for privacy-invading activities. They have many potential uses. Security, even childcare. Then again, there are some people we really need to be on guard against."

"Criminals, you mean."

"Or paramilitary groups. Or weird cults."

"I understand. Yes, all very interesting. You don't see any moral issues here, do you?"

"I'm sure, sir, there are all sorts of moral issues. But ultimately, the people who decide how to regulate these things are legislators, not people like me. Right now, I just want to learn as much as I can, to raise my level of understanding another notch."

"Well said." Mr. Vance nodded, and went back to examining Rick's notebook.

That lonely manager had by now approached with his tray, setting drinks on the table before Miss Helen, Mr. Vance, and Rick. They each lowered their voices to thank him, after which he turned and walked away.

"You have to understand, Rick," Mr. Vance said, "I'm not trying to give you a hard time. I'm just, well, testing you a little. Sizing you up." He turned to Miss Helen: "So far, he's done remarkably well."

"Vance, dear. Would you like something with that coffee? I see there are doughnuts over there. How about one? You always used to love doughnuts."

"Thank you, Helen, but I have a dinner engagement tonight." He glanced at his watch, then faced Rick again: "Now, think about this, Rick. Atlas Brookings believes there are many gifted children outside its gates, just like you, who for economic or other reasons have never received an AGE boost. Our college also believes society is making a grave mistake by not allowing these children's talents to flourish. Unfortunately, most other institutions don't share our view. Which means we receive far more applications than we can accept, and all from children just like yourself. We can filter out the hopeless ones, but after that, frankly, it's pretty much a lottery. Now, Rick. You just said you don't want a backdoor. So let me ask you a question. If that's really the case, **then why am I sitting here in front of you right now?**"

The moment the words left his mouth, Mr. Vance's expression changed abruptly, and I almost cried out involuntarily. Rick looked equally startled. Only Miss Helen didn't seem surprised; rather, she looked as if she had been waiting for something she'd been dreading. She smiled faintly and spoke:

"I'll answer that for him, Vance. Yes, we **are** asking you to look out for us. We know you have that authority. So we are asking for your help. Let me rephrase. **It is I** who am asking you. I'm asking you to help my child, to give him a fighting chance in this world."

"Mom..."

"No, Rick dear, that's how it is. The one asking Vance is me, not you. And we **are** asking him to open a backdoor. Naturally."

I had been mistaken in thinking we were the manager's only customers. Now I realized that three booths away, a forty-two-year-old woman was sitting alone. I hadn't seen her before because she had been pressed right against the window, her forehead practically touching the glass, staring out into the dimness. I wondered if the manager had failed to notice her too, and if her loneliness had deepened, believing the manager was deliberately snubbing her.

"You know, Helen," Mr. Vance said, "you're adopting a strange strategy right now. A backdoor, like any other form of corruption, works best when it's not spelled out. But let's leave that aside." Mr. Vance leaned forward. "Just now I thought it was Rick asking for my help. That's one thing. He's a most unusual and likable young man. Things were going smoothly. Then look what you just did. You tell me this is actually about me doing a favor for *you*—you, Helen. After all these years. All these years you never returned my messages. So many minutes, so many hours, so many days, so many months, so many years I've spent thinking of you."

"Do you have to say this here? In front of Rick?" Miss Helen was still smiling gently, but her voice trembled.

"Rick's a smart kid. He's the one who ultimately faces the success or failure. So why hide it from him? Let him see the full picture. Let him see what this is really about."

Once again, Rick's gaze crossed the aisle to me; once again, I tried to return an encouraging smile, a smile that was both mine and Josie's.

"But what is this really about, Vance?" Miss Helen asked. "Is it really that complicated? I'm just asking you to help my son. If you're not willing, we can politely part ways, and that's the end of it."

"Who said I don't want to help Rick? I can see he's a gifted young man. These sketches show real promise. I have every reason to believe he could thrive at Atlas Brookings. The problem is, right now, **it's you asking me, Helen**."

"Then I never should have spoken. Before I opened my mouth, everything was going swimmingly. I could see you two hitting it off, Rick speaking to you with genuine respect. But the moment I stepped in, the trouble started."

"Trouble? You bet there's trouble, Helen. Twenty-seven years' worth. Twenty-seven years you've refused any contact with me. I wasn't harassing your mother, Rick. I don't want you thinking that. At first, I was—well, let's say my tone might have been a bit emotional. But I never harassed her, never threatened her, never accused her. I only pleaded. Is that fair, Helen? A fair description?"

"Perfectly fair. You were persistent, but there was never any unpleasantness. But Vance, does this have to be said in front of Rick?"

"Alright. I'll grant you that. Maybe I should stop here. Maybe it's your turn to say a few words, Helen."

"Sir? I don't know what happened in the past. But if you feel there's anything inappropriate about our request..."

"Wait, Rick," Mr. Vance said. "I want to help you. But I feel it's time we gave your mother a chance to explain her behavior."

For several seconds, no one spoke. I looked at the manager, wondering if he was listening, but he just stared into the gloom outside his window, showing no sign of having heard anything of interest.

"I admit," Miss Helen said, "I treated you very badly in the past, Vance. I accept that. But then again, I treated myself badly, and just about everyone else badly too. You mustn't feel I was singling you out. My badness was evenly distributed."

"Perhaps. But I'm not just anyone. We lived together for five years..."

"Yes. And I really do want to apologize. Sometimes, Vance—and Rick, I don't mind saying this in front of you—I often wish I could gather everyone, every single person I've wronged, and have them line up in a long queue. Then I'd walk down the line, you know, like a monarch. One by one, shaking each hand, looking into each pair of eyes, saying: I'm sorry, I really was terrible."

"Lovely. So now I have to stand in that queue. What an honor to receive Her Majesty's apology."

"Oh god, that came out terribly. I was just trying to express... express how I feel. I knew that phrasing made it sound awful. But when I look back, I really find it unbearable, so I think, if only there were some solution like that. If I were Queen, then yes, I could..."

"Mom, really, I know what you mean. But maybe that's not the best way..."

"Once, you **were** a Queen, Helen. A beautiful Queen. Back then, you thought you could do anything with impunity. I'm a little sad, but also a little glad. Glad you didn't get away with it. Glad it all caught up with you in the end, that you finally have to pay the price."

"And what price have I paid, Vance? You mean my poverty? I ask because I don't mind that so much, you know."

"Maybe you don't mind poverty, Helen. But you've become fragile. And for that, I think you mind a great deal more."

Miss Helen fell silent for a few more seconds, while Mr. Vance kept his eyes wide open, fixed on her. Finally, she spoke: "Yes. You're right. Over these years, compared to the days when we knew each other, I've become fragile. So fragile a gust of wind might blow me apart. I lost my beauty, not because of time, but because of this fragility. But Vance, dear Vance. Won't you even partially forgive me now? Won't you help my son? Vance. I'd give you anything, everything, only I can't think what I have left to give. Nothing. Just this plea. So I beg you, Vance, beg you to help him."

"Mom, please. Don't. Please don't..."

"You see my predicament, Rick. I don't quite understand what your mother means right now. She says she wants to apologize, but for what? It's all too vague. I think, Helen, perhaps we'd better stick to concrete details."

"I'm just asking you to help my son, Vance. Isn't that concrete enough?"

"Details, Helen. For instance, that night at Miles Martin's house. You know which night I mean."

"Yes, yes. That night I told everyone you hadn't read the Jenkins Report..."

"Your remark brought the house down, at my expense, Helen. And you knew exactly what you were doing."

"Then, Vance, I apologize for that night. I lost control, I wanted revenge. I hoped..."

"Another detail. No particular order, just whatever comes to mind. That voicemail you left for me at that hotel. In Portland, Oregon. Did you think those words weren't hurtful?"

"Deeply hurtful. A despicable, loathsome message. I haven't forgotten it. I... I can still hear it in my head, it always bursts in when I least expect it. One second I'm enjoying a moment of peace, the next, there I am, picking up the phone in my mind, leaving you that message again, only this time I change it. I edit it, so the words aren't so cruel. Because I never actually heard the message myself, I sometimes feel it's not too late to fix it. I can't help it, it's a little trick my brain plays on me, and then that awful feeling comes back. Believe me, Vance, I've punished myself countless times for that message. And you have to understand, back then, I didn't know how to technically delete a message after leaving it..."

"Mom, enough. Sir? I think this is bad for my mother's health. She's been doing well lately, but..."

Miss Helen touched Rick's arm, signaling him to stop. "Vance, I want to apologize," she continued. "I want to beg. I want to say I treated you badly in the past; if you wish, I can swear to you I will punish myself, keep punishing myself, until I've made amends for all of it."

"Mom, let's go. This isn't good for you."

"If you like, Vance, we can arrange to meet again. Say, in two years, right here. Then you can verify whether I've kept my promise. You can examine me from head to toe, check if I've properly punished myself..."

"Enough, Helen. If Rick weren't here, I'd very much like to tell you what I think of that."

"Sir? I don't want your help at all. I want nothing more to do with this right now."

"No, Rick, you don't know what you're saying," Miss Helen said. "Don't listen to him, Vance."

Mr. Vance stood up, saying, "I have to go."

"Mom, please calm down. This isn't that important."

"You don't know what you're saying, Rick! Vance, don't go yet! We can't part like this. You used to love doughnuts. Won't you have one now?"

"I agree with Rick. This isn't doing you any good, Helen. The best thing is for me to leave. Rick? I liked those sketches, and I like you. Take care of yourself. Goodbye, Helen."

Mr. Vance walked away down the aisle between the two rows of booths, not looking back at any of us; then he passed through that glass door and stepped out into the darkness outside. Miss Helen and Rick remained sitting shoulder to shoulder, heads bowed, staring at the blank space on the table before them. Then Rick spoke: "Clara. Come up here and sit with us."

"I was thinking," Miss Helen said.

Rick leaned close, an arm around her shoulder: "What are you thinking, Mom?"

"I was wondering if what just happened was enough. If it could satisfy him."

"Honestly, Mom. If I'd known things would go even slightly in this direction, I never would have agreed."

I slipped into the seat Mr. Vance had vacated, but neither Miss Helen nor Rick looked up at me. I looked at Miss Helen, thinking of how she and Mr. Vance had once fallen in love, deeply, madly. I couldn't help wondering if the Miss Helen and Mr. Vance of those days treated each other the way Josie and Rick do now. And if someday, Josie and Rick might turn on each other with that same coldness. I thought again of Father in the car talking about the human heart, how complex it is, and I saw him standing in the yard, right in front of the low-hanging sun, his figure and his evening shadow merging into a single elongated shape, while his hand reached up to unscrew the protective cap from the nozzle of the Cootings machine, and I stood anxiously behind him, holding that plastic water bottle, the bottle containing that precious solution.

"What happened just now?" Miss Helen asked. "What will Vance do next? Will he help? He could at least have told us, whatever he decides."

"Excuse me," I said, "I don't want to raise false hopes. But based on my observation, I believe Mr. Vance will decide to help Rick."

"You really think so?" Miss Helen asked. "Why?"

"I could be wrong. But I believe Mr. Vance still likes Miss Helen very much, and therefore will decide to help Rick."

"Oh, you dear robot! I so hope you're right. I didn't know what else I could do just now."

"Mom, to hell with him. I'll manage fine either way."

"He's not nearly as ugly as I imagined." Miss Helen said, gazing out at the dark, empty street. "In fact, he's not ugly at all. I just wish he'd told us. Whatever he decides."
