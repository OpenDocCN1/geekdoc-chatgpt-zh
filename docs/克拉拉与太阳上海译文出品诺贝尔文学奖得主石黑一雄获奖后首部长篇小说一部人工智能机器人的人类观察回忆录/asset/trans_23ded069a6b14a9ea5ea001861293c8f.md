
After a while I decided that sitting in that chair waiting for the Sun wasn't an impolite thing to do. When I sat down, I fully anticipated that due to the change in angle, my surroundings should present a picture different from before, but the result still shocked me: everything before me hadn't just been readjusted, but had been fragmented again into different zones—and not just the usual squares, but many irregular shards. Inside some shards, I could see parts of Mr. McBain's farm tools—a spade handle, the lower half of a metal ladder. In another shard, I knew what I was seeing was the rims of two plastic buckets placed side by side, but perhaps due to poor lighting conditions, they appeared only as two intersecting ovals.

I knew the Sun would soon be beside me; although I sometimes felt I should stand up, as if greeting a customer, another voice told me that as long as I sat still, I would actually avoid excessive presumption and disturbance, and be less likely to provoke the Sun's displeasure. So I made my form cling as closely as possible to the shape of the folding chair, and waited quietly. The Sun's beams became increasingly distinct, the orange-yellow color increasingly vivid, and I even felt these beams might be causing hay chaff to peel off the haystack and float into the air, because there were clearly many more particles suspended before my eyes now.

Then I had a thought: if my judgment was correct, if the Sun was passing by Mr. McBain's barn on the way to his true resting place, then I couldn't be overly courteous. I had to seize the opportunity boldly, otherwise my efforts—and Rick's help—would all come to nothing. So I gathered my thoughts and began to speak. I didn't actually speak those words aloud, because I knew the Sun had no need for words in the ordinary sense. But I hoped to express myself as clearly as possible, so I rapidly and silently formed those words—or something like them—in my mind.

"Please make Josie well. Just as you made the Beggar Man well."

I lifted my head slightly, and among the fragments of farm tools and stacks of hay, I saw a section of a traffic light, and half a wing of Rick's mechanical bird. At that moment, the Manager's voice echoed in my mind: "That's impossible." And the boy AF Rex's voice: "You're so selfish, Klara." So I spoke again:

"But Josie is just a child. She hasn't done anything unkind."

Then I remembered the trip to Morgan Falls, the mother's closely scrutinizing eyes across the picnic table, and that bull, glaring at me as if I had no right to pass before his field; I immediately realized I might have angered the Sun, because I had intruded before him in just this way, and precisely at the moment he needed rest. I formulated an apology in my mind, but the shadows in the barn were stretching longer now; if I spread my fingers before my eyes at this moment, I knew their shadows would stretch all the way back to the entrance. Obviously, the Sun was unwilling to make any promise concerning Josie, because despite his compassion, he still could not distinguish Josie from other humans, and some of those people, because of their pollution and inconsideration, had greatly angered him; suddenly I felt how foolish I was, to come to such a place and make such a request. The orange light filling the barn grew even more intense, and I saw Rosa again, sitting on the hard ground, a pained expression on her face, reaching out to touch her stiff leg. I bowed my head low, curling my body as small as possible within the shape of the folding chair, but then I immediately remembered that any chance to appeal to the Sun was fleeting; so I plucked up courage, spoke in the form of half-words, pushing this speech to flash through my mind:

"I understand how abrupt and rude it is for me to come here. The Sun has every reason to be angry with me, and I fully understand you may not even wish to consider my request. Even so, given your great compassion, I thought perhaps I might still ask you to delay your journey a little longer. To hear another proposal. Suppose I could do something special to please you. Something that would make you especially happy. If I could do that, then, in return, would you be willing to show Josie special kindness? Just as you helped the Beggar Man and his dog that time?"

As these words flashed through my mind, a distinct change occurred around me. The red light in the barn was still thick, but now it had an almost gentle quality—so much so that the many shards still fragmenting my surroundings seemed to float in the Sun's final rays. I saw the lower half of the glass display cart—I recognized its small casters—drifting slowly upward until it was obscured by an adjacent shard; I looked up, gazing all around, but could no longer see any trace of that terrifying bull. At that moment, I understood I had won a crucial advantage, but must not waste a single instant, so I pressed on, no longer bothering to formulate half-words, because I knew I had no time left.

"I know how much the Sun hates pollution. Know how much it saddens and angers you. Look, I have seen and confirmed that pollution-making machine. Suppose I could manage to find that machine and destroy it. End its pollution. Then, in return, would you be willing to consider giving Josie special help?"

The light in the barn dimmed then, but it was a friendly dimness; soon the shards vanished, the fragmentation indoors disappeared. I knew the Sun had moved on again, so I rose from the folding chair and for the first time walked toward the open back of Mr. McBain's barn. Then I saw how the fields stretched toward the middle distance until they met a line of trees—a kind of soft fence—and just behind that line of trees, a weary, no longer scorching Sun was gradually sinking into the earth. The sky was turning into night, stars faintly visible, and I could see the Sun smiling kindly at me as he sank into his resting place.

Out of gratitude and respect, I continued standing at the open back of the barn until his last glimmer disappeared beneath the ground. Then I passed through the dim interior of Mr. McBain's barn and exited the way I had come.

---

When I re-entered the meadow, the tall grass swayed gently around me. Crossing the field in the dark was a daunting task, but what had just happened buoyed me up so much I hardly felt a shred of fear. Even so, the uneven ground reminded me of the dangers ahead, so when I suddenly heard Rick's voice not far away, I was gladdened.

"Is that you, Klara?"

"Where are you?"

"Over here. To your right. I didn't listen to you, didn't go straight home." I walked in the direction of the voice, the grass gradually thinning, and found myself standing in a forest clearing again. This clearing—a small circular area—looked like the work of a vacuum cleaner, the grass inside once again only ankle-high, a crescent moon hanging high in the night sky above me. Rick was sitting there, seemingly on the ground, but as I approached, I saw he was sitting on a large half-buried rock. He smiled at me calmly.

"Thank you for waiting for me," I said.

"Just selfishness. Afraid you'd get stuck here all night, malfunction. That would be big trouble for me, since I'm the one who brought you here."

"I believe Rick waited for me out of kindness. I am very grateful."

"Did you find what you were looking for after you went in?"

"Oh, yes. At least, I believe I did. And I believe we now have reason to hope. Hope for Josie. Hope that she will get well. But I have a task to complete first."

"What kind of task? Maybe I can help."

"I'm sorry, I can't discuss this with Rick. I believe what was reached tonight is an agreement. A covenant, you could say. If I speak of it without reservation, it might jeopardize the fulfillment of the contract."

"Okay. I don't want to jeopardize anything. But if there's anything you think I can do for you..."

"I will tell you frankly. The most important thing Rick can do is strive to get into Atlas Brookings University. Then Josie and Rick can continue side by side, and the wish expressed in that kind painting will still have a chance of coming true."

"God, Klara, my mom's obviously doing your job for you. She makes it sound so easy. But you have no idea how much it takes for a kid like me to get into a place like that. And even if I succeed, what about my mom? Am I just supposed to leave her here alone?"

"Miss Helen may be stronger than Rick thinks. And even without being lifted, Rick has a special talent. If he gives it his all, I believe he will be accepted by Atlas Brookings. Besides, Miss Helen said she has a secret weapon to help him."

"Her secret weapon? Just some jerk she knows who helps run the place. An old lover of hers. I want nothing to do with him. Listen, Klara, we should go back."

"You're right. We've been out a long time. Miss Helen will be worried. Also, if I can get back home before Josie's mother comes in, I can avoid a lot of awkward questions."

---

Two days later, when the doorbell rang around ten in the morning, Josie still seemed to be guessing who it was; she got up, hurried out of her bedroom, onto the landing. I followed her out, and when Rick walked past Housekeeper Melania into the hallway, Josie turned to look at me, an excited smile on her face. But then, just as she headed for the top of the stairs, she put on a completely expressionless face.

"Hey, Melania," she called down, "do you know who this weirdo is?"

"Hello, Josie." Rick looked up at us, a shy smile on his face. "I heard a rumor: maybe we're friends again."

Josie sat down on the top step; though I was behind her, I could clearly see she was now wearing her most benevolent smile.

"Oh, really? How strange. Wonder who started that."

Rick's own smile grew confident then. "Just gossip, I guess. By the way, I really like that painting. Framed it last night."

"Really? With one of those frames you make yourself?"

"Honestly, I used one of my mom's old frames. We have so many old frames lying around everywhere. I took out a zebra photo and swapped yours in."

"Good swap."

Housekeeper Melania went to the kitchen then, and Rick and Josie just stood there, one at the bottom of the stairs, one at the top, grinning at each other. Then Josie must have given a signal, because both moved with sudden speed—she stood up, he reached for the banister.

Just as they walked into the bedroom together, I remembered Housekeeper Melania's previous instruction, so I followed them in. For a while after that, everything seemed like a return to the old days: I sat on the button sofa, facing the rear window, Rick and Josie behind me, laughing and talking about old foolishness. At one point, I heard Josie say:

"Hey, Rick. I was wondering, is this the right way to hold it?" By the reflection in the window glass, I saw she was holding a table knife left over from breakfast. "Or should it be like this?"

"**How** would **I** know?"

"I thought you might know, you're British, aren't you? My chemistry professor says you should hold it **like this**. But what does she know?"

"What do **I** know? Why do you keep saying I'm British? I never actually lived there, you know."

"You used to say it yourself, Rick. Two years ago, or three? You kept insisting how British you were."

"Did I say that? Must have been a phase."

"Oh yes, lasted several months. You were full of 'pardon me this' and 'excuse me that.' So I thought you might know how to hold a knife."

"But why would British people know better than anyone else?"

After a few minutes, I heard Rick walk around the bedroom, then say:

"You know, one reason I like this room so much? It smells like you, Josie."

"What? I can't believe you'd say that!"

"I mean it in the best possible way."

"Rick, you really can't say that to a girl!"

"I don't usually say it to girls. I only say it to you."

"Excuse me, what did you say? So I'm not a girl now?"

"Hey, **usually** don't say it to girls. What I mean is—I just mean—I haven't been here in a while, so I'd forgotten everything about this room. How it looks, how it smells."

"God, you're annoying, Rick."

But there was laughter in her voice; after a pause, Rick continued:

"At least we're not angry with each other anymore. I'm happy."

After another silence, Josie spoke: "Me too. I'm happy too." Then she added: "Sorry I kept going on before, saying all that stuff about your mom and everything. She's a good person, I didn't mean those things. And sorry I keep being sick. Making you worry."

By the reflection in the window glass, I saw Rick step closer to Josie, put one arm around her. Then, a moment later, his other arm went around her too. Josie let him hold her, though she didn't raise her own arms to return the embrace, as she usually did when saying goodbye to her mother.

"Can you smell me properly now?" she asked after a while.

Rick didn't answer that, but said: "Klara? You there?"

When I turned, the two had separated slightly, both looking at me.

"What is it?"

"Maybe you should, you know—avoid disturbing us, like you always say."

"Oh, yes."

The two watched me get off the button sofa and walk past them. At the door, I turned and said:

"I've been trying to avoid disturbing you. Just still worried about foolishness inside." Seeing their confused faces, I continued: "Someone instructed me to ensure no foolishness occurred. So I stayed in the room, even when you were playing the bubble game."

"Klara," Josie said, "Rick and I aren't planning to have sex, okay? We just have some things to say to each other, that's all."

"Okay, of course. Then I'll withdraw."

With that I left the room, out onto the landing outside, gently closing the door behind me.

---

In the following days, I often thought of that Cootings machine, and how I might find and destroy it. I tested various excuses in my mind, seeing which one would let me accompany Mother into the city, and once in the city, allow me freedom of movement for long enough—but none of them sounded the least bit credible. Josie noticed I was often distracted and would say to me: "Klara, you're spacing out again. Maybe your solar's low." I even considered confiding in Mother, but immediately rejected the option, because I felt she would neither understand nor believe the agreement I had reached. But just then, before I could take any action myself, an opportunity arose spontaneously.

One evening, just an hour after the Sun had gone to bed, I was in the kitchen, standing by the refrigerator, listening to its reassuring hum. The ceiling light wasn't on yet, so I just stood there in the little light cast from the hallway. Mother had returned from the office not long before, and I had come down to the kitchen to let her and Josie be undisturbed in the bedroom upstairs. After a while, I heard her footsteps on the stairs, then walking toward the kitchen. Her figure appeared in the doorway, making the kitchen even darker, and she spoke:

"Klara, I want to give you advance notice. After all, this concerns you."

"What is it?"

"Next Thursday, I'm taking the day off work. I'm driving Josie into the city, and we're staying overnight. We were just talking about it. Josie has an appointment."

"An appointment?"

"You know, Josie's been having her portrait painted for a while. Remember those times she went to your store? That's why we went to the city. It's been interrupted a long time because of her health, but she has a bit of strength back now, so I want her to go to the studio again. Mr. Capaldi's been very patient, just waiting all this time."

"I understand. Will Josie need to sit still for a long time?"

"Mr. Capaldi has his methods, he won't tire her out. He can take photos first, then work from them. Even so, he still needs her to visit occasionally. I'm telling you this because this time I want you to accompany Josie. I think she'd rather have you with her."

"Oh, yes. I would be very happy to."

Mother walked a few more steps into the kitchen, and now I could see the hallway's dim light only illuminated one edge of her face.

"When she goes in to see Mr. Capaldi, I want you, Klara, to stay by her side. In fact, Mr. Capaldi is dying to meet you. He's very interested in AFs. Obsessed, you could say. You don't mind, do you?"

"Of course not. I look forward to meeting Mr. Capaldi."

"He might have a few questions for you. Related to his research. Because, as I said, he's obsessed with AFs. You don't mind?"

"No, of course not. And I believe a trip to the city will be good for Josie, now that she has a little strength back."

"Good. Oh, right, we'll likely have a passenger too. In our car, I mean. Our neighbor needs a lift."

"Rick and Miss Helen?"

"They have business in the city too, and she doesn't drive anymore. Don't worry, everyone fits. You won't have to squeeze in the trunk."

Just the following Sunday, around two or three in the afternoon, Rick not only came to visit himself but brought his mother along; taking this opportunity, I learned more about the trip. I went out onto the landing again, so as not to disturb Rick and Josie in the bedroom. I leaned on the railing, looking down the hallway, and could hear Mother and Miss Helen's laughter coming from the kitchen. I couldn't make out their words clearly, unless one of them shouted a sentence. At one point, Miss Helen called out loudly: "Oh, Chrissy, that's too much!" Then burst out laughing. Not long after, I heard Mother laughing just as loudly, saying: "It's true, it's true, it's absolutely true!"

Because I couldn't hear much of the content, nor see Mother's expression, I couldn't make a reliable judgment, but my feeling was that Mother was in her most relaxed state since entering this house. I was straining to listen when the bedroom door opened and Rick came out.

"Josie's in the bathroom," he said as he walked over to me, "seems polite to wait out here at a time like this."

"Yes, that's considerate."

He followed my gaze, eyes looking past the railing, then nodded toward the adults talking downstairs.

"They get on pretty well," he said. "Shame Mrs. Arthur isn't around more. Having someone to chat with Mom like this—it really does her good. Whenever she's with Mrs. Arthur, she's always happy. I try my best. But I've never been able to make her laugh like that. I guess, being her son, it's hard to relax."

"Rick is certainly an excellent companion for Miss Helen. But as you see, even when you're not there, she can find other companions to laugh and chat with."

"I don't know. Maybe." Then he added: "Listen, I've been thinking this through properly these past few days. From start to finish. What you said that night. I agree with you now. I promised Mom I'd try. Give it my all, my real all, to get into Atlas Brookings."

"Wonderful!"

He leaned further over the railing, perhaps straining to hear their words, and I even worried his height might make him topple over. But then he straightened up, both hands on the rail.

"I even agreed to go meet this... **man**," he said, lowering his voice. "Her old lover."

"The secret weapon person?"

"Yeah, Mom's secret weapon. She thinks he can pull strings for me. I agreed to that too."

"Maybe that will bring the best outcome. The wish in Josie's kind painting might be a step closer to coming true."

"Maybe they're talking about that downstairs right now. How it took me so long to finally be turned around by my mom. Maybe that's what they find so funny."

"I don't think there's malice in their laughter. I think Miss Helen must be happy about Rick's promise. And full of hope."

He was silent for a moment, listening to the voices downstairs. Then he said: "I guess we're getting a ride into the city with Josie and Mrs. Arthur."

"Yes, I know. I've been invited to go with you too."

"Oh, that's good. Then you and Josie can give me moral support together. Because I'm not exactly looking forward to begging this guy for help."

Suddenly a shout came from the bedroom: "Great! Now everyone's abandoned me!" Then, just as Rick turned back toward the door: "Hey, Klara, you come back in too. It's fine. We're not planning a sex show."

Two days later, I would hear more about this trip to the city, in an unexpected way.

It was a rainy weekday, no visitors. After lunch, Josie went to the Big Room for her rectangular board tutoring, and I went upstairs to the bedroom. I sat on the floor, amid a pile of magazines, when Housekeeper Melania appeared at the doorway. She looked down at me, her face neither kind nor angry, and I thought she had come to scold me for leaving Rick and Josie alone in the bedroom just now, despite her warning to watch out for foolishness. But she took another step into the room and said to me in a kind of rough whisper:

"AF. You want to help Miss Josie, don't you?"

"Yes, of course."

"Then listen. Madam is taking Miss Josie to the city Thursday. I said I wanted to go with them. Madam said no. I said okay, Madam still said no. She said no because she knows better than anyone I'd sniff things out. She said she wants to bring AF. So listen. You take good care of Miss Josie in the city. Hear me?"

"Yes, Housekeeper." I whispered back too, though Josie couldn't possibly hear us, "But please explain a little more. What exactly are you worried about?"

"Listen, AF. Madam takes Miss Josie to see Mr. Capaldi. The portrait guy. That Mr. Capaldi is a son-of-a-bitch bastard. Madam says you watch. Then you watch that son-of-a-bitch good. You want to help Miss Josie. We're partners." She glanced back at the door, though no sound of Josie finishing class and coming out came from downstairs.

"But Housekeeper, surely Mr. Capaldi just wants to paint Miss Josie's portrait?"

"Portrait my foot. AF, you watch that son-of-a-bitch close, or Miss Josie will be in big trouble."

"But, surely..." I lowered my voice further, "surely Mother would never..."

"Madam loves Miss Josie. But Miss Sal's death messed Madam up bad. Understand, AF?"
